## PREMISE STATEMENT

Mei Chen, 34, landscape architect, has been crossing between two versions of the Pacific Rim for several months through a liminal space she calls the Lantern Garden. The novel opens at the end of that period — the last four to six weeks of crossings — and follows her testimony to an Environmental Crossing Tribunal six months later.

This is not a story about choosing between worlds. It is a story about discovering that the capacity to exist between them is not a curse to be resolved but a form of being that runs in Mei's maternal line — and that her grandmother, Wǎng Liling, made the same crossing fifty years before her, lived three years in Jun's world, and left something there that may change the outcome of an ecological war.

**Identity-revelation endgame:** Mei transforms. She does not choose. She does not stay. She does not leave. She becomes what she has always been: a bridge. The world's crises — Jun's ecological collapse, her mother's unexamined grief, her grandmother's hidden life — remain unresolved in the way that real crises do. But Mei is no longer suspended between them. She inhabits the suspension itself.

---

## NARRATIVE FRAME

**CHOSEN FRAME: Testimony**

Mei is giving a recorded statement to the Interworld Environmental Crossing Tribunal, an inter-world body that emerged after the crossings became known to both world governments. The Tribunal is investigating the ecological, political, and personal implications of the crossings. Mei testifies approximately six months after the last crossing.

**Rationale:** Testimony is the Nguyen move — the formal, retrospective voice that is simultaneously authoritative and strategic. Mei knows she survived and transformed; the reader knows this from the first page, which removes the false tension of survival and redirects attention to *how she understood what happened to her*. The Tribunal frame also imbeds Jun's ecological stakes into the very institutional structure of the narrative: the body she testifies to may be complicit in the delays that allowed the Cascade Collapse. Her unreliability is not confusion but calculation. She discloses exactly what she chooses to disclose. The grandmother secret emerges through this strategic disclosure — Mei decides, mid-testimony, to tell the full truth.

The testimony voice also permits temporal layering: Mei testifying in the present, Mei experiencing events in the past four to six weeks, and Liling's story emerging as past-within-past. Three layers, one voice, one point of view.

## COMP TITLES AND WHAT WE INHERIT FROM EACH

**Ruth Ozeki, *A Tale for the Time Being*:** The use of found objects and multiple temporal streams to construct identity. The device of the reader becoming the writer's collaborator in meaning-making. The Buddhist inflection of impermanence as presence, not absence. From Ozeki we take: the idea that paying attention to someone's story is itself a form of rescue. Mei pays attention to Liling. Liling has been waiting for this.

**Ursula K. Le Guin, *The Lathe of Heaven*:** The interrogation of what it means to "fix" a world. George Orr's dreams improve the world and worsen it simultaneously; the novel argues against the presumption that one mind can or should correct reality. From Le Guin we take: Jun's ecological mission is not triumphant. The novel refuses easy hope. The coalition may fail even with Mei's help. The seed bank is a gift, not a solution.

**Viet Thanh Nguyen, *The Sympathizer*:** The retrospective confessional voice that is both complicit and critical. The way the narrator's identity is fractured by serving two sides. The formal irony of testifying to an authority that helped create the conditions being testified about. From Nguyen we take: Mei's testimony is not innocent. She withheld things from Jun, from her mother, from herself. The Tribunal is not neutral. The testimony is an act of power, not just disclosure.

## THEMATIC CORE

**Primary theme: Identity is not location.** Mei has treated her crossings as a choice problem — which world is hers? The revelation is that she is not *from* either world; she is *of* the crossing itself. This is not magical thinking but phenomenological fact: she has been changed by the Lantern Garden. She cannot be unchanged. The question was never "where do you belong?" The question was "what are you, now?"

**Secondary theme: The grandmother's choice as the true origin story.** Liling's three years in Jun's world, her return to her own world, her silence — this is not a failure. It is a completed arc. Mei's liminal capacity is not an aberration but an inheritance. This reframes the novel's central conflict: it was never Mei vs. Jun vs. home. It was Mei discovering that she is her grandmother's continuation.

**Tertiary theme: Ecological grief as the condition of existence in Jun's world.** The Cascade Collapse is not backdrop. It is the moral weight of the novel. Staying means joining a losing war. Leaving means abandoning someone in his worst moment. But the transformation reveals a third option: Mei can cross in both directions and carry what each world knows. This is not a solution. It is a way of fighting from the between-place she inhabits.

## CHARACTERS

### MEI CHEN (protagonist, sole POV)

Age: 34. Landscape architect, Vancouver. Taiwanese-Canadian, raised bilingual. Mei's relationship to physical space — her professional identity — is what makes the crossings first comprehensible and then irreversible: she reads terrain the way other people read faces. In Jun's world, her spatial intelligence becomes a tactical tool in the ecological coalition. In her world, it has been feeling hollow; she designs spaces for people who will never truly inhabit them.

**Before the novel begins:** Mei has been crossing involuntarily for several months. She has met Jun. She has been conducting a version of two lives. She is exhausted and frightened and, she will admit to the Tribunal six months later, obscurely glad.

**Character arc:** Mei begins the novel convinced she must choose. She ends it understanding that the choice was always a false binary enforced by people (including herself) who could not tolerate liminality. The grandmother revelation cracks this open: Liling chose, and chose again, and it was enough. Mei's transformation is quieter — she doesn't choose. She becomes.

**What she withholds in testimony:** The full content of what Liling said in the last lucid moment. The exact location of the seed bank (she gives the coalition coordinates, not the Tribunal). The specific nature of her relationship with Jun (she characterizes it as "collegial"). She is not lying. She is protecting. This is who she is.

### WǍNG LILING (grandmother, emotional core)

Age at novel's present: 88. Former botanist specializing in mycorrhizal systems. Now in Zhonghe Memory Care, Taipei, with moderate-to-advanced dementia that has lucid windows. Mei's maternal grandmother. Liling's crossed between the same worlds in 1972–1975, lived in the Pacific Rim equivalent of Jun's world, fell in love with a man named Kenji (Jun's world), came back when she discovered she was pregnant — Mei's mother, Huimei, is the reason she returned.

**What she carries:** Fifty years of silence about three of the most important years of her life. A secret seed bank she planted in what is now the contested forest at the heart of the Cascade Collapse zone. The certain knowledge that Mei would find the garden — she recognized the signs months ago when Mei began to show fatigue and temporal gaps. She has been waiting.

**How she appears in the novel:** Three forms. First, as a confused elderly woman in memory care, saying things that seem like dementia-speech but carry encoded meaning Mei misses initially. Second, in two extraordinary lucid windows — one near the novel's midpoint, one near the end — during which she speaks clearly, strategically, and with great love. Third, after her death in Chapter 30, as a presence in Mei's understanding of herself.

**What she is NOT:** A magical figure. Her lucidity is physiological, not supernatural. Her seed bank was planted by a trained botanist with exceptional mycorrhizal knowledge. Her crossings worked the same way Mei's do. She was a scientist who found a phenomenon and used her training to respond to it.

### JUN NAKAMURA

Age: 37. Ecological scientist and coalition leader, Jun's world. His world's Pacific Rim suffered a delayed ecological cascade beginning in the 1990s — a crisis that was nearly averted in the 1970s and has been accelerating since. Jun leads the Cascade Coalition, a consortium of scientists, Indigenous land stewards, and policy advocates fighting the collapse of temperate rainforest ecosystems across what would be southern British Columbia and northern Washington.

**His mission:** The fungal network project — seeding an artificial mycorrhizal bridge across fragmented forest patches to slow the Cascade's advance. The science is plausible; the odds are poor. He has been running on borrowed time and adrenaline for three years. Mei's arrival — first as mysterious, then as someone with genuine spatial expertise — gives the coalition something it didn't have: a landscape architect who can model network topology across terrain.

**His relationship with Mei:** Real, adult, and secondary to the plot in the sense that Nguyen's love interests are secondary — present, important, and not the engine. Jun loves Mei in the way that people love each other when they are both running toward something and running away from loss. He wants her to stay not just for himself but because he believes her capacity to cross means the two worlds' ecological knowledge could be combined. He is right about this. His error is thinking she must *choose* his world to do it.

**His arc:** Jun begins the novel as someone who believes in choosing sides. He ends it — grudgingly, with grief — accepting that Mei's liminality is the thing that may make the coalition's work survivable. He does not get resolution. The forest continues to die. The seed bank is a hope, not a victory.

### HUIMEI CHEN (Mei's mother)

Age: 62. Lives in Taipei. Has spent her life feeling like a secret — the child of a woman who returned from somewhere unannounced, who was never quite fully present even when she was home. Huimei does not know about the crossings. She does not know about Liling's three years. She has constructed a life on top of a foundation she could feel was uneven but could not name.

**Her arc:** Mei tells her a simplified version of the truth. The novel does not require Huimei to accept or transform on page — her arc is gestural, present in small moments: she begins visiting her mother differently, asking questions she'd suppressed for forty years. This is the family reckoning the novel promises: not a confrontation but a re-seeing.

### THE TRIBUNAL (frame narrative)

Unnamed chair. Institutional, careful, not malicious — but representing a body that has moved slowly enough that the Cascade Collapse has cost years of potential intervention. The Tribunal's interest in Mei is partly scientific, partly jurisdictional: what does her testimony establish about the crossings, and who has claims over them?

## WORLD ARCHITECTURE

### World A (Mei's world)
Contemporary Vancouver and Taipei, 2026. Our world, largely as we know it. Mei's Vancouver is wet, green, politically anxious about ecological futures but not yet experiencing the kind of collapse Jun's world is living through.

### World B (Jun's world)
A Pacific Rim that diverged from ours approximately fifty years ago. A ecological catastrophe was narrowly avoided in the 1970s — but not cleanly, and the delayed consequences are arriving now. The forests of what we'd recognize as coastal British Columbia and northern Washington are in visible decline: visible die-offs, beetle explosions, smoke seasons that last eight months. Jun's world is recognizably ours but further along a trajectory we can see from where we stand.

### The Lantern Garden
A liminal space that exists simultaneously in both worlds — not between them but overlapping with both. Physically, it appears as a garden of lantern-fruited plants (Physalis-like, bioluminescent in the liminal state) that occupies the same terrain in both worlds but is perceptible only by those with the crossing capacity. Mei experiences it as a specific location in what would be a park near her Vancouver apartment. In Jun's world, the same coordinates are at the edge of the contested forest.

**The crossing mechanics:** Crossings are triggered by sustained attention — Mei has to want the crossing and be in the Garden space. They are not instant; she passes through a liminal interval that feels like minutes but may be longer. Each crossing costs physical fatigue and occasional memory gaps (she loses peripheral memories, not core ones). She cannot control what she loses. This is why Liling came back with gaps in her published record — three years of institutional memory erosion, not just absence.

---

## THE SEED BANK

Liling's fifty-year-old gift to Jun's world: a buried, sealed mycorrhizal seed matrix planted in the 1970s in a location that is now at the heart of the Cascade Collapse zone. Liling was one of the leading mycorrhizal scientists of her generation; she understood that the forest in Jun's world was being asked to survive conditions it hadn't evolved for, and she planted a matrix of species from her world's healthier equivalent ecosystems. The seeds have been in suspended viability for fifty years. The coalition doesn't know they exist. When Mei discovers the coordinates and leads the coalition to the site, the matrix is viable. This is not a solution. It is a tool — the best tool the coalition has.

The seed bank is also the novel's proof of concept: Mei is not the first bridge. The worlds have been entangled since 1975.

### PROLOGUE · TESTIMONY, SESSION ONE · (~500 words)

*Voice: First-person testimony, present tense of the Tribunal session.*

Mei sits before the Tribunal. The room is described — formal, institutional, two flags she doesn't recognize as any national standard. The Tribunal chair asks her to state her name, occupation, place of residence. She states her name and occupation. For place of residence, she pauses. The chapter ends before she answers.

*Function:* Establish the testimony frame. Confirm that Mei survived and is cognitively intact. Introduce the withheld answer that the novel will spend 85,000 words approaching.

---

### ACT I — THE WEIGHT OF THE CROSSING
*Weeks 0–1, Chapters 1–7, target: 17,000 words (~2,400 per chapter)*

**Chapter 1 · The Tenth Week (~2,500 words)**
*Testimony frame: brief, establishes that she's describing "the tenth week of crossings, or what I believed was the last phase."*

Jun's world. Mei has just crossed. She arrives at the edge of the Cascade zone to find Jun's team in crisis — a test plot has died overnight, three months ahead of worst-case projections. We meet Jun in emergency-mode, tactical, frightened. Mei's spatial read of the die-off pattern gives the coalition something: the collapse is moving in a specific vector she recognizes from fire-corridor modeling. She hasn't told anyone how she got here. She says she flew in from the coast.

*Character beat:* We see Mei's competence. We see her compartmentalization. We see Jun's respect for her and the romantic undertow, handled lightly.

---

**Chapter 2 · Residue (~2,300 words)**
*No testimony frame — the chapter is pure past.*

Mei's world. She wakes in her Vancouver apartment, displaced. The residue of the crossing: fatigue, a gap in her memory of the previous Wednesday (she doesn't know what she did Wednesday morning). She goes to her office. She models green roofs for a tower development. The work feels paper-thin. Her mother calls — Liling has been asking for her.

*Character beat:* Establish Mei's Vancouver life as increasingly hollow. Establish the cost of the crossings: memory erosion is real and random. Establish her mother as a worried presence.

---

**Chapter 3 · The Care Facility (~2,400 words)**

Mei flies to Taipei. Zhonghe Memory Care is described — not grimly, but honestly: the smell, the light, the way time moves differently there. Liling is in a middle phase: mostly confused, but with flashes. In this visit, Liling says something during a confused spell that registers as strange only in retrospect: *"The garden blooms at the crossing. You mustn't leave your shoes there."* Mei assumes it's dementia drift. She holds her grandmother's hand and talks about the weather.

*On the way out:* Mei notices a photograph on Liling's bedside table — a place she almost recognizes. A garden. She takes a photo on her phone.

*Character beat:* Establish Liling as a person, not a metaphor. Her confusion is real; her encoded knowing is also real. Mei's relationship with her grandmother is warm, grief-adjacent, not yet urgently meaningful.

---

**Chapter 4 · The Cascade (~2,400 words)**
*Brief testimony frame: "I want to be specific about what Jun was fighting. I think the Tribunal has not fully understood the scale."*

Jun's world. The full coalition meeting. Jun presents current collapse models. Mei sits in and takes notes. The science is explained through Jun's presentation: temperate rainforest die-off, beetle infestation, smoke, ash, the loss of the mycorrhizal network that knits the ecosystem together. The fungal network project — seeding an artificial bridge — is their last best option. It needs a spatial framework Mei can provide: which patches to seed first, which corridors will propagate the network fastest.

*Character beat:* Jun is a scientist who has also become a general in a losing war. The romance between Jun and Mei is present but disciplined — they're both too exhausted for anything easy.

---

**Chapter 5 · Asked and Not Asked (~2,400 words)**

Jun's world. Jun asks Mei, directly, whether she intends to stay permanently. She can't answer. He doesn't push. They walk the edge of the forest at dusk — the first scene where the physical world of Jun's side is rendered in full, sensory detail: the wrong birds, the wrong quality of silence where there should be insect noise, the orange tint of the air that means smoke from two hundred kilometers away.

Jun says: *"I've stopped asking how you get here. I've started asking what keeps you from deciding."*
Mei: *"I don't think it's a decision."*
Jun: *"What else would it be?"*

*Character beat:* The novel's central question posed clearly, before Mei has the language to answer it. Jun's framework (choice) vs. Mei's emerging sense (ontology).

---

**Chapter 6 · What the Living Say (~2,200 words)**

Mei's world. Second visit to Liling, this one unplanned — Mei is in Taipei for a work presentation and stops by. Liling is more confused than last time, then briefly and startlingly clear: she grabs Mei's wrist and says, *"You smell like it. Like the crossing."* Then she's gone again — drifts back into confusion, asks for someone named Kenji.

Mei calls her mother that evening. Huimei says: *"She often asks for Kenji. We don't know who that is. Some old friend, maybe. You know how it is."*

*Character beat:* The name Kenji drops in and Mei files it without urgency. Liling's momentary recognition is disturbing but Mei interprets it as dementia-pattern, not signal.

---

**Chapter 7 · The Photograph (~2,300 words)**

Mei's world. She has the photo from Liling's bedside (the one she photographed on her phone in Chapter 3). She looks at it properly for the first time. The garden in the photograph: the lantern-plants. The specific quality of light. She recognizes it. It is the Lantern Garden, or its equivalent in Liling's era — the same place, decades ago, with a young woman standing at the edge of the frame. The woman is not facing the camera. She is facing into the garden.

*End of Act I.* Mei books a return flight to Vancouver and cancels her next client meeting. The crossings are no longer just happening to her.

*Testimony frame (brief, chapter's end):* "I want to be clear about what I did and did not know at the end of the first week. I knew the photograph was significant. I did not know it was a door."

---

### ACT II — THE ECOLOGY OF SECRETS
*Weeks 2–3, Chapters 8–17, target: 28,000 words (~2,800 per chapter)*

**Chapter 8 · Archive (~2,700 words)**

Mei's world. She shows the phone photograph to her mother over video call. Huimei looks at it a long time. *"Where did you find this?"* Mei: *"On Nǎi Nai's bedside table."* Long pause. Huimei says she doesn't know the garden. Grandmother traveled a great deal when she was young — botanical fieldwork. *"She never talked about it much."*

Mei begins pulling Liling's published record. Taiwan Botanical Institute archive, digitized. She reads the papers — mycorrhizal systems in Pacific Rim conifer forests, 1968–1972. Then a gap. 1972–1975: nothing. Not a single paper. Conference records show her registered for a 1973 symposium in Vancouver — no record of her presenting.

---

**Chapter 9 · The Fracture Line (~2,900 words)**
*Testimony frame: "Jun had been awake for forty hours when I arrived."*

Jun's world. The fungal network project hits a setback: their primary test site at the southern edge has been logged illegally — a contractor working for a company the coalition has been fighting in court. Jun is trying not to collapse. The coalition meeting is tense; two members want to abandon the southern corridor entirely and concentrate resources north.

Mei's spatial analysis keeps the southern option alive: she can see from the topology that the southern site, if they seed it before the next beetle-season, will create a propagation vector the northern route doesn't have. The coalition votes to try. Jun, afterward, leans against the wall and covers his face with his hands. Mei stands next to him and says nothing. This is the scene where their relationship deepens beyond professional respect.

---

**Chapter 10 · The Botanist's Record (~2,800 words)**

Mei's world. She hires a research assistant in Taipei — a young archivist named Jing-wei — to dig deeper into the institutional record for Liling's lost years. Jing-wei finds that Liling's Canadian visa was renewed three times between 1972 and 1975 but she was not at any Canadian university or research institution during that period. Her Taiwan residency registration lapsed. She was, in the official record, *not anywhere*.

Mei reads Liling's 1968–1972 papers more carefully. The mycorrhizal work is extraordinary for its time — she understood the forest's underground signaling network in ways that weren't widely accepted until the 1990s. She was ahead of the field by twenty years.

---

**Chapter 11 · A Small Victory (~2,700 words)**

Jun's world. The southern test plot responds. The fungal inoculation is taking — early signs, fragile, but real. The coalition celebrates; someone opens wine that's been waiting for three years. Jun is quietly euphoric. He and Mei walk through the test plot at night with headlamps, looking at the markers. He takes her hand.

*This is the love scene.* Not explicit but specific: the way they stand together in a dying forest that is, for this moment, doing something alive. The romance between them is real. The novel honors it without letting it become the engine. What they love about each other is the same thing that makes their situation impossible by any conventional logic: they are both in the wrong world, fighting for the right one.

---

**Chapter 12 · The Missing Years (~2,800 words)**

Mei's world. Jing-wei sends the first results. The gap in Liling's record isn't the result of incomplete archiving. In 1972, Liling's colleagues at the Taiwan Botanical Institute submitted a missing-persons report. She'd told them she was going to Vancouver for the symposium and hadn't returned. The report was closed in 1975 when she simply appeared again at the Institute, alive, healthy, no explanation provided.

The institutional note on the case closure is brief: *Subject returned voluntarily. No criminal investigation warranted. Personal matter.*

*Personal matter.* Three years.

---

**Chapter 13 · What Huimei Knows (~2,700 words)**

Mei calls her mother and tells her what she's found — the missing persons report, the three-year gap. Huimei goes quiet for a long time. Then: *"I know she was gone. I was very young. My bà bà told me she'd gone to work in Canada. When she came back she was different. More careful. More... present. But not quite here."*

Mei: *"Was she pregnant when she came back?"*

Longer silence. *"I was born in 1976. Nine months after she returned."*

Neither of them says what this means yet. The scene ends with Huimei saying, *"Some things are better not examined too closely,"* and Mei understanding that she is going to examine them exactly that closely.

---

**Chapter 14 · What Staying Would Mean (~2,800 words)**

Jun's world. Mei spends a full week in Jun's world — the longest sustained stay since she began crossing. She works embedded with the coalition's mapping team. She eats at the team's communal kitchen. She sleeps in Jun's apartment. She lives, for one week, as if she has made the choice.

What she discovers: it would be enough. Jun's world, Jun's work, Jun himself — it would be enough. She would be helping with something real, something mattered, with someone she loves. She understands why Liling stayed three years.

She also understands why she can't. The understanding is pre-verbal. The week ends; she crosses back.

---

**Chapter 15 · Found (~2,800 words)**

Mei's world. Jing-wei has found more: a colleague of Liling's from the 1975 period, an elderly man now in a care home in Nantou. He remembers Liling returning. He remembers she brought seed samples. He thought they were from Canada — she'd said so. He helped her catalog them. They weren't filed with the Institute; she took them when she left.

She took them somewhere. She planted them somewhere.

---

**Chapter 16 · The First Crossers (~2,900 words)**

Jun's world. Mei asks Jun if there are records of people crossing before her. He hesitates. There's a story — considered a legend in his world, not officially documented — of a woman who appeared in the Cascade forest zone in the early 1970s, worked with local botanists for two to three years, then vanished. No one knows what happened to her. She went by a name that's been half-corrupted in the oral record: *Lin*, maybe, or *Ling*.

Mei keeps her voice level. She asks for everything Jun knows about this story. He has what the coalition archivist has: two photographs and a partial field journal.

The journal is in Traditional Chinese. The handwriting is Liling's.

---

**Chapter 17 · Garden and Woman (~3,000 words)**

*This chapter earns its length.*

Mei translates the journal fragments. They cover six months of mycorrhizal fieldwork in Jun's world, meticulously scientific. The final entry: Liling has discovered she is pregnant. The crossing has not prevented this. She will return to her world. She leaves something behind: *"The matrix is in the ground. It has what it needs. Fifty years is enough if the climate holds. If someone finds this before then, tell them to look under the old cedar on the ridge. The one with the split trunk. The package is wrapped in birch bark. They will know what to do with it."*

*End of Act II.* Liling — alive, confused, waiting in the care facility — says, as Mei enters the room that evening in Taipei, with perfect clarity: *"You found it, didn't you. The garden."*

*Testimony frame:* "I want the Tribunal to understand that I knew, at this moment, that the next question I had to ask would not be 'which world do I live in.' It would be 'who am I, that this is my inheritance.'"

---

### ACT III — THE FAMILY RECKONING
*Weeks 4–5, Chapters 18–24, target: 22,000 words (~3,100 per chapter)*

**Chapter 18 · Liling Speaks (~3,200 words)**

The longest lucid window in the novel. Liling is fully present for ninety minutes. She tells Mei everything: the crossing, Kenji, the three years, the pregnancy, the return. She speaks matter-of-factly, the way scientists speak about findings. She is not asking for absolution. She made the choices she made; they led to Mei's mother, who led to Mei. She knew, when Mei started showing the signs — the fatigue, the temporal gaps, the smell of the Garden — that this conversation was coming.

*What Liling gives Mei:*
1. The capacity is genetic, passed through the maternal line. She is not an anomaly. She is an inheritor.
2. The seed bank coordinates — the old cedar on the ridge, the split trunk. She memorized them. She gives them to Mei now.
3. Permission Mei didn't know she needed: *"I came back because of her. But I never left. Not really. And neither will you. That's not weakness, little one. That's what we are."*

---

**Chapter 19 · The Inheritance (~3,000 words)**

Mei alone. She walks Taipei at night processing what Liling told her. This chapter is interior — Mei's thoughts, her re-reading of her own life in light of the inheritance. She has always felt most alive in-between: between two cultures, between two professional modes, between languages. She has always felt slightly wrong in any single place. She thought this was a flaw. Now she understands it is the organ that makes the crossing possible.

The chapter ends with a formal realization, stated plainly: *I am not suspended between worlds. I am the bridge itself. This was always true. It is true now in a different way than I understood.*

---

**Chapter 20 · Jun and the Determinism Argument (~3,100 words)**

Jun's world. Mei tells Jun the full story: Liling, the crossings, the seed bank, the journal. Jun is stunned. He sits with it. Then: *"Then you were always going to find me. The crossing always leads to someone fighting for the forest. Maybe that's what it's for."*

Mei bristles. She is not a mechanism of fate. She is not the universe's instrument. The fight that follows is important: Jun's ecological thinking — systems, patterns, inevitable outcomes — collides with Mei's refusal to be a narrative. *"I did not cross because the forest needed me. I crossed because I found the Garden and I walked into it. What I did with where I landed was my choice."*

Jun, after a long pause: *"Then what are you choosing now?"*

Mei: *"I'm choosing what I am."*

He doesn't understand this yet. He will.

---

**Chapter 21 · Huimei (~3,100 words)**

Mei calls her mother from Jun's world. She tells her a version of the truth — edited for what Huimei can hold. She tells her that grandmother lived abroad for three years, had a great love, came back because she was pregnant. She does not tell her about the other world. She does not tell her about the crossings. She tells her: Grandmother loved you. Coming back for you was the choice she made. It was not a failure.

Huimei cries. Not dramatically — quietly, the way people cry when they've been holding something for decades and something small finally gives. She says: *"I always felt like I was the reason she couldn't be happy."* 

Mei: *"You were the reason she could be."*

*This is the family reckoning the novel promised.* Not a confrontation. A re-seeing.

---

**Chapter 22 · The Collapse Accelerates (~3,000 words)**

Jun's world. The worst models were wrong. The Cascade is accelerating faster than the coalition's revised projections. The beetle-fronts have merged across two corridors; the southern test plot is at risk. The coalition fractures: two senior members leave. Jun gives a speech to the remaining team that is one of the novel's set-pieces — not optimistic, not defeated, refusing to be either. *"We are not here because we believe we will win. We are here because we know what it would mean to stop."*

Mei watches him and understands why she loves him, and why she cannot be what he needs her to be in the way he thinks he needs it.

---

**Chapter 23 · What Liling Left (~3,200 words)**

Liling's second lucid window — briefer than the first, more urgent. She repeats the seed bank coordinates, then says something Mei hadn't expected: she has been going back. In her dementia, she has been crossing. The Lantern Garden doesn't require full consciousness; it seems to seek her. She has been spending time there — in the between-place — and she is not frightened.

*"It's quiet there,"* Liling says. *"I think I'll stay there for a while. When I go."*

This reframes the dementia: what looks like absence may be presence elsewhere. Mei cannot be certain. She chooses to believe it.

After Mei leaves, she calls Jun. She tells him about the seed bank. She gives him the coordinates. *"There's a matrix in the ground at those coordinates. Fifty years old, mycorrhizal, from our world's Pacific Rim. It was planted by a botanist named Wǎng Liling in 1974. Your world has had it this whole time. You just didn't know."*

Jun is silent for a long time. *"That's not nothing."*

Mei: *"No. It's not."*

---

**Chapter 24 · The Wrong Question (~3,400 words)**

*This is the novel's thematic pivot.*

Mei goes to the Lantern Garden. She has been avoiding it except as a transit point. Now she goes to it with attention, not purpose. She sits in it for the first time — not crossing, not returning. Just present in the between-place.

What she discovers: the Garden is not a doorway. It's not a portal. It's a place. It exists in its own right. The crossings happen *through* it, but the Garden is not the crossing. It is the space that permits crossing.

She has been thinking of herself as something that exists in one world or another, with the Garden as the hinge. She understands now: she is a kind of the Garden. She is the place that permits crossing. She is not trying to decide where to live. She has been living here — in the liminal space itself — and not recognizing it as home.

*End of Act III. Testimony frame:* "There are things I am not going to tell this Tribunal. I want you to know that my silence is not evasion. It is the protection of what cannot yet be examined without damage. I'll tell you what I choose to tell you, and I'll tell you why, and you will have to decide what to do with the parts I keep."

---

### ACT IV — THE TRANSFORMATION
*Week 6, Chapters 25–31, target: 17,000 words (~2,400 per chapter)*

**Chapter 25 · The Garden, Unfiltered (~2,500 words)**

Mei returns to the Lantern Garden with full attention. She stays for what feels like hours — may be hours, may be days; time is different in the between-place. She examines the lantern-plants. She maps the space as a landscape architect. She understands that this is *work* — that the Garden is something that can be known, not just traversed.

She is home. She is terrified. She accepts both.

---

**Chapter 26 · What She Tells Jun (~2,400 words)**

Jun's world. Mei sits with Jun and tells him, as plainly as she can: she is not going to stay in his world. She is not going to return to her own world and sever the connection. She is going to become what she is — a bridge, a crossing-capable entity who moves in both directions and carries what each world knows.

She lays out what this might mean practically: she can bring ecological data from her world. She can consult on the coalition's modeling without living in Jun's world. She can carry seed materials, if needed. She can be a link between two versions of the same forest fighting the same collapse at different speeds.

*This is not a consolation.* Jun will hear it as one at first.

---

**Chapter 27 · Jun's Grief and His Understanding (~2,400 words)**

Jun's response: anger first. This sounds like leaving. This sounds like she's found a way to be half-present and calling it a gift. He says things he will regret. Mei absorbs them without withdrawing.

Then grief. He is so tired. He is fighting a losing war. He wanted one thing to not be a loss.

Then — slowly, over the hours of a very long evening — he begins to see it. The bridge. The data she could carry. The seed bank his coalition didn't know existed, planted by a woman from Mei's world fifty years ago. The worlds are already entangled. Mei isn't proposing something new. She is naming what's already true.

He doesn't say he's okay with it. He says: *"I'm not happy about this."* And then: *"But I can see it. I can see what you're describing."*

This is enough.

---

**Chapter 28 · The Cedar on the Ridge (~2,300 words)**

Jun's world. Mei accompanies the coalition team to the seed bank location — the old cedar with the split trunk, coordinates from Liling's memory. They find the package. Birch bark, sealed with pine resin, still intact after fifty years. Inside: mycorrhizal matrix, dried and compressed, with detailed field notes in Liling's handwriting, translated now by Mei in real time for the coalition scientists.

The matrix is viable. It will take six months to culture and deploy. It is not salvation — the coalition's ecologist says this carefully, not wanting false hope — but it gives the fungal network project options it didn't have. The southern corridor may hold.

*Liling's science was fifty years ahead of what anyone recognized it as being.*

---

**Chapter 29 · The Mother (~2,500 words)**

Mei's world. She visits Liling again and this time brings her mother. Huimei hasn't visited in months — she finds it too difficult. But she comes now.

They sit with Liling together. Liling is confused, mostly, but when Huimei takes her hand, she seems to feel something. She says, in a clear moment: *"You smell like your father."* It's the first time she's ever mentioned him to Huimei.

After, in the hallway, Huimei cries in a different way than before. *"She loved him,"* Huimei says. *"She actually loved him."* She means: her mother wasn't broken. She was full. The grief was the fullness, not its absence.

Mei: *"Yes."*

---

**Chapter 30 · Liling's Death (~2,400 words)**

Liling dies on a Tuesday. Quietly, in her bed, with Mei and Huimei beside her. No drama; no final lucid speech. She is simply less present, and then gone.

In the last minutes, she opens her eyes briefly. She does not look at Huimei or Mei. She looks at a point in the middle distance, and something in her face changes — not pain, not grief, not even peace, exactly. Recognition. As if someone she was expecting has arrived.

*Later, Mei will wonder if she crossed in the end. She will never know for certain. She chooses to believe it.*

Mei does not tell the Tribunal this. This belongs to the family.

---

**Chapter 31 · The Last Crossing (~2,900 words)**

*This is the final chapter of the novel's past-strand.*

Mei crosses into the Lantern Garden. She does not go all the way through to Jun's world. She stops in the between-place. She sits among the lantern-plants, which are glowing — she's never seen them glow before, but they glow now, and she understands she's never stayed long enough in the Garden itself to see it.

She takes out her phone and opens a file — the spatial model she's building of the Lantern Garden as a place, not a transit point. She works for a while in the between-light, mapping what she can observe.

She does not cross. She does not return. She is here, in the suspension itself, and the suspension is not suspension: it is the most precise location she has ever occupied.

*She is here.*

*The world stays unresolved. The Cascade Coalition is fighting. The seed bank is being cultured. Jun is awake at a desk somewhere making models that may or may not be accurate. Huimei is at a table in Taipei, holding a photograph of a garden. Liling is wherever she crossed to.*

*The forest is dying. The forest is also, in a test plot in the southern corridor, doing something alive.*

*Mei is here.*

---

### EPILOGUE · TESTIMONY, SESSION FINAL · (~1,000 words)

*Voice: First-person testimony, present tense of the Tribunal session.*

Mei concludes her testimony. The Tribunal chair has been patient. He asks the questions that institutional processes require: jurisdiction, residence, allegiance, legal standing. Mei answers them honestly and without apology.

Final exchange:

CHAIR: And what do you claim as your place of residence, for the record?

MEI: [pause] The crossing itself.

CHAIR: That isn't a recognized—

MEI: I know.

*End.*

*The epilogue does not resolve the Tribunal's jurisdiction question. It does not tell the reader what happens to the Cascade Coalition. It does not tell us whether Jun and Mei speak again, or whether the seed bank saves the forest. These questions remain open because the novel is not about their resolution. The novel is about Mei arriving at the correct answer to the correct question.*

*She has arrived.*

## WHAT EDITOR-EMMA SHOULD FLAG

1. **Tonal consistency in testimony frame:** The Tribunal scenes need a consistent register — formal but not dry. Mei is intelligent and strategic in what she reveals; the prose in those sections should feel slightly different from the past-strand narration. Flag if the frames blur.

2. **Liling's two lucid windows:** These are the novel's highest-stakes scenes. Ch. 18 especially. The risk is that Liling becomes a vehicle for information rather than a person. Emma should flag any moment where Liling is delivering exposition rather than being present.

3. **Jun's ecology:** The Cascade Collapse needs enough scientific specificity to be credible without becoming a lecture. Flag if any coalition scene loses narrative momentum to explain mycorrhizal biology.

4. **The romance:** Real, honored, not central. Flag if any chapter tips the balance — if Jun and Mei's relationship starts to feel like the engine rather than one of several important weights.

5. **The transformation (Ch. 25 / Ch. 31):** These are the hardest scenes. Transformation that is internal and ontological rather than dramatic risks feeling like nothing happened. Flag if the emotional stakes aren't landing — these scenes need to feel earned, not stated.

---

## WHAT RESEARCH-RINA SHOULD VERIFY

1. **Mycorrhizal science:** The seed bank premise — a 50-year-old mycorrhizal matrix in birch-bark wrapping remaining viable — needs scientific grounding. What are the actual viability timelines for mycorrhizal inoculants? What conditions would allow preservation? The science doesn't need to be perfect but it can't be obviously wrong.

2. **Taiwan Botanical Institute, 1968–1975:** Does such an institution exist (or equivalent)? What were mycorrhizal researchers doing in this period? Rina should verify that Liling's academic arc is plausible for a Taiwanese woman botanist of that era.

3. **Pacific Rim forest ecology:** The Cascade Collapse as described — temperate rainforest die-off, beetle infestation, mycorrhizal network collapse — draws on real processes. Rina should verify current science on mycorrhizal network resilience, cascade die-off patterns, and whether the fungal network seeding project Jun leads is scientifically coherent.

4. **Interworld tribunal as institution:** This is a fictional body in the novel's world. Rina should flag any elements of its description that would strain credulity — it needs to feel like something that could plausibly emerge from two governments negotiating over the crossings, not science fiction bureaucracy.

5. **Vancouver/Taipei geography and travel logistics:** Mei crosses through the Lantern Garden at a location near her Vancouver apartment. When she visits Taipei, she's at Zhonghe Memory Care (a real district). Rina should verify that the geographic and logistical details in both cities are accurate.

## OPEN QUESTIONS FOR THE NEXT DRAFT PASS

- **Kenji:** We name him and Liling asks for him in her confusion. Do we ever see him more directly? Option A: we don't — he is fully mediated through Liling's memory and the journal fragments. This is the disciplined choice. Option B: we find a photograph of him in the coalition's historical records and Jun has to deliver that information to Mei. This adds a scene but risks over-plotting.

- **The testimony authority:** Is the Tribunal investigating the crossings to protect them, regulate them, or eliminate them? This political ambiguity should remain ambiguous in the testimony frame — Mei doesn't know either — but the nature of the threat the Tribunal might pose should be palpable.

- **Huimei's knowledge:** How much does Huimei know by the end? The novel tells Huimei a "simplified version" of the truth. Does she eventually learn about the crossings directly? The novel can leave this open — a future conversation that hasn't happened yet. But editor-emma should weigh in on whether Huimei's arc feels complete enough given what she knows.

- **The final image:** The last chapter ends with Mei in the Garden, glowing. The epilogue is testimony. Does the novel end on the testimony's last line, or does the epilogue end on a stage direction (Mei rises, the recording light goes out)? This is a craft question for the late revision stage.
