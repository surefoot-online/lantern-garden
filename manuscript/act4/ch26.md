### Chapter Twenty-Six · The Ridge Survey

The ridge survey was scheduled for two days after the morning at the monitoring station, and I was on it as I had been on everything with the coalition for the past weeks: in the dual capacity that I had stopped trying to justify and started simply occupying, the landscape architect and the person who understood something about the territory that the landscape architect couldn't see in the standard instruments.

Priya organized the survey party: herself, Jun, Henriksen, and me, plus two of the coalition's fieldwork technicians, a young woman named Sol and a man named Terrence who had been in the Cascade zone for three years and knew the terrain in the way that field technicians know it — in the body, in the specific knowledge of where the unstable ground is and which exposed ridge to cross before ten in the morning when the thermal winds came up.

We drove the logging road — the one the coalition had been fighting to keep closed to extraction traffic — to the survey departure point. The road was rough in the way of roads that have been argued over for years: the contested infrastructure of a contested forest, the surface showing evidence of use by competing claimants. Henriksen drove. Jun had the topographic and substrate models on his tablet. I had my own notebook and the spatial model I had been building of the ridge corridor since the first survey, the one Liling's coordinates had led us to.

The walk in was an hour. The ridge terrain is different from the test plot terrain: higher, drier, the conifer species changing as you climb, the quality of silence different at that elevation — less the wrong silence of the beetle-damaged lower forest and more a silence that was conditional, waiting. The substrate underfoot had the specific sponge quality of deep humus over the mineral layer that Liling had chosen for the matrix burial: I had learned to recognize it in the days since the first survey, the specific give of a soil that was biologically active at depth.

Jun walked ahead of me. He moved through the terrain with the efficient physicality of someone who has spent fifteen years in field conditions — stepping over roots without looking, reading the slope ahead and adjusting, not wasting motion. I had noticed in the past two days that he was moving differently than he had before our conversation. Not better or worse — differently. The efficiency was the same but the purposefulness had shifted. Something in the orientation of it. Before our conversation, his movement in the field had the quality of someone fighting toward a position. Now it had the quality of someone moving through known territory with full awareness of how much of it remained unmapped.

I want to be honest about this: I am probably reading too much into how a person walks. I am a spatial reader, and sometimes I read things that are projections rather than observations, and I do not always know the difference in the moment. I am reporting what I perceived.

The survey itself was professional, careful, and crowded with the specific competence that the coalition's fieldwork always produces when the conditions are not in crisis mode. Priya collected substrate samples at twelve designated points along the ridge corridor, working with the precision of a mycologist who knows that contamination of the sample is the enemy of the data. Henriksen documented the existing vegetation at each point — the canopy composition, the understory density, the evidence or absence of beetle activity. The two technicians ran the connectivity analysis, measuring gap distances between the existing tree clusters that would need to be bridged if the corridor was to function as a propagation vector.

I mapped.

This was the work I had been doing for the coalition since the beginning, but in the ridge's terrain it was different — or I was different in it. I had been mapping Jun's world's ecology as a landscape architect maps a client site: reading the space, understanding its arguments, proposing interventions. On the ridge, I was mapping a place I had come to understand differently. A place where I had stood the first time with the coordinates in my notebook and the smell of the birch bark seal in my memory and something that was not quite seeing, but was adjacent to seeing, of the Garden's edge nearby.

The ridge was viable. I had known this from the substrate data, from the topographic models. The survey confirmed it in the specifics: the corridor was narrow — narrower than the eastern corridor had been before the beetle-front closed it — but it had the right mineral substrate, the right elevation, the right gap distances for the matrix's propagation needs. It was not the corridor you would have chosen first. It was the corridor you chose when the one you would have chosen first was gone.

Priya said, at the seventh sample point: "I want to show you something."

She had a hand lens out. She was crouched over the substrate sample she'd taken, which she'd opened carefully onto a piece of white sampling cloth. "Look at the hyphal threads."

I crouched beside her. The hand lens showed what the naked eye couldn't: at the edges of the substrate sample, where the deep humus met the mineral layer, there were hyphal threads. Faint, very fine, the pale network of fungal colonization.

"Is that from the matrix?" Jun asked. He had come over when Priya called.

"It's Rhizopogon," Priya said. "Same genus as two of the species in Liling's preparation. I can't tell you from a hand lens whether it's related — that would need sequencing. But this was already here. In the substrate around the burial site, radiating out." She looked up. "She didn't just plant a matrix. She planted a matrix that has been colonizing the surrounding substrate for fifty years. Slowly. Below frost depth. But expanding."

The silence was different then. The specific silence of a group of people who are standing in the presence of something they had not expected to find.

"How far?" Jun asked.

"I don't know. We'd need the full survey to know the extent. But if the colonization has been expanding radially for fifty years at mycorrhizal expansion rates—" Priya paused. "The ridge corridor may already be partially networked. Not in the way we'd deploy from a culture, not concentrated — but present. The biological foundation may already be here."

Henriksen said, quietly: "She built the road before we needed it."

No one responded to this immediately. It was accurate, and sometimes accuracy takes a moment to simply occupy the space without comment.

We worked until mid-afternoon. The thermal winds came up at eleven as Terrence had predicted, and we adjusted — moved along the sheltered side of the ridge for the upper survey points, accepted that two of the planned sample locations were inaccessible by midday and would need a second visit. The work had the quality of all good field work: focused, adaptive, unglamorous, in the service of information that would matter later.

At the end of the survey, walking back toward the logging road in the last light that could be called afternoon, Jun and I found ourselves behind the rest of the team. Not deliberately — Priya and Henriksen and the technicians were moving at their own pace, and Jun and I had stopped at a point on the ridge where the view opened westward, the Cascade zone visible in the distance in its complicated beauty: the surviving forest and the dying forest and the smoke that had cleared in the week's relatively clean air.

We had not spoken about the crossings since the monitoring station.

"The Garden," Jun said.

I looked at him.

"Is it here? At these coordinates?" He was looking at the ridge, not at me. "When you came here the first time. Did you feel it?"

This was not a scientific question. The asking of it cost him something — I could see it in the way he held his body while asking, the specific tension of someone who has decided to step past the edge of their own methodology.

"Yes," I said.

"What does it feel like? From inside the crossing." He looked at me then. "You've been precise about the mechanics and the effects. You've told me the cost. You've told me what it looks like from outside — the time loss, the fatigue, the gaps. But I've never asked what it feels like from inside."

I thought about this. I thought about the Garden on the Tuesday morning, the sitting, the luminescence, the quality of the light that I had tried to describe and found words inadequate for.

"Like being very exactly in a place," I said. "More exactly than is usually possible. In my world, in Jun's world — there's always a slight disproportion between being in a place and knowing you're in it. The awareness of yourself as the observer creates a gap between you and what you're observing." I paused, trying for the precision. "In the Garden there is no gap. I am the place as much as I am in it. The way — when you're doing fieldwork and you're fully attending to the substrate sample, and there's a moment where you stop being someone looking at the sample and become someone who is the act of looking — like that. But sustained. And spatial."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Is it frightening?" he asked.

"It was at first," I said. "Now it's more like — it's like finding out a word you've been using imprecisely your whole life actually has a precise meaning, and the precise meaning fits something you've been trying to describe. Not frightening. More like relief."

He looked at the ridge for a long time. The late-afternoon light on the Cascade zone in the distance, the specific amber of that world's cleared-air days.

"I don't have access to it," he said. "I never will."

"No."

"That's what I've been — part of what I've been sitting with." He exhaled slowly. "You have a relationship with this territory that I will never be able to have. You understand something about this forest that my entire career has only been approaching from the outside."

"You understand things about the forest I don't," I said. "I can read its spatial grammar. You understand its history. You know fifteen years of its behavior — you know when it was doing better and when it started doing worse and what the mechanisms were. That's not accessible to me from the crossing. The crossing gives me presence. It doesn't give me time."

He considered this. "Different instruments," he said.

"Yes."

"Same territory."

"Yes."

We stood for a while in the light, on the ridge above the forest that Liling had begun a conversation with fifty years before either of us arrived in it, the forest that the coalition was fighting for and that the Garden was already partly in the ground of, the forest that was dying and surviving simultaneously in the specific condition of things that are in the middle of whether they will live or not.

"I'm glad you're here," he said. Not performing it — stating it, in the flat register of the fact he had decided to report.

"I know," I said.

We walked back to the road in the last light. The work of the day was complete. The survey data was in Priya's samples and in my notebook and in the coalition's documentation system, and it would need analysis, and the analysis would need decisions, and the decisions would need time that the forest didn't fully have. This was the condition. This was the work.

The drive back was mostly quiet, which was the right kind of quiet — the quiet of people who have worked together in the field and are now carrying the accumulated intelligence of the day's observations, each in their own way, each doing with it what they need to do before it can become the next thing.
