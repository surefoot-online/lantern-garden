### Chapter Twenty-Seven · The Coalition Fractures

The meeting happened the following morning and I am going to describe it to the Tribunal in the way it happened, not in the way that makes it easier to understand. Easy understanding is a form of falsification when the thing being understood was not easy.

The meeting was called by Yiu.

I did not know that, going in. The morning briefings were Jun's domain — he ran them, he set the agenda, he had the final word on what the coalition discussed and when. When I arrived and found Yiu standing at the front of the room with the maps on the display and Jun sitting in the middle of the rows instead of at the front table, I understood that something had been negotiated before I arrived. The quality of the seating arrangements told me: this is not a normal briefing.

There were twelve people in the room. The full coalition, which I had not seen assembled since the session after the beetle-front merger. They had the look that the coalition always has in full assembly: the specific exhaustion of people who have been running on reduced sleep and high urgency for long enough that the exhaustion has become a baseline and they no longer notice it except as a slight roughness around the edges of everything.

Yiu began without preamble. This was itself informative — Yiu, who had been the most careful speaker in every coalition discussion I had attended, was speaking without his usual preparatory gestures. He had decided something and was delivering it.

"I'm proposing we abandon the eastern corridor re-route," he said.

He paused to let this register. It registered. The room absorbed it with the specific collective quiet of people who have heard the thing that was on everyone's mind said aloud.

"I've run the models for the alternate eastern routes," he said. "Both of them. With the best available substrate data and the most optimistic propagation estimates. The eastern re-route, via either alternate, will take eighteen months at minimum. Eighteen months to establish basic propagation in the corridor. Add six months for network connectivity. That's two years before the eastern route contributes anything meaningful to the coalition's objectives." He pointed at the map. "The ridge corridor, with Liling's matrix deployed by early winter, could be functioning as a propagation vector in eight months. Possibly six, if the existing substrate colonization Priya found is as extensive as the preliminary data suggests."

He looked at the room.

"I'm proposing we concentrate all available resources on the ridge," he said. "Let the eastern corridor go. Not abandon it permanently — acknowledge that we don't have the capacity to develop it on a timeline that matters, and stop spending resources on models and maintenance that aren't getting us where we need to go."

The room was very quiet.

Henriksen stood up.

I have been watching Henriksen since my first coalition meeting. He is older than most of the coalition's active members — he has been in this fight since before Yiu arrived, before Jun took over the coalition's direction, since the period when the Cascade's trajectory was still being debated rather than observed. He has the quality of long-running commitment: not idealism, which tends to deplete, but something more durable. The conviction that you show up because the alternative is not showing up, and you already know what not showing up looks like.

"I want to understand what we're trading away," he said. "Before we decide we're trading it."

"Eleven monitoring nodes in the eastern corridor," Yiu said. "Two sub-stations. Four years of substrate data. The propagation modeling for seven eastern cluster sites." He said this evenly — not without acknowledgment, but without drama. The way you read a balance sheet.

"And the people," Henriksen said. "The indigenous land stewards who have been working the eastern corridor with us since the beginning. The ranching families on the eastern edge who supported the coalition against significant local pressure. We committed to that corridor. There are people who organized their practices around our commitment to it."

"There are also people on the ridge," Yiu said. "The Stó:lō territory runs through the ridge corridor. They've been asking for more coalition presence at that elevation for two years."

"That's not the same as releasing our commitments on the eastern side."

"No," Yiu agreed. "It's not. I'm not saying it is."

The argument was not about the map. I want to be precise about this for the Tribunal. The argument was about what kind of organization the coalition was — whether it was a scientific body that made decisions on the basis of optimal resource allocation toward the most achievable ecological outcome, or whether it was a community organization that had relationships and responsibilities that shaped what decisions were available. Both frameworks were defensible. Both were also incomplete.

This is what I have found to be true of every difficult organizational decision I have witnessed: the argument appears to be about strategy, and it is also about identity. And identity arguments are harder than strategy arguments because they don't resolve by reference to data.

Jun was listening. He had not spoken since Yiu began. I had been watching him — sitting in the middle of the rows, where he had placed himself deliberately, not at the head where he would need to chair, not at the back where he could observe without accountability. The middle, where he was a member rather than a director, and his listening had the full weight of a man who had given fifteen years to something and was not going to shortcut the decision about its future.

"I want to hear from the people who have been running the eastern nodes," he said.

Two of them spoke. A woman named Bea who had been monitoring the eastern cluster's northern edge for three years, and a man named Gil whose family had owned the adjacent ranch for two generations and who had been a coalition ally since the beginning. Both of them described what they had built in the eastern corridor — the relationships, the trust, the specific kinds of knowledge that accumulate when people watch a piece of land closely for years. Both of them said, without drama, that walking away from the eastern corridor would cost things that could not be recovered.

Then Yiu said: "I know what it costs. I'm saying we can't afford the alternative."

The room sat with this.

Priya said, from her seat in the back: "The matrix deployment is eight weeks out, minimum. We don't have to make this decision today." She said it the way she offers information in any context — precisely, without advocating beyond the data. "The ridge culture is proceeding. We will have better numbers in two weeks. Making the eastern decision before we have the ridge numbers is making it with less information than we're going to have."

This was the most useful thing said in the meeting.

It did not resolve the argument. Arguments of this kind don't resolve in single meetings; they proceed, through time and evidence and the gradual accumulation of positions, to a decision that the parties can live with even if none of them got what they wanted. What Priya's observation did was create a frame: not yet. Not until we know more.

Henriksen sat down. Yiu gathered his papers. The meeting ended in the way that difficult meetings end when they have been useful without being resolved: the room dispersing, pockets of continued conversation forming near the coffee and near the door, the maps still on the display showing the territory as it was rather than as any of the arguments in the room would have it.

I stayed near the window.

This is what I want to tell the Tribunal: a coalition running out of time does not stop working. It does not stop caring. The fracture does not break the thing; it reveals the shape of what is holding the thing together. The coalition was, in that room, in that morning, demonstrating what it was: a collection of people with different frameworks and different commitments who had all independently decided that stopping was not available to them. The decision was not unanimous. The resources were not infinite. The time was not sufficient.

They kept working.

I recorded in my notebook: *The argument is not about the map.* I wrote it knowing it would make no sense to anyone who didn't have the room in their body the way I had it. I wrote it anyway.

Jun found me after, near the window.

"What do you think?" he said.

"About which argument?"

"Either."

"Yiu is right about the timeline," I said. "Henriksen is right about what the commitments cost. Priya is right that you should wait for better data before deciding." I paused. "You don't want me to tell you what to decide."

"No," he said. "But I want to know if you see something I'm missing."

I thought about it honestly. I thought about the ridge, the survey, the hyphal threads in Priya's sample. I thought about Bea's description of three years of watching and Gil's two-generation relationship with that particular piece of land.

"The eastern corridor may not need to be abandoned," I said. "It may need to be held differently. At lower resource cost, with the relationships maintained but the intensive monitoring reduced. Intermediate options between 'full commitment' and 'walking away' are usually available and usually get lost in arguments that frame it as binary."

He looked at me for a moment. Then he turned back toward the maps, still on the display, and picked up his tablet. Across the room, Priya was already on her phone. Henriksen had found Bea and was talking to her near the door. The work had resumed without anyone having resolved anything.
