### Chapter Twenty-Nine · Huimei and Liling

My mother had been in New Taipei for four days before I arrived.

She had not called me when she decided to go. She had simply booked the flight and gone, which was so unlike her — Huimei who considers, who plans, who runs the decision through its implications before she acts — that when the care facility nurse mentioned at the end of a call that "Mrs. Chen, your mother's daughter, she's been here every day this week," I had to sit for a moment with the fact of it. My mother, who had spent years finding the visits to the care facility too hard, had gone. On her own. Without telling me.

I booked the flight for the following day.

She was in the common room when I arrived — in the plastic chair beside the snake plant, with a book in her lap that she was not reading. She looked up when she heard my footsteps and her face did the thing it does when she sees me: the slight forward motion, the opening quality, the face's version of reaching. She stood.

We did not embrace for long. My mother and I have always been physical in the particular way of people who are physically restrained — brief, warm, precise. We sat down together near the snake plant.

"She's been good today," my mother said. "Calm. The respiratory issue resolved over the weekend, the doctor says the medication adjustment worked." She looked at the corridor. "She's been in her chair since morning. They said she seemed comfortable."

"Has she been present?" I asked.

"In and out. She knew me this morning, for a few minutes. She called me by name." My mother's voice had the quality of a person reporting something ordinary that is not ordinary. "I told her we were coming to visit together." She paused. "I don't know if she understood. But I told her."

We went to the room.

Liling was in the sea-glass chair. She looked older than the last time — the skin around her eyes thinner, the specific evidence of the past weeks' respiratory difficulty still present in the way she was sitting: slightly forward, the body finding its best position for the lungs. Her hands in her lap. Her eyes not on the wall's light rectangle but on her hands.

We came in and sat. My mother in the family chair, me in the second chair that the staff moved in, the one that is always slightly too close to the door.

Liling did not look up immediately.

My mother reached out and took her hand.

Liling's hands — the long fingers, the swollen joints, the thin skin. My mother's hands over them. The two pairs of hands in the room's afternoon light.

Then Liling looked up. She looked at my mother.

She was not in a lucid window. I want to be precise about this for the Tribunal, because the distinction matters: the two lucid windows I have described were specific physiological events — the chin angle, the field-station quality, the professional register of her speech. This was not that. This was something different. She was looking at my mother with recognition — true recognition, the face that knows the face it's seeing — but from within the dementia's haze rather than through a break in it.

The look was enough.

"Huimei," she said. Her daughter's name. Precisely.

My mother's hand tightened slightly on her mother's.

They sat looking at each other. Liling's face with the expression I had seen at the end of the Ch.23 visit — the quality of someone inside the room of a memory, occupying it rather than observing it — and my mother's face with something I had not seen before on her. Not the careful affect she usually brings to the care facility. Not the love that is held at a slight distance because the distance is the only way to keep functioning. Something more direct. The face of someone who has stopped managing the encounter and has arrived in it.

Then Liling said: "You smell like your father."

My mother went still.

Liling was looking at their joined hands. The interior quality deepened — she was somewhere, in the smell and the hands and whatever the dementia allowed of that particular kind of remembering: not the full cognitive reconstruction that the lucid windows permitted, but the body's knowledge, the older kind, the kind that lives below what language can reach. "Cedar," she said. "He smelled like cedar. From the forest." Her voice was quieter than the lucid windows, more fragmented, the syntax dissolving at the edges. "Patient man. Very patient."

My mother was not moving. I was not moving.

"Patient with everything," Liling said.

My mother made a sound — not speech, a sound. The sound of something landing. She brought Liling's hands up slightly, close to her chest, the gesture of someone receiving something.

Liling's eyes drifted. Not away, not into the agitated confusion — into the interior again, deeper. The hands held.

We sat there for a long time.

The afternoon light moved through the window in its incremental way. The corridor made its corridor sounds — the distant movement of the afternoon round, a voice further down the hall, the specific quality of a place that is organized around people who are very slowly going somewhere no one can follow.

After a while — I don't know how long, time in that room has never corresponded to the time outside it — Liling's eyes opened again. She looked at my mother's face.

"You are very like him," she said. "The good parts."

My mother cried. Not the quiet crying from before — this was quieter, something that didn't quite make sound but changed the quality of her breathing and the set of her face, and she did not try to stop it or manage it. She sat with it, holding her mother's hands.

Liling drifted away. The hands held. We stayed.

---

In the parking lot afterward, the New Taipei evening coming in from the east — the city's specific quality of light at that hour, the warmth that comes off the roads and the buildings even when the sun is low.

We sat in my mother's rental car without starting it. The parking lot of a care facility in the early evening is its own kind of place: neither the world you have come from nor the world inside, an intermediate zone that has the quality of all intermediate zones — the place where you sit before you decide to re-enter things.

My mother was looking at the care facility's entrance.

"Was she happy?" she said.

The question came from somewhere she had been a long time. I could hear the duration of it — all the years she had been not asking it, had been living with the not-asking, had been constructing around the absence of the question a life that was full and real and did not depend on the answer. Now she was asking.

I thought about what I knew. Not what I could infer or imagine or wanted to be true, but what I actually knew, from the ninety minutes in the sea-glass chair and from the briefer windows and from the field journal and from the way Liling had said *he stayed, I came back* — not with regret, but with the specificity of a fact she had lived inside and learned to hold.

"She had a full life," I said carefully. "She had work she loved — extraordinary work. She had someone she loved. She had three years of both." I paused. "She came back because she couldn't leave you to become without her. That's a choice that contains a lot of things — love and grief and the specific knowledge that you cannot unmake certain choices. Whether any of that adds up to happiness—"

I stopped. I was trying to be honest rather than kind, which is sometimes the same thing and sometimes not.

"I don't know what the word means," I said, "when the life is that particular shape."

My mother was quiet.

"It's not a shape happiness is usually applied to," I said. "You love someone and you leave. You come back to someone you also love. You carry both, for fifty years, in a silence that isn't quite secrecy and isn't quite peace. You plant something in the ground in a forest you'll never see again and you trust the future to send someone who will know what to find." I looked at the care facility. "That's a specific kind of life. I'm not sure happiness is the right instrument for measuring it."

My mother made a small sound. Agreement, maybe. Or the sound of recognizing that the question you've been carrying for decades might not have the answer you were looking for, and discovering that the absence of the answer is itself a kind of answer.

"She loved him," my mother said. It was a statement now, not the questioning form she had used before. A landing.

"Yes."

"And she loved me."

"Yes. More."

"I know." She said it without bitterness or competition — as information, received. "I believe that. I think I have always believed that, underneath the part of me that didn't want to." She looked at her hands in her lap. "I think I understand now."

"What do you understand?"

She was quiet for a moment. "That a life doesn't have to have a single shape to be a whole life. That you can go somewhere and come back changed, and the change is not a failure. That the things she lost she also carried, and carrying them was not the same as being defeated by them." She looked at me. "You know this. You already know this."

I thought about the Garden. The sitting in it. The luminescence. The spatial model in my notebook and the understanding that had arrived, finally, about what the suspension was and what it was not.

"Yes," I said. "I think I do."

"She was brave," my mother said. Not a question. "Whatever she was, she was brave."

"She was a scientist who walked into a garden because it was professionally interesting," I said. "And then she was a person who stayed for three years because the work was right and the person was right. And then she was a mother who came back because she could not leave her daughter to become without her." I paused. "That's not bravery, exactly. That's just — that's a life making itself. The choices one after another, each one following from the one before."

My mother looked at the care facility's entrance again. "I want to visit more," she said. "Even when she doesn't know me. I want to be there. I want—" She paused. "I think I've been staying away because I was protecting myself from what I felt when I was there. I don't want to protect myself anymore."

"Okay," I said.

"Will you come with me? When you can?"

"Yes," I said. "When I can, I'll come."

She started the car. We drove through the New Taipei evening toward the hotel, and she asked me about Jun, and I told her what I could tell her, which was the real parts without the parts that required a framework she didn't have. I told her about the ridge survey and the ridge's specific silence and the way the hyphal threads had looked in Priya's hand lens in the afternoon light. She listened with the particular quality she brings to things she doesn't fully understand but knows are important: complete attention, without comprehension as a prerequisite.

"He loves what he's fighting for," she said.

"Yes."

"Like she did."

I thought about Jun at the monitoring station in the early morning, sitting with his frame removed and his methodology revised and his grief held without drama in the specific discipline of someone who respects the truth enough to receive it. I thought about Liling at thirty-five, in the Vancouver park in 1972, walking into a garden because she was a botanist and it was professionally interesting.

"Yes," I said. "Like exactly that."
