### Chapter Thirty · The Corridor

The connector seeding went in over two weeks.

I was there for the first three days of it — the ridge sequence, the substrate preparation, the inoculation at the primary connector sites — and then I crossed back to Vancouver for the professional work that could not be deferred any longer, and I followed the monitoring data remotely, Sera sending the daily readings in the precise, data-forward format she used for everything.

The beetle-front held at elevation.

This was the conditional that had been carrying the most uncertainty — not the culture, which Priya had confirmed; not the substrate, which Thomas's route assessment had resolved. The beetle-front's temperature sensitivity was the variable that could not be modeled with certainty from the coalition's available data, because the temperature records at the relevant elevation were incomplete and the beetle populations' response to early cold was not fully characterized. It was the conditional that held the most weight.

On a Tuesday in late September, Sera's message arrived: *Front has not advanced above 1,100m. Temperature gradient consistent with beetle establishment threshold. Current estimate: front stable through November. Monitoring continues.*

I read the message three times.

Then I opened the spatial model — the corridor topology, the mycorrhizal network propagation vectors, the connector seeding sites and their projected establishment timelines. I ran the updated parameters through the propagation model with the new temperature constraint in place.

The corridor was viable.

Not complete — not saved, not guaranteed. The network would need years to establish full connectivity. The beetle-front would remain a pressure. The eastern alternate was narrower than the original plan and would require supplemental seeding in the second year. There were things that could still go wrong, and they needed to be in the model, and they were in the model.

But the corridor was viable. The probability distribution had shifted past the tipping point the coalition had been fighting to reach for three years. With the matrix deployed, with the connector seeding in the ground, with the beetle-front holding — the trajectory of the Cascade Collapse in the southern corridor had changed.

Not reversed. Changed. The forest was not going to be what it was. But it was not going to be what it had been heading toward. Something else was possible now.

I sent the model to Jun with a message that said: *The conditions are met. See attached.*

His response was four minutes and said nothing except: *Yes.*

I sat with that for a while.

Then I opened the Burrard corridor project files and spent the afternoon on the mycorrhizal connectivity specification, which the clients had approved in the latest revision and which the contractors would begin implementing in October. I wrote the implementation notes with the precision that Hana Park required — field-verifiable, specific, no ambiguity about the contractor's required actions. The work was good. The methodology was sound. I had learned the methodology in a forest that was fifty years further along the same trajectory, and I was applying it here, in my world, in the prevention phase, in the work that kept this city's version of the corridor from becoming Jun's world's version.

The prevention work. The same work from the other side of the emergency.

I worked until the late afternoon. Then I went to the park.

The park in late September had the quality that the park had in every season: the Douglas firs in the northeast quadrant going about their patient centenarian business, the ordinary proceeding of the city on all sides, the clearing with the lantern-plants unremarkable to anyone who didn't know what they were.

I stood at the clearing's edge.

Not crossing. Not tonight. I had crossed three times in the previous two weeks — the acknowledgment, the connector seeding, one evening at the monitoring station going through the updated model with Jun and Thomas. Three crossings in two weeks was within the range I maintained without the fatigue cascade that the early months had produced. I had learned the rhythm of it the way I learned any site: by being in it enough to know its logic.

I stood at the edge and looked at the Garden.

The lantern-plants in the late September afternoon. The threshold light that had no time-of-day quality — neither the grey of the morning nor the amber of the evening, just the Garden's own quality. The plants in their naturalistic distribution, arranged by the logic of a space that had been doing what it did for much longer than either world had a record of.

My grandmother had stood here fifty years ago. She had been thirty-five years old, standing at the edge of a garden she had found in a Vancouver park because she was a botanist and the garden contained species she had never seen. The specific posture I knew from the photograph: the slight forward lean, the weight on the front foot. The beginning of motion.

I stood at the edge and looked in with the full attention I had been learning to bring to it.

The Garden looked back. This was not metaphor. A space that you attend to fully receives your attention and reflects it back through its spatial qualities — the way the light behaves when you are watching it, the way the acoustic character of a space shifts when someone in it is fully present. The Garden reflected my attention back with the threshold light gathering slightly, with the lantern-plants doing the deepening luminescence that I had come to understand as the Garden's equivalent of a site saying *yes, you are reading me correctly.*

I stood at the edge for a while.

Then I crossed.

Not to Jun's world — not all the way through. Into the Garden, to the between-place, to the space that was my home in the sense that a person is at home in the place they return to when they have been other places long enough.

I sat in the Garden.

The luminescence was there — not dramatic, but present, the lantern-plants in their deepening quality, the between-light doing what it did. The space was the space. Larger than a transit-corridor reading could contain. The edges not visible from the interior, the way the edges of a forest were not visible from inside it.

I took out my notebook.

I had been mapping the Garden across a series of visits since the Tuesday in late summer when I had first sat in it and understood what it was. The map was incomplete — would be incomplete for some time, perhaps always, because the Garden was not a thing you could fully map from within it. You could chart what was visible from a given position, chart the positions themselves, build a picture of what could be seen and infer what could not. A landscape architect's approach to an unmappable site. The discipline of working with what was there and being precise about the limits.

I opened the notebook to the current page of the map.

I wrote: *September 23. Visibility from central position: consistent with previous visits. The connector between the two entry points — my world's park and Jun's forest edge — is not a straight line. It curves slightly east before reaching the far entry. I have been taking this path by habit; the slight eastward curve adds perhaps thirty seconds to the crossing interval. I do not know if this matters.*

I wrote: *The four Garden-specific fungal strains in the matrix. Priya's hypothesis: connector species. Translators. The ecology of the between-place contributes to the ecology of the worlds it connects. This means the Garden is not passive. It is an active participant in what moves through it.*

I wrote: *My grandmother's question, which is also my question: what can be learned from the Garden that has not been learned yet? The map is incomplete. The mapping continues.*

I sat for a while in the luminescence with the notebook in my lap.

The world was unresolved. The corridor was viable but not complete. The Tribunal's determination was open on two questions and closed on none of the hardest ones. Liling was gone in the form of a person who could be visited and was present in the form of what she had left — in the matrix, in the seven corridor strains and the four Garden strains, in the field notes she had written in 1974 that were now translated and in the coalition's archive and in the record before the Tribunal.

Jun was awake in his world, at the monitoring station or in the briefing room or at the north ridge, doing the work that was the correct work, with the morning data and the spatial models and the specific quality of his patience with difficult systems that I loved in him because it was the same quality I recognized as mine.

My mother was in Taipei, at her table in the apartment with the high ceilings and the road outside that ran toward the night market, beginning to look at certain things differently. She had not asked me the question yet. She would, eventually, and I would be ready to answer it, and the answer would be the version she could hold, and the version she could hold was real and true.

The forest was doing its invisible work.

I sat in the Garden and I let myself be where I was.

This is the correct answer to the correct question.

Not where do you live. Where are you.

Here.

In the between. In the Garden. In the suspension that is not suspension — in the most precise location I have ever occupied, the location that turns out to have been available all along, that was always home, that I had been moving through in the hurry to arrive somewhere else and that had been waiting for me to arrive here.

The lantern-plants in their luminescence. The threshold light. The space larger than its visible edges.

I am here.
