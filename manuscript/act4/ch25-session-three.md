### Chapter Twenty-Five · Session Three

The final session.

I arrived early, which was not like me — I was a precisely-on-time person, a function of professional training and the calendar discipline that the double life had required. But this morning I was early by fifteen minutes, and I stood in the corridor outside the hearing room and looked at the door and thought about doors.

Doors were not thresholds. The door was the thing itself: wood, hinge, a specific piece of architecture that was its own object, with its own properties, which you could examine in its own right rather than always in relation to what was on the other side of it.

I was about to walk through a door I had been walking toward for six months. The testimony would close. The Tribunal would begin its deliberations. The thing I had been building — the argument, the spatial model of the argument — would be handed over to a body that would do with it what bodies did.

I did not know what they would do.

I knew what I needed to have said.

---

The chair opened the final session with the questions she'd been reserving. They came in order, the order of a carefully structured inquiry: the factual questions first, the ones where precise answers were available. She had read everything. She had the supplemental materials marked. She asked about the seed bank's chain of custody — a legal term applied to something that had been in the ground for fifty-two years, which required a certain amount of lateral thinking to accommodate. I explained it the way I would explain an unusual site condition: here is what was found, here is the state it was found in, here is the evidence for the methodology's integrity.

"The chain of custody begins," I said, "with my grandmother's field notes, which were sealed with the preservation package and are written in her hand, using notation systems consistent with her published work between 1968 and 1972. The package's preservation method is consistent with the instructions she gave me in the lucid window I described in Session Two. The viability of the cultures is consistent with her stated methodology — she told me she had selected for species that would maintain viability in a low-nutrient mineral substrate; Priya Krishnamurthy's analysis confirms that the viable strains are precisely those species."

"And the source of the cultures," the chair said. "They came from your world."

"Yes."

"Species that do not naturally occur in Jun's world."

"Some of them don't. Seven of the eleven are species that were historically present in the corridor but have been reduced or absent since the fragmentation. Four are novel to the regional literature — they may be from my world, or from a period of the corridor's history that predates the current documentation. Priya is characterizing them."

"And you are introducing biological material from one world into another."

"My grandmother did," I said. "Fifty-two years ago. The introduction has already occurred. What the coalition is doing now is cultivating and deploying what she introduced."

The chair made a note.

"Ms. Chen," she said. "I want to ask you directly about the Tribunal's jurisdictional concern. The crossing capacity you've described — and the Garden through which crossings occur — have implications for both worlds that are not only ecological. The question of what flows between the worlds, and who has the right to determine what flows between them, is the question this Tribunal was constituted to address."

"I know," I said.

"You've argued that the Garden is prior to institutional jurisdiction. You've argued that your crossing capacity is an ontological condition rather than a choice. You've argued that the ecological work you've performed in Jun's world is a continuation of work that began before any institutional framework existed." She looked at me. "These arguments, taken together, appear to be arguing that the Tribunal has no authority over the crossings."

"No," I said.

"No, they don't appear to argue that, or no, the Tribunal has no authority?"

"They don't argue that the Tribunal has no authority. They argue that the Tribunal's authority is more limited than a literal reading of its charter would suggest." I paused. "The Tribunal was constituted to address the implications of the crossings for both worlds. I'm arguing that the crossings predate the Tribunal, that the ecological implications of the crossings were already in motion before the Tribunal existed, and that an effective response to those implications must acknowledge what was already in motion rather than starting from the legal fiction that the crossings began when the Tribunal began to count them."

The environmental law specialist was writing rapidly.

"Practically," I said, "I am asking the Tribunal for three things. First: that the seed bank's deployment by the coalition be recognized as a continuation of an intervention that began in 1974, not as a new introduction of biological material subject to present-day cross-world transfer protocols. Second: that the corridor's ecological significance — and the coalition's work to preserve it — be taken into account in whatever regulatory framework the Tribunal establishes for future crossings. Third: that the question of my place of residence not be answered by the Tribunal, because the question assumes a category that the Tribunal's frameworks cannot currently accommodate, and a false answer is worse than an open one."

I looked at the chair.

"That's what I'm asking," I said. "The rest of the testimony is descriptive. What I've just said is the argument."

The room was quiet for a long time.

The jurisdictional philosopher had stopped writing for the second time. He was looking at his notebook. He had the expression of someone who has been building a structure in his mind for several sessions and has just found the piece that clarifies which parts don't fit.

He said, without looking up: "The open question is better than the false answer."

"Yes," I said.

"That's a significant thing to ask a regulatory body to accept."

"I know. I am not asking you to accept it because it's comfortable. I'm asking you to accept it because it's correct."

---

The final hour.

Mäkelä, the ecologist, asked the questions I'd been waiting for — the ones that were not about jurisdiction, not about the Garden, but about the forest. What would happen without the corridor. What the failure mode looked like in terms the Tribunal could hold. She was asking, I understood, for the record — for the version of the testimony that would be read by people who would make decisions, who needed the stakes to be clear.

I told her.

"Without the corridor," I said, "the Cascade Collapse proceeds to a tipping point within three to five years at which active management can no longer affect the trajectory. The forest that eventually regenerates in the corridor zone will be a different ecological community — adapted to the new conditions, not to the conditions the existing ecosystem evolved for. The mycorrhizal network that supports the current community — the one that Liling's matrix was selected to restore — will not reconstitute in anything recognizable as the original composition. The succession goes forward, but it goes forward into a different kind of forest."

"And the corridor changes that," Mäkelä said.

"The corridor changes the probability distribution. It doesn't guarantee the outcome. The three conditionals I've described are not certain. They are the conditions under which the recovery scenario is possible. Without the corridor, the recovery scenario is not available at all." I paused. "What my grandmother gave the coalition is a chance. Not a certainty. She couldn't give certainty. She gave the most useful thing available, which was the correct species in sufficient viability for the correct site conditions. What the coalition does with it determines whether that gift is used."

Mäkelä wrote this down.

"And in your world," she said, "is there an equivalent collapse?"

I thought about the green roofs in Vancouver. The Burrard corridor. The northeast corner drainage that was technically correct and visually misleading, which I had spent two hours adjusting so that the underlying logic was legible in the surface form.

"In my world," I said, "the equivalent collapse is possible in a future that is close enough to work toward preventing. The mycorrhizal network science is understood. The intervention methodology is available. The question is whether the political will is there, at the scale and the speed that the science requires." I paused. "I do the prevention work in my world. It is the correct work. It is the work that keeps Jun's world's situation from becoming my world's future."

"Two sides of the same problem," Mäkelä said.

"Two stages of the same problem," I said. "My world is perhaps twenty years behind Jun's world in the trajectory. What the coalition can teach us about intervention methodology — what my grandmother's work has already taught us, across the fifty years — is applicable in my world as prevention. The crossing runs in both directions. The knowledge runs in both directions."

"Which is your argument," she said.

"Which is my argument," I said.

The chair closed her notebook.

"Ms. Chen," she said. "We have one remaining question from the original session. You were asked your place of residence and declined to answer. I'd like to put that question to you now, on the record, for the final session."

I had known this was coming. I had spent six months arriving at the answer.

"My place of residence," I said, "is the Lantern Garden."

The court reporter's keys went briefly. Then stopped.

"I want to elaborate on that answer," I said, "because I am aware that it does not fit the forms this body uses, and I want the record to reflect that the inadequacy is in the forms, not in the answer."

I looked at the two flags in the corners. I had spent enough time with them to have made my peace with their ambiguity.

"I sleep in a Vancouver apartment," I said. "I spend professional time in both worlds. I have family in Taipei. I have someone I love in Jun's world, and work I am committed to in both. But when the question is where I am *from,* where I am most precisely located, where the coordinate is that corresponds to what I am — the answer is the Garden. The space that is of both worlds and prior to both. The space that permits crossing. I am its resident in the sense that a person is a resident of a place they return to, not to visit but to be."

The chair looked at me for a long moment.

"That is not a recognized category of residence," she said.

"I know," I said.

She wrote in her notebook.

"The Tribunal will take this testimony under advisement," she said. "We will convene in closed session to deliberate and will issue our determination within thirty days." She looked up. "Is there anything else you wish the record to reflect before we close?"

I thought about this.

"I want the record to reflect," I said, "that the work being done in Jun's world is not my work. I am one element of a coalition that has been running this response for three years, under conditions that the word *difficult* does not adequately name. Jun Nakamura's coalition deserves to be seen in this record as the thing it is, which is a group of people who have been refusing to look away from something that would be easier to look away from. Whatever the Tribunal determines about jurisdiction and residence and the legal framework for the crossings — that work is real. What it is fighting for is real. The forest is real."

I paused.

"The record should reflect that."

The chair wrote.

The recording light went out.
