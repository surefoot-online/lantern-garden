### Chapter Twenty-Nine · The Acknowledgment

Thomas had prepared the acknowledgment over the preceding week.

I had not known what to expect — I had attended the preliminary site visit at the cedar and had been present when the matrix was found, but the formal acknowledgment was something Thomas had indicated he would prepare in his own time, in his own way, in the tradition that was his family's tradition and his community's tradition and that was not the coalition's methodology but was inseparable from the coalition's work.

We drove to the north ridge on a Monday morning. The full coalition — or the nearest thing to it: Jun, Priya, Luca, Sera, Thomas's assistant Mia, two of the ecologists from the university department. Ten people in two trucks on the logging roads, through the secondary growth, through the old-growth approach, to the trailhead that was not a formal trailhead and never had been.

Thomas set the pace. He moved through the forest with the ease I had observed on the first visit, the decades of this specific terrain in his body. The rest of the coalition moved behind him. I was near the front, beside Jun, the autumn light coming through the old-growth canopy in its specific way — cleaner now than the smoke season's peak, the quality of the light something that could almost be called clear.

The cedar was forty minutes in.

When we arrived, Thomas stood with his hand on the bark for a moment. The rest of us stood around him at a distance that felt correct — not too close, not performatively distant. The forest doing its autumn work: the deciduous understorey in its first color, the canopy above holding its needles with the patience of things that did not need to hurry.

Thomas began to speak.

He spoke in two languages, which I had half-expected and had not prepared for, which meant it moved through me without the management that preparation would have provided. The Halkomelem first — the language of his family's relation to this land, which I did not speak and did not need to speak because the register was clear, the specific quality of words being returned to the place where they originated. Then English — not a translation, something parallel, two approaches to the same acknowledgment from two directions.

He named the cedar. He named the ridge. He named the mycorrhizal network that had been here before the fragmentation and that the coalition was working to restore. He named the ones who had worked for this restoration: the coalition scientists, the land stewards, the people in both categories who were present on the ridge.

Then he named Tanaka Kenji.

He said the name with the specificity of someone naming a person who was not here and needed to be named into the space.

"Kenji Tanaka kept this site for thirty-four years. He maintained it without knowing its full significance, because he trusted that someone would come who would know. He was right. We received what he maintained." Thomas's voice was not formal; it was the voice of someone speaking from the place where the formal and the personal were not separate. "We receive it now. We will make the best use of it we know how to make."

He named Wǎng Liling.

He said the name in the way he had said Kenji's — with the full weight of someone present in the space who needed to be present in the naming. He described what she had done. He described the methodology with precision that told me he had read Priya's analysis and the translation of the field notes I had given the coalition. He had understood the science and the science was in the acknowledgment alongside the rest.

"She prepared this for the forest," he said. "Fifty years before we needed it. She understood that the forest would need it before the forest knew to ask." A pause. "We receive it. We will carry it forward."

Then he looked at me.

"Mei Chen." He said this with the same quality as everything else: not as ceremony, not as performance, as statement. The confirming quality. "You found what was left here. You understood what it was and you brought it to the people who could use it. You are part of this. Your grandmother is part of this. The capacity runs through the maternal line and it runs through this place and the two things are connected." He looked at the cedar. "The forest is connected. The people who love the forest are connected through the forest. This is what we know."

He placed both hands on the cedar's bark.

The coalition stood with him.

Jun was on my left. I was aware of him with the lateral attention that I had learned to give the people beside me without turning toward them — the spatial read, the body-knowledge of a presence. His hands were in his pockets. His face was turned toward the cedar. He had the quality of someone doing the thing that Thomas had described: being connected through the forest.

After a while Thomas removed his hands from the bark and stepped back.

"The culture is in the ground," he said. "The work continues."

---

We walked back in the changed light of early afternoon. Priya walked beside me for part of the way.

"The culture deployment timeline," she said. "I want to adjust the connector seeding sequence. Can you look at the topology tonight?"

"Yes," I said.

"I'm also running a supplemental viability assessment on the novel strains — the four I couldn't initially characterize." She glanced at me sideways. "I have a hypothesis."

"Tell me."

"The novel strains are not from your world's Pacific Rim population. I've been running comparative analysis against available databases and I'm not finding matches in any population I can identify." She paused. "My hypothesis is that they're from the Garden."

I stopped walking.

Priya stopped beside me. The rest of the group was ten meters ahead; we had the conversation in the specific acoustic privacy of being at a slight distance from the others.

"From the Garden," I said.

"The between-place. The liminal space." She said it with the same precision she brought to mycorrhizal viability assessments: this is the hypothesis, here is the evidence, the evidence is provisional but consistent. "The Garden has its own biology. The lantern-plants are a clear example — you've described them as Physalis-like but distinct. If the Garden has its own fungal community, it would be distinct from either world's populations. And a mycorrhizal scientist crossing through the Garden — spending time in the Garden, collecting specimens from the Garden — would potentially have access to strains that don't exist in either world."

I thought about Liling walking into the Garden because she was a botanist and a garden that appeared in a park and contained species she had never seen was professionally interesting.

"She went in," I said.

"Yes," Priya said. "And she was there for three years — crossing in both directions, spending time in the between-place. In three years, a mycorrhizal scientist with Liling's training and access to the Garden's substrate would have —"

"She would have characterized everything she could reach," I said. "That was her methodology. You don't encounter an undescribed fungal community and leave without characterizing it."

"No," Priya said. "You don't."

We stood for a moment in the old-growth section with the afternoon light coming through and the forest speaking its patient language.

"The Garden strains," I said. "If that's what they are. What are they doing in the matrix?"

"That's what I need to determine," Priya said. "My preliminary assessment is that they're connector species — they bridge the communication chemistry between the different mycorrhizal communities that the other strains represent. They're not the primary network builders. They're the translators." She looked at me steadily. "They make the other strains legible to each other. Strains from your world's Pacific Rim population, strains from this world's historic corridor community — the Garden strains are what allow them to speak a common language."

I held this.

"My grandmother," I said.

"Yes."

"She understood the network better than anyone in her field because she could read the forest's spatial grammar. And then she spent time in the Garden, which had its own biology, and she found what the Garden had to offer, and she included it in the matrix."

"I believe so," Priya said. "It will take more analysis to confirm."

The Garden had given Liling something to give the forest. Not a crossing. Not the capacity. Something biological, something that belonged to the between-place and that the between-place had, apparently, been producing without anyone to receive it until someone arrived who had the training to understand what they were finding.

I thought about the Garden's luminescence when I had sat in it. The lantern-plants' deepening glow under sustained attention. *The Garden is beautiful when it glows.* Not only the plants. The whole ecology of the between-place, doing what ecosystems do when someone who understands them is present: being visible.

My grandmother had been the first person to map it.

I was the second.

I walked back to the group.
