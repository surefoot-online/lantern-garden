### Chapter Thirty · Liling's Death

The call came on a Tuesday.

I was at the ridge. The second deployment day — we had suspended the first deployment for weather and resumed it on a three-day window that the meteorological models suggested would be the last stable period before the ridge elevation dropped below the working temperature threshold for the deployment equipment. I was at the seventh inoculation site, about two hundred meters north of the cedar, with my notebook open and the spatial model updating as each site was completed, when my phone rang.

The number was the care facility's main line. Not the nurse's direct line, which I would have recognized. The main line, which they use for certain kinds of calls.

I answered.

The voice was calm. The voice of someone whose professional task is the delivery of difficult information, performed with the care that the task requires. Liling had died that morning, in her bed, the way the doctor had indicated was likely: not in distress, not with drama, simply less present and then not present. Her daughter was there. They had been together since early morning.

I thanked the nurse. I stood at the seventh inoculation site for a moment with the phone in my hand and the notebook open and the ridge around me in its early-winter silence.

Then I went to find Jun.

He was at the fourth site, checking the penetration depth with Terrence. He looked up when I came toward him — read something in my face, or in my movement, the spatial reader's equivalent, which is what he does — and came to meet me.

"Liling," I said.

"When?"

"This morning. My mother was there."

He stood very still. Not the working stillness — the different stillness, the stillness of a person receiving something.

"Go," he said. "We have coverage. Go."

"The deployment—"

"Will happen. It's happening. Priya and Sol have the remaining sites. Go."

I went.

The flight from Vancouver — I crossed back first, through the Garden's early-winter quietness, arriving in Vancouver's morning and going directly to the airport — was four hours. I had a window seat and I sat in it and looked at the North Pacific below and did not sleep and did not plan and did not make any lists of what needed doing. I sat with the fact of it. Liling. My grandmother. The woman in the sea-glass chair who had organized her knowledge and delivered it in the order she had organized it, who had said *your memory will not hold everything the way it holds the spatial things, write it down*, who had said *the Garden is beautiful when it glows, I haven't seen it glow in a long time, you will*, who had looked at my mother from within the dementia's haze and said *you smell like your father.*

The fact of it: she was gone.

I landed and took the taxi to the care facility. My mother was in the common room — in the chair beside the snake plant where I had always found her when she waited. She stood when she saw me and I went to her and we held each other in the way we hold each other: brief, warm, precise, the precision of it its own form of saying the thing.

"Was she—" I said.

"Quiet," my mother said. "It was very quiet. She opened her eyes once, near the end. She looked—" She paused. "She looked at something. Something in the middle of the room. Her face changed." My mother was not crying; she had been crying, I could see, for some hours, and had arrived somewhere past it. "I've been trying to think of what to call what her face looked like. It wasn't peace, exactly. It was—"

"Recognition," I said.

My mother looked at me. "Yes. That's it. Recognition."

I had known, before she said it. I had known from the phone call, from the way the nurse had said she had seemed comfortable, from the specific quality of the way she had been going in the Garden — the between-place that she had been visiting in her dementia, the space that had been seeking her, that was quiet and that she had said she would stay in for a while, when she went. I had known the shape of it before I had the information to confirm it.

I did not tell my mother this. There was no language for it that would not require the framework she didn't have, and the moment was not the moment for framework. The moment was for sitting.

We went to the room.

She was in the bed, not the chair. The arrangement was careful and kind — the care facility had done what it does, with the gentleness that is its best quality. I sat in the family chair beside the bed. My mother sat in the second chair. The light from the window was the mid-morning light that I knew well from months of visits: the rectangle on the wall, warmer now because the cloud had cleared.

I sat with her for an hour.

I want to tell the Tribunal this plainly: I sat with her body for an hour in the room where the light moved on the wall, in the quiet that was not the absence of her but the presence of what she had been, all of it still in the room — the field notebooks I had transcribed, the fifty years of the crossing, the three years in Jun's world, the love and the choice and the matrix buried in the ridge and the daughter raised in Taipei and the granddaughter who had finally come to ask the right questions.

The grief was clean.

I mean this precisely. I do not mean it was simple or easy or painless. I mean it was coherent — the grief had the shape of the thing it grieved, and the shape of the thing was a whole shape, a life that had been lived from one end to the other with full attention, that had done what it needed to do, that had given everything it had to give. You grieve what was real. The grief was real. That meant she had been real.

She came back. She stayed. She left.

I did not tell the Tribunal the part about the recognition — the moment at the end when she looked at the middle of the room. This belongs to the family. The Tribunal does not need it, and what the Tribunal does not need I do not give it.

In the hallway afterward, waiting for my mother, a nurse came by on the afternoon round. A young woman I had seen before but didn't know by name. She asked, with the professional kindness that is the facility's best quality, whether there was anything I needed.

I said no, and then I said: "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"In the last days," I said. "Did she seem to be going somewhere? In the way that — I know this probably sounds—"

"No," the nurse said. She said it in a way that meant she knew what I was asking. "It doesn't sound strange. We see it. A lot of them, near the end — they seem to be moving toward something. Not away. Toward." She paused. "Your grandmother, the last few days — she seemed very comfortable. More than comfortable. She seemed to be waiting for something."

"I think she was," I said.

The nurse looked at me. She did not ask what I meant. The same understanding that does not require details: the willingness to receive someone's knowledge without making it conditional on explanation.

"She seemed peaceful," the nurse said. "That's the word that keeps coming to me."

"Yes," I said. "I think it's the right word."

My mother came out of the room. We walked together toward the exit, through the corridor and the common room with the snake plant, and out into the New Taipei afternoon, which was warm and ordinary and full of the city's specific sounds, and we stood in it together for a moment before we went to do the things that needed doing.
