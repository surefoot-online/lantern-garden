### Chapter Twenty-Eight · The Determination

The Tribunal's determination arrived twenty-seven days after the final session.

It came as a document. This was the form institutional determinations took: formal, precisely structured, the language calibrated to achieve a specific kind of legibility across the range of people who would receive and read and act on it. I read it the way I read any site document — for the spatial logic first, then for the details, then for what it said between the lines that were there and the lines that were not.

It was longer than I had expected. It ran to forty-two pages, which was unusual for a determination that could have been stated in a paragraph. The length was, itself, a statement: the Tribunal had encountered something that did not fit its frameworks and had responded by expanding the frameworks rather than simplifying the determination to fit the existing ones. This was, in my assessment, the correct response. It was also the harder one.

The operative section ran as follows:

*The Tribunal determines that the Lantern Garden, as described in testimony and supported by supplemental evidence, constitutes a liminal space of prior existence to any jurisdictional claim either world government may advance over the terrain it occupies. The Tribunal declines to assert jurisdiction over the Garden itself or over the crossing capacity described by the witness. The Tribunal further determines that the ecological intervention in Jun's world — the mycorrhizal seed bank preservation of 1974 and its subsequent deployment by the Cascade Coalition — does not fall within the scope of present-day cross-world transfer protocols, as the intervention predates those protocols by more than five decades.*

*With respect to the witness's future crossings: the Tribunal determines that the crossing capacity is an inherent characteristic of the witness's person, not a behavior subject to regulatory prohibition. Future crossings may be conducted without prior notification to the Tribunal, provided that any deliberate transfer of biological material between worlds be documented and reported to this body within thirty days of the transfer.*

*The question of the witness's place of residence remains open. The Tribunal notes the witness's characterization of the Lantern Garden as her place of residence and declines to rule on its adequacy as a jurisdictional answer pending further development of the relevant legal frameworks. The question is accordingly designated as open and ongoing.*

I read this twice. Then I read the supplemental findings, which contained, in Section IV:

*The Tribunal notes, with respect to the ecological situation in Jun's world, that the work of the Cascade Coalition represents an ongoing intervention in a situation of documented ecological significance. The Tribunal recommends that this body's successor, or an appropriate interworld environmental body to be constituted, consider the coalition's work as a matter of ongoing concern and provide such institutional support as may be consistent with the regulatory frameworks developed from the present determination.*

Not a mandate. A recommendation. An open door.

In institutional language, a recommendation in a determination was the most a Tribunal could offer without exceeding its brief. It was the door held open, the hand extended without requiring the receiving. The same form as the NTU administrator who had written *extended field research period* in 1975 and asked no further questions.

Someone had understood something, without understanding the specific thing. And they had written the language that permitted the continuation.

I sat for a long time with the determination in front of me.

Then I called Jun.

He answered immediately. "Tell me," he said.

I read him the operative section. I read him the recommendation in Section IV. I read him the sentence about the open question.

He was quiet for a long time.

"An open question," he said.

"The most useful kind," I said. "An answered question closes what it answers. An open one keeps the space available."

"The recommendation," he said. "An interworld environmental body."

"It's a recommendation, not a mandate. Whether it becomes anything depends on both governments' willingness to act on it. Which may take years. Or decades."

"But the record exists now," he said.

"The record exists. The testimony is there. Whatever institutional body eventually concerns itself with this — it will have the testimony as its starting point." I paused. "The corridor is in the record. Liling is in the record. What she planted is in the record."

He was quiet again.

"The corridor," he said. "The deployment."

"Tell me."

"Priya completed the ridge substrate preparation yesterday. The inoculation begins Monday." His voice had the quality I had learned to read as the closest thing to hope that Jun permitted himself: not optimism, not expectation. The specific alert quality of someone who has seen, across the full spatial model, that the probability distribution has shifted. "The eastern alternate is viable — Thomas's northern option confirmed. The connector seeding goes in next week." A pause. "If the beetle-front holds at current elevation for another six weeks—"

"Two conditionals," I said.

"Two conditionals," he said. "And the culture is in the ground."

I thought about Priya in the annex, running the culture through its growth curve, confirming the viability week by week with the same patience that Kenji had applied to walking to the cedar twice a week for thirty years. The same patience in different forms: maintaining the thing that needed maintaining, trusting that the next person would know what to do with it.

"I'm coming back," I said. "I want to see the inoculation. I want to see Priya put the culture in at the cedar."

"I was hoping you'd say that," he said. "Thomas wants to do the acknowledgment. The formal one. He's been waiting for you."

"Yes," I said. "I want to be there for that."

"When?"

"A week," I said. "I need a week here first."

"I'll see you in a week."

---

I spent the week in Vancouver.

Not managing the apartment or managing the professional calendar or managing the distance between the life I had and the life I was living. I was in the apartment, in the city, in the specific quality of Vancouver in late summer, which was the quality I had been moving through for two years without quite being there for it: the particular grey-green light of a Pacific coast city in the last days of August, the Douglas firs in the park maintaining their slow conversation with the weather, the neighbor's cat on the fence in the alley in the position that could not possibly be comfortable.

I worked on the Burrard project. I walked the site with the full attention of someone who was actually present. The site was doing what it was supposed to do. The drainage was working. The species were establishing. The spaces I had designed for inhabitation were being inhabited.

People eating lunch on the benches. A child running a spiral around one of the new street trees. A woman standing at the transition zone's south edge photographing the mountains.

Good design was invisible. It made its argument through the arrangement of things in space. The argument was received in the body before the mind registered that an argument had been made.

I walked the site. I took notes. I was present.

On Thursday evening I went to the park.

Not to cross — not tonight. Just to the park itself, in the late-summer evening, the firs in the northeast quadrant doing their patient work in the hour before dark. The lantern-plants in the clearing, low and dense and unremarkable to anyone who didn't know what they were.

I sat on the grass at the clearing's edge. The edge was its own location: the point where the park's ordinary existence and the Garden's specific existence touched without merging. I had been living at edges for years without understanding that the edge was not a compromise between two locations but a location in its own right.

I sat until the dark came all the way down. Then I walked home, made tea, and opened the substrate model for the eastern alternate that Thomas had sent me. I read the topology. I found what he had found and confirmed it — the northern route was narrower than the original but its substrate profile was better. The mycorrhizal network had a preference for this material.

I wrote to Thomas: *The northern route is correct. The narrowing is not a problem if the seeding density is adjusted at the transition point — I've marked it on the attached model. The substrate here will propagate faster than the wider eastern corridor would have. You may lose two months on timeline but gain three on network density at the junction.*

I sent the message and closed the laptop.

Then I picked up my phone and took the photograph I had been meaning to take since the hotel in New Taipei: the alley view from the kitchen window. The recycling bins. The neighbor's cat on the fence, still in the position that could not possibly be comfortable. The way the late-evening light came through at the alley's west end and made the ordinary shapes briefly something else.

The ordinary, doing its patient work.

I kept the photograph.
