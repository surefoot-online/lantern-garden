### Chapter Twenty-Eight · The Viable Matrix

The numbers came eleven days later and not fourteen, because Priya does not wait for a deadline she doesn't need.

She sent the message at six-forty in the morning: *Culture results. Come to the lab when you can. Significant.* I was already at the monitoring station — I had crossed the night before, late, arriving in the amber-dark of Jun's world's early spring and going directly to the hayloft room and sleeping until the equipment hum woke me at five. I read the message and put on my coat.

The coalition's field lab was in the building adjacent to the monitoring station: a converted storage structure that had been outfitted with basic mycological culture equipment by a coalition member who had donated two years of post-doctoral lab space to the effort. It was not a formal research facility — it was a working lab, which is different: purpose-built, stripped of anything not in service of the work, the surfaces clean with the specific cleanliness of a place where contamination is the enemy of the data.

Priya was at the culture bench when I arrived. She was not alone: Sol was there, and Terrence, and within ten minutes Jun arrived, and shortly after him, Henriksen. Word had moved through the small community of the monitoring station the way it does in small communities: not announced, but sensed.

"Come and look," Priya said. She was not the kind of person who withholds for effect; she had something to show and she was showing it.

On the culture medium — the specialized agar she had prepared according to the conditions Liling's field notes specified, with the precise mineral substrate ratios and the moisture levels calculated from the burial protocol — there were threads.

Hyphal threads. Not the faint traces from the ridge survey but the full, dense growth of an established culture. The medium was covered with it: the pale, branching network of living mycorrhizal fungi, growing outward from the central inoculation point in the specific pattern that Priya had taught me to recognize as vigorous colonization.

"Rhizopogon vinicolor and Pisolithus tinctorius," Priya said. "Both. The two primary ectomycorrhizal species from the matrix preparation. Both viable. Both actively colonizing." She pointed to two separate culture dishes. "I ran them in isolation first, then in combination. The combination is this one."

The combination dish had the densest growth.

"This is what she prepared," Priya said. "Fifty years ago. She prepared these two species to work together. The combination outperforms either individual species' colonization rate by approximately forty percent." She paused, with the specific quality of pause that precedes something she wants to be careful about. "I've read the literature on Pisolithus in long-term dormancy. The theoretical maximum viability in optimal conditions is considered to be around twenty to thirty years. What we have here is a culture from a fifty-year-old matrix that is growing at rates consistent with freshly prepared inoculant."

The room was quiet.

"She chose the species for dormancy resilience," Priya said. "And she preserved them in conditions that exceeded the theoretical optimum. The birch bark, the pine resin, the mineral substrate — she understood the chemistry. She understood it better than we do now, in some respects, because she understood what the matrix would need not for survival in a laboratory but for survival in soil, at depth, at a consistent sub-zero temperature, for a duration she calculated rather than guessed." She looked at the culture dishes. "This is extraordinary work. I want to say that for the record. This is some of the most precise mycological preservation work I have ever seen."

Jun was standing to my left. I did not look at him.

"Can we open the main sample now?" he said. His voice was careful — careful in the way he is careful when something matters more than he wants to show.

We had brought the outer birch bark package from the cedar to the lab intact; Priya had opened the outer wrapping to take the culture samples but had kept the main matrix sealed in its original material for this moment, when they would know whether the culture was viable before opening the full preparation.

Priya went to the storage box where the package had been kept at the preservation temperature the matrix required. She took it out and set it on the central bench. The birch bark was fifty years old — visibly aged, the white exterior darkened at the edges, the bark itself thickened and weathered in the way of things that have lived in soil. But intact. The pine resin seal at the wrapped edges was still present, still formed, still doing what it had been doing for fifty years: excluding oxygen, maintaining moisture, holding the matrix against the time when someone would open it and know what to do.

Priya opened it carefully. The way you open something that has been waiting.

Inside: the matrix. Dried and compressed, with the specific layered structure of a prepared mycorrhizal inoculant — the dark mineral substrate forming the base, the compressed mycelial material above it, the specific smell that I recognized from the first opening, the smell that had brought back the cedar and the ridge and the coordinates that Liling had given me: earth and resin and something alive, something that had been alive for fifty years in the dark and was still alive.

Folded within the matrix, the field notes.

I had transcribed the field notes at the ridge, reading Liling's handwriting aloud for Jun in real time while the coalition documented. Now Priya read the notes herself — read them with the focus of a mycologist reading another mycologist's work, with the specific quality of attention that recognizes craft.

"She notated the viability conditions for each species," Priya said. "The precise substrate ratios. The moisture percentages. The temperature ranges. The projected dormancy duration under each condition set." She turned a page. "She was modeling a fifty-year dormancy, and she was checking her model against the available science for each variable." A pause. "She had sources I don't recognize. She was ahead of what was published."

"She was working in Jun's world's conditions," I said. "The forest's specific ecology here — she would have had access to field observations that the published science didn't have. She was working from data no one in her world had collected."

Priya looked at me. "She took field notes. In her own world and here?"

"She was a botanist. She collected."

Priya looked back at the notes. Something in her expression — I had not seen this precise expression on Priya before. It was not the controlled affect she usually maintained in the field and the lab. It was something past the professional register.

"This is a gift," she said.

It was the word I had hoped someone would use and had not let myself use. Jun used it now, standing beside the central bench with his hands in his jacket pockets and his eyes on the open matrix and the field notes and the culture dishes behind us full of living things that had been preserved for fifty years by a woman who understood enough to know what to preserve.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

We stood there for a moment — all of us in the lab, the fieldwork technicians and Henriksen and Jun and Priya and me — in the specific quality of silence that comes when something you have been fighting for reveals itself as already present, already here, already doing its work in the ground where you were fighting over the ground.

Not salvation. A gift. The distinction matters.

Priya said: "I need six weeks. Possibly five. The culture is accelerating faster than I projected." She pulled herself back to the operational register with the purposefulness of someone who knows that the best response to a gift is to use it well. "If I have six weeks and the culture continues at this rate, I can produce enough inoculant for the primary deployment in the ridge corridor. With the existing substrate colonization that I found at the survey, the deployment should establish quickly. Faster than a normal deployment from scratch."

"Deployment by early winter," Jun said.

"Tight," Priya said. "But yes. Tight."

Jun looked at the culture dishes. "Then we start," he said.

The deployment was four weeks later.

I want to be honest with the Tribunal about the deployment: it was not a dramatic moment. It was not the kind of thing that announces itself as significant while it is happening. It was difficult, physical work in difficult terrain, in the first cold weather of the Cascade zone's early winter — the temperature at ridge elevation dropping below freezing at night and rising only marginally during the working hours, the ground hard where it had not been worked, the mineral layer requiring equipment to penetrate in the places where the hyphal expansion from Liling's original matrix had not yet softened it.

We worked in a team of eight: Priya, Jun, Henriksen, Sol, Terrence, two volunteer field workers from the university ecology department, and me. We worked in shifts — three people deploying while two rested and three prepared the next inoculation sites — and we worked from before dawn until the light was gone, for three consecutive days. The ridge corridor at deployment scale was demanding in the way that fieldwork always is: the gap between the spatial model and the actual terrain, the specific problems that only appear when you are on the ground, the adjustments that good fieldwork requires in real time.

I mapped continuously. I was the navigation system for the deployment team — adjusting the sequence of inoculation sites as we encountered terrain conditions that the model hadn't fully captured, identifying the optimal substrate penetration points, tracking the spatial coherence of the developing corridor as each site was completed. This was the work I was for, in this world, at this moment. I felt it as clearly as I have ever felt a skill at full use.

On the second afternoon, near the end of a long deployment sequence, working a section of ridge about three hundred meters from the cedar, I felt the Garden.

I want to be precise: I did not cross. I did not leave the deployment site, I did not step away from the team, I did not lose any time from the work. What happened was a quality of presence in the air around the cedar's vicinity — the specific quality that the Garden has when I am within its territory. The edge of it. The way the light changes, barely, and the acoustic quality shifts in the way I had learned to recognize.

The Garden was there. Not as a transit point, not as a threshold — as itself. As a place. It was at the ridge's coordinates because it had always been at the ridge's coordinates, because Liling had chosen the ridge because the Garden was there, because the Garden was there because the ridge had the substrate and the elevation and the forest's specific underground intelligence that the Garden recognized as home.

I kept working. I did not cross. I registered the presence and continued the inoculation sequence and did not mention it to anyone on the team.

What I understood in the moment — understood without stopping to understand it, in the continuous attending of focused work — was that the Garden had not been waiting for the coalition's deployment. The Garden had not needed us. The Garden had been at the ridge's coordinates for longer than the coalition had existed, longer than Jun's world had a name for the Cascade Collapse. The coalition's work mattered — it mattered enormously, it was the mechanism by which the forest on the human side of things would survive or not — but the Garden was doing something else. Something older and more patient and not contingent on whether the deployment succeeded or the eastern corridor was abandoned or the models were incomplete.

The Garden adjusts. It routes. It finds the viable path.

I had been trying, for months, to understand my relationship to the coalition's work through the lens of the crossing. I had been thinking: the crossing brought me here, the crossing is how I serve this world. But the crossing didn't bring me here to serve. The crossing brought me here because the crossing is what I do, and what I do landed me in the vicinity of work that needed what I had. The distinction sounds small. It is not small.

I am not instrumental. I am not the Garden's mechanism for aiding the coalition's mission. I am a person who crosses, who maps, who understands space, who loves Jun, who inherited the capacity from a woman who understood mycorrhizal networks before the field caught up to her. These are facts about what I am. What I do with them is choice.

I chose to be here, on this ridge, in this work. The Garden was also here. The distinction is the space between tool and person, between instrument and agent. I need the distinction to remain clear. To the Tribunal and to myself.
