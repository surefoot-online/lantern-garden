### Chapter Thirty-One · The Garden

I crossed the following week.

Not in emergency, not because the coalition needed the spatial model updated or the deployment documentation reviewed or any of the operational reasons I had been using to justify the crossings for months. I crossed because the Garden was where I needed to go, and because I had learned — in the sitting on the Tuesday morning, in the map growing in my notebook, in the understanding that had arrived gradually and then all at once in the between-place's luminescence — that needing to go to the Garden was its own sufficient reason.

My mother was in Taipei for the next two weeks, handling Liling's affairs. She had what she needed and she was managing with the purposeful competence she brings to practical necessities. I had flown back to Vancouver the day before: a single day to be in my own apartment, in my own world's light, to let the grief settle into the space it was going to occupy for the duration, which would be a long one.

Then I crossed.

I went to the park. The Tuesday-morning quality was not specific to Tuesdays, I had learned; it was specific to the attention I brought, which on any morning could be the full attention or the transit attention, and I brought the full attention this time. The garden in my world's park. The lantern-plants in their naturalistic distribution, the pale fruit translucent in the late-morning light. The quality of the air that changes when you are in the Garden's vicinity: the slight shift in the acoustic register, the barely perceptible difference in the light.

I went in.

Not through. To.

I sat down in the same place I had sat on the Tuesday, in the site analysis morning, and I did what I had done then: I attended. Not immediately to the spatial model, not to documentation. First: attending. Being present before being productive. The lesson I was still learning about the Garden, which required presence before it offered itself to be known.

The lantern-plants in their positions. The light. The quality of the substrate beneath me — the specific give of the Garden's ground, which is not quite earth but has the properties of earth, which holds. The acoustic quality at the edge of hearing: not intrusive, not demanding. Persistent, as I had written. Present.

I did not cross immediately to Jun's world.

I stayed.

There is a way that I have been in the Garden for months — the transit mode — and a way that I was learning to be in it: the inhabited mode. The difference is the difference between a person walking through a room to get to the door and a person who lives in the room. The transit mode is necessary, is functional, is not wrong. The inhabited mode is something different: a relationship to the space rather than a use of it.

I opened my notebook.

The spatial model I had been building since the Tuesday was on the first twelve pages. The distribution mapping, the light quality annotations, the acoustic observations, the two entry/exit points and their slight qualitative difference, the micro-topographic features I had been able to identify from the sitting position. A landscape architect's site analysis of a place that no instrument could measure and no building code described, a place that existed in the gap between the describable and the described.

I turned to a fresh page.

I began to draw.

Not the analytical mapping — the experiential mapping, which is a different document. Not the diagram of what the space does but the record of what the space is: the specific distribution of the lantern-plants as they presented themselves to me, not as points on a grid but as presences, each one in its position relative to the others, each one participating in the spatial grammar of the Garden in the way that the plants in any well-established naturalistic planting participate — not random, not designed, but arrived at over time through response to condition. The way a garden looks when it has been making itself for long enough to know what it wants to be.

I drew for what I estimated was four hours, accounting for the Garden's time-relationship with both worlds, which had stabilized into something I had learned to read. Four hours in Garden time corresponds to roughly ninety minutes in either world's calendar — the specific ratio was approximate, the mathematics of liminal space being what they are. I was aware of the time only as a background texture, not as a pressure.

What I found in the drawing, as I often find things in the drawing before I find them in the thinking:

The Garden was not symmetrical between the two worlds. The entry point from my world and the entry point from Jun's world were not equivalent. My world's entry point was at the Garden's center — or near its center; the Garden's edges being unknowable from the interior, I cannot say definitively where the center was. Jun's world's entry point was at the Garden's edge. Or what presented itself as an edge: a threshold quality, a density change in the lantern-plant distribution, a slight shift in the light.

This was new information. I had been thinking of the Garden as symmetrical — a corridor with equivalent doors at either end. The asymmetry meant something I was going to need time to understand. Not a hierarchy — not one world more central than the other. Something else. Something about the direction of the crossing's logic: entering from my world is entering the Garden's center. Entering from Jun's world is entering from the edge.

I wrote in my notebook: *Perhaps the crossing isn't between two equivalent points. Perhaps the Garden is located in my world's territory — or the capacity is, which amounts to the same thing. The crossing to Jun's world is moving outward from the Garden's center toward its edge. The return is moving inward.*

I wrote: *This would explain why the crossings are asymmetrical in their cost. Going to Jun's world costs more than returning. The fatigue gradient has always been directional. I have been assuming the cost is about the distance. Perhaps it's about the direction: outward from center requires more energy than inward toward it.*

I wrote: *Liling came back. She came inward, toward the center. She carried what she had learned and she came home, and home was the Garden's center, which corresponded to her world. I come inward, toward the center, and come home to my world. Jun's world is the outward edge. The work I do there is outward work — expansion, extension, the deployment of what the Garden's center contains toward the territory at its edge.*

I sat with this for a while.

The lantern-plants were glowing.

I had not registered the shift precisely — it had happened in the way the previous Tuesday's shift had happened, gradually, the way light changes in the afternoon, the way you notice it only when it has already shifted. The faint luminescence of the Garden's ordinary state had deepened into something more visible. Not the fairy-tale version of glowing — not anything that would strike someone as supernatural from a distance. But present. A quality of the lantern-plants that was not just the light reflecting off them but the light coming from within them, the bioluminescence that Liling had named and that I had now seen twice: once on the Tuesday and now.

I had stayed long enough, again. I had sat still long enough for the Garden to show me what it was.

I thought about Liling. In the Garden, in her dementia, in the crossing she had been doing from inside the care facility in the months before her death. She had been here, in this light, in this specific between-place. She had called it quiet. She had said she thought she would stay here for a while, when she went.

I looked at the lantern-plants in their luminescence and I tried to understand what she might have been experiencing in those visits. Not the same as my visits — she had been coming from within the dementia, from the parts of the brain that were still accessible, with the cognitive limitations of the late stages. Not the analytical presence I was bringing but something more essential, something that didn't require the full apparatus.

I chose to believe she had been here. I cannot know what the crossing permits from inside the dementia's late stages, cannot know whether what she experienced was the Garden in any form I would recognize, or whether the between-place takes a different shape when the cognitive apparatus that usually navigates it is no longer intact. But I chose to believe it, in the way you choose to believe some things before you have the language to test them: because it felt true, and because the alternative was a kind of loneliness I did not want to give her.

Whatever the crossing requires in terms of the body's intelligence — the spatial sense, the liminal organ, the structural capacity that Liling had called maternal and I had come to understand as ecological — it didn't require the cognitive apparatus to be intact. It ran deeper. It ran in the body's knowledge, the older kind, the kind below what language can reach.

If I was right, she had been in this light. In this quiet. She had been coming back to the between-place the way a person returns to the room they love best in the house they have lived in longest, the room that contains the most and asks the least. She had been resting here. She had been present here in the fullest way available to her.

I sat in the luminescence and I did not try to name what I felt. I let it be what it was: the specific quality of being in a place where someone you have loved has also been, where the space holds the residue of their attention, where the air itself carries something of what they understood.

She was gone. She had also been here.

Both were true.

I thought about the Tribunal, which had been present in my testimony since the beginning as the frame and destination of everything I was reporting. The Tribunal that would ask me about residency, jurisdiction, allegiance. The Tribunal that had been moving slowly enough that the Cascade Collapse had cost years of potential intervention. The Tribunal in its manufactured-grey room with its laminate table and its carefully neutral flags, trying to understand and manage and regulate something that did not fit the categories its forms were designed to receive.

I thought about what I would give them and what I would hold back. The coordinates had gone to the coalition. The seed bank was in the culture and would be in the ridge's ground. Jun's forest had what Liling had given it. The Tribunal would receive what I chose to give it, which would be enough to satisfy its questions and not enough to diminish what the Garden was.

The garden keeps what it keeps.

I am what I am: the crossing itself, in the form of a person. Not a mechanism, not a bridge, not an instrument of any ecology's logic. A person who crosses, who maps, who understands space, who loves someone fighting a losing war with a precision that refuses to be defeated, who inherited from her grandmother the organ that makes this possible, who sat in the between-place and let it glow.

I closed the notebook. I sat for a moment longer in the luminescence.

The lantern-plants did not dim. They simply continued to be what they were, the way the Garden continues to be what it is when the crosser moves away from it: present, complete, not contingent on being observed.

I thought: the beetle-front has closed the eastern corridor. The ridge deployment has begun. Priya's culture is in the ridge's ground. Jun is at the monitoring station with the overnight data and the bad coffee and the methodology that is larger now than when I met him, larger and less certain and more honest. My mother is in Taipei, sitting with her mother's absence and her mother's full life, learning what it is to love someone completely without needing their life to have been simple. Huimei — who does not know about the crossings, who knows about Kenji and the three years and the love and the return and the matrix, who knows the parts that could be given to her without the framework she doesn't have — is beginning to visit the care facility without armor. She is going to sit in the empty room and hold the space of it.

The forest continues to die, in the specific places it is dying.

The forest also, in the ridge corridor and the southern cluster and the seven orange monitoring flags and the growing hyphal network that Liling began fifty years ago, continues to live.

Liling is wherever she crossed to.

I am here.

I stood. I stood in the Garden for a moment, looking at the lantern-plants in their luminescence, the spatial grammar of the place I had finally learned to inhabit — not the transit version, not the between-as-corridor, but the between itself, the place in its own right, the home that runs in both directions and extends into both worlds and belongs to neither because it belongs to the crossing.

The lantern-plants marked the threshold.

I stepped through.
