### Chapter Twenty-Five · Jun's Grief

The monitoring station in the early morning has a specific sound. The equipment does its work through the night — the humidity sensors, the network telemetry, the overnight logging of temperature gradients at the substrate level — and by five in the morning the station has accumulated a kind of background hum that is neither mechanical nor alive, but somewhere in the register between. I had been waking to it for most of the past week.

Jun was already at the table when I came through from the hayloft room.

This was not unusual. Jun sleeps in the way he does everything: efficiently, without excess, getting the minimum required and returning to work. What was unusual was the quality of his sitting. He was not looking at his screen. He was looking at the table, or rather — I recognized this after a moment — he was looking at nothing. The screen was open but dark. The coffee mug was in his hands but not being drunk from. He was simply sitting, in the particular stillness of someone who has gone somewhere internal and is not ready to come back.

I did not say anything. I went to the small kitchen partition and poured coffee from the pot that Sera always set to brew at four-thirty, and I sat in the chair across from him, not the one beside him, because the chair across the table is for working conversations and the chair beside him is for proximity without agenda, and I was not sure yet which one he needed.

He looked up when I sat.

"Good morning," he said. His voice had the quality it carries when he hasn't slept enough — a slight roughness at the edges, the body reporting honestly where the mind is trying not to.

"Good morning."

We sat with the coffee. The equipment did its background work.

"I've been thinking," he said, after a while.

"I know."

He looked at me. "How do you know?"

"Because you're not moving," I said. "When you're not moving, you're not thinking about the work. The work makes you move."

Something shifted in his expression — the recognition of being seen accurately. He looked back at the table.

"I've been thinking about what you told me," he said. "About what the crossing is. What you are." He paused. "What Liling was."

I waited.

"I believe it," he said. "I want to be clear about that. I'm not sitting here because I've decided not to believe it — I've moved past that. I believe the crossings are real, I believe the Garden is a place, I believe you are what you are." He set down the coffee mug. "The part I'm sitting with is what it means for how I've been understanding the work."

I had known this was coming. I had known it from the day I'd explained everything in this room, from his careful listening and his measured responses and the particular silence he'd carried since — the silence of someone revising something deeply held.

"Tell me," I said.

"I've been an ecologist for fifteen years," he said. "My entire methodology is built on a foundational premise: that the systems we study are bounded. Measurable. That if we understand the parameters well enough, we can model the behavior, predict the outcomes, intervene with precision. That the work of conservation is about knowing the system well enough to help it." He looked at the dark screen. "The entire coalition is built on this premise. Every model Sera runs, every substrate analysis you've done, every culture Priya is running with Liling's matrix — all of it assumes a closed system. One forest, one crisis, one world."

"Yes," I said.

"And the crossing means that assumption is wrong," he said. It was not a question. "The system isn't closed. It's never been closed. Liling crossed fifty years ago and planted something in my world's soil, and that matrix has been sitting there changing the local mycorrhizal composition — changing it, Mei, even dormant — for fifty years. Every model I've run that didn't account for that was wrong. Not approximately wrong. Foundationally wrong."

I considered how to respond. I had thought about this in the three days in Vancouver, in the walking and the processing. I had not thought about what it would mean for Jun specifically — what it would cost someone whose methodology was his primary instrument for staying in relation to a world that was dying.

"The models were the best available," I said carefully. "With the information available."

"Yes," he said. "That's the problem. That's exactly the problem." He pressed his fingertips against the table — not drumming, but pressing, the way you press a tool against a surface to feel its resistance. "The best available information was incomplete in a way I couldn't have known. In a way no one could have known. But the gap between 'couldn't have known' and 'the model was wrong' doesn't close, Mei. The model was wrong. We have been making decisions based on it for years. We have been allocating resources and abandoning corridors and making arguments to policy bodies based on models that didn't include an entanglement that has been real for fifty years."

I said nothing. This was the correct response.

"The southern corridor," he said. "We almost abandoned it. You have the records from that meeting — before Liling's matrix changed what was possible, before we even knew the matrix existed, we came within two votes of abandoning the southern corridor entirely and concentrating everything on the northern route. If we had done that, we would have lost the one vector where the matrix is going to do its work." He looked at me. "We nearly lost it. Because our model was incomplete."

"But you didn't lose it," I said.

"We didn't. That's not a methodology. That's luck." He paused. "Or the crossing's logic. Or whatever Liling's science and your arrival add up to when you take them together. But I'm an ecologist. I can't build a methodology on 'we didn't lose it because something we didn't know about intervened.' I need to be able to say: here is what I know, here is what the model predicts, here is the intervention we are implementing based on that prediction. And I can't say that anymore. Not cleanly."

This was the grief. Not the anger from the argument after I'd explained the crossings — the anger had been protective, useful, the kind that comes when a framework is threatened and the mind defends it. This was what came after the defending: the specific quiet of a person who has laid down the weapon and is now standing in the open without it.

I thought about what Liling had said about the understanding that doesn't require details. The willingness to hold the door open without needing to know what you were doing in the cold.

I thought about what I did and did not know how to give him.

"Jun," I said. "Can I say something that might sound like comfort but is meant as information?"

He looked at me.

"The incompleteness of your models doesn't mean the methodology is wrong," I said. "It means the system is larger than you had mapped. That's a different problem." I paused, working toward the precision. "A navigator with a partial chart is not failing to navigate. A navigator with a partial chart, who doesn't know the chart is partial, is navigating with the best available instrument. When a new piece of the coastline is discovered, you add it to the chart. The methodology doesn't change — the data does."

"You're telling me to add the crossing to the chart."

"I'm telling you the crossing was always part of the territory. You're just learning its coordinates now."

He was quiet. The equipment hummed. Outside, the particular quality of Jun's world's early morning: the tree line against the sky, the amber-tinged light that was becoming familiar to me as just light, as the light of this specific place I was coming to know.

"The thing I trusted," he said, finally, was not at full volume — it was the sentence that comes from somewhere under the careful voice.

He stopped.

Not the pause of someone choosing words — the stop of someone who has reached the edge of them. He looked at his hands. I did not fill it. I sat in it with him, in the specific silence of something that was trying to be said and wasn't going to make it, and I let the silence be what it was.

"Was the frame," he said, eventually. "The ecological frame. The idea that if I learned the system well enough, I could fight it intelligently. That the intelligence was the weapon against the helplessness." He looked at his hands. "But the system was always bigger than I knew, and a bigger system means more vectors of loss. More ways the model fails. More ways the work I've been doing might have been aimed at the wrong target."

"Or not wrong," I said. "But incomplete."

"The difference between 'wrong' and 'incomplete' is very small when the forest is dying."

I received this. It was true. I did not try to argue with it.

"I know," I said.

"I don't need you to fix this," he said. And I understood from the precision of his saying it that it was what he needed to say, not a request or a limit but a fact about where he was. "I'm telling you because — I think you should know what it costs. To have the frame removed. Even if what replaces it might be better. The removal itself is its own kind of loss."

"I know," I said again.

"Do you?" He looked at me. Not challenging — genuinely asking.

I thought about what it had cost me to stop managing the crossings and start inhabiting them. The specific quality of the disorientation when the managed version collapsed. The three days in Vancouver, walking and processing, the recognition that the shape I'd been holding my life in wasn't the shape it needed. The grief of that — because it was a grief, the grief of a coherence that turns out to have been a performance.

"Yes," I said. "The shape you've been living in was real. The fact that it was incomplete doesn't make the living you did in it not real."

He looked at me for a long time.

"I loved it," he said. It was the most naked thing I had heard him say. "I loved having a frame. A clean methodology. A way to understand what I was fighting that gave me tools to fight with. I loved — I loved the feeling of being equal to what I was up against, even when the odds were wrong, because the methodology was sound." He paused. "I'm going to have to learn to work in a system I can't fully measure. That's not what I trained for."

"No," I said. "But it may be what the forest needs."

He absorbed this.

The morning advanced. The light came through the monitoring station's window and did what light does in that room at that hour — illuminated the table and the coffee and the man sitting across from me who was revising everything he believed about the work he had given his life to, revising it in real time, without drama, with the specific discipline of someone who respects the truth enough to receive it even when it costs him.

I did not try to make it easier. I had learned, in the months of managing the crossings and in the three days of letting the management go, that the attempt to ease someone's grief before it has completed itself is not kindness. It is impatience dressed as care.

I sat with him while he sat with it. I drank my coffee. The equipment hummed.

After a while he picked up his coffee mug and drank from it. He looked at the screen, which had gone dark in the interval, and tapped it back to life. The overnight telemetry appeared. He looked at it in the professional mode — the numbers, the trends, the significance or non-significance of what the night had produced.

"The substrate temperatures at the southern cluster," he said. "They held above establishment threshold overnight. Third night in a row."

"Good," I said.

"Priya said the culture is looking strong. She'll have preliminary numbers by end of week."

"Good."

He nodded once. The single nod — not the nod of someone who is done with what he's been sitting with, but the nod of someone who knows that the work continues alongside the sitting, that the two are not mutually exclusive, that a person can be in grief and do the work at the same time. That this, in fact, is what most of the work requires.

He did not say anything else about the frame. I did not ask him to.

We worked until the rest of the coalition arrived and the morning meeting began. What I did not tell him — what I understood sitting in that room while the equipment hummed and the light changed and he revised his world without once asking me to revise mine — was that loving someone does not mean offering them certainty. It means staying present while they find their way into a larger truth. It means sitting across the table instead of beside it when that is what the moment requires.

It means not fixing the grief.

The grief is also a form of knowledge.
