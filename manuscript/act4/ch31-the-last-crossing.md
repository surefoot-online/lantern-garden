### Chapter Thirty-One · The Last Crossing

I want to tell the Tribunal one more thing before the final statement.

There is a crossing I have not described in detail — not because it was secret, but because it was the last one before the testimony began, and the last crossing is different in kind from all the crossings that precede it. Not in mechanism. In what you carry when you go.

I crossed on a Sunday morning in November, six weeks after Liling died.

Not to Jun's world. I had crossed to Jun's world the previous Tuesday — a site visit to the north ridge with Priya, reviewing the first-season connectivity data from the deployed matrix. The data was what the model had predicted: establishing, fragile, real. The corridor was talking in its underground language, the network building at the edges, the new strains and the historic strains beginning to find each other through the connector species that Priya was now characterizing formally. The Garden strains. The translators.

Priya had looked at the data for a long time. Then she said: "The connector species are establishing faster than the primary strains."

"Of course they are," I said.

She looked at me sideways.

"Translators don't wait for permission," I said. "They establish wherever two languages are trying to speak."

She was quiet for a moment. "That's going in the paper," she said.

"It's not original — "

"It doesn't need to be original. It needs to be precise."

We drove back to the station in the particular silence that follows good field data: the silence of people who have been looking for something for a long time and have found evidence that it is doing what they hoped it would do. Not celebration — the scientific form of relief, which is quieter and more durable than celebration and costs less in the morning.

Jun met us at the station with coffee and the updated eastern corridor model, which had two new viable nodes that Thomas's team had found in the weekend survey. Four conditionals had become two had become zero: the corridor was viable, the culture was in the ground, the translators were establishing. What remained was the work — not the crisis-work, not the running-out-of-time emergency-response work that the coalition had been doing for three years. The maintenance work. The monitoring. The long-game work of a system that had been given the tools for its own recovery and was now beginning to use them.

"This is what it looks like," Jun said, looking at the new nodes, "when it's working."

"Yes," I said.

He looked at me. "You've seen it before."

"I've seen the theory of it. I've modeled the propagation. This is the first time I've been here when the data is coming back and confirming the theory."

"Is it different from what you expected?"

I thought about this. "It's quieter," I said.

He almost smiled. "It always is," he said. "The die-off is loud. The recovery is quiet. The die-off makes news. The recovery makes monitoring data." He set down the coffee mug. "Which is why you have to come back. Every season. You have to keep coming back and checking the data because the recovery won't announce itself."

"I know," I said.

"I'm saying it anyway," he said. "I'm saying it so it's said."

"I heard it," I said.

He nodded.

So it was not Jun's world I crossed to on the Sunday morning in November. I went to the Garden.

The Vancouver park in November had the specific quality of a Pacific coast autumn: everything wet, the leaf litter dark and dense, the smell of decay that was also the smell of soil rebuilding itself, the firs in the northeast quadrant unchanged because the firs did not do autumn in the deciduous sense and never had. The city moved around the park's edges in its Sunday-morning mode: unhurried, a kind of accumulated ease in the pace of people who had nowhere particular to be and were not performing the having-nowhere to be. I had always liked this quality of Sunday mornings. It was one of the things that Vancouver did that was specific to Vancouver.

I walked to the clearing.

The Garden in November was the Garden in November: the lantern-plants still present, lower to the ground now in the cold, the fruiting structures with the pendant droop of late season, but still there, still doing what they did, the bioluminescence faint in the grey morning light. I had been tracking the Garden across seasons for four months and the seasons affected the visible surface but not the underlying quality. The between-place was the between-place regardless of what November was doing to the plants at its edges.

I went in.

And I did not come back out the other side.

I sat in the Garden on a Sunday morning in November with the notebook in my lap and the damp cold of a Vancouver November around me, which was not the same as the Garden's cold — the Garden had its own temperature, its own relationship to cold and warmth, which I had been mapping alongside the visual topography — and I attended.

I want to describe what attending to the Garden looks like, for the record.

It looks like sitting with a notebook in your lap and not writing in it. It looks like the attention that a landscape architect brings to a site she is trying to understand before she tells it what she wants from it — the preliminary attention, the open attention, the non-directional receptivity that precedes the analysis. It looks like patience that is also presence: not waiting for something to happen, but being there in the quality that allows something to be seen.

I had been learning this. Not just in the Garden — in my work, in the sites I visited for the Burrard project and the Yaletown plaza and the green roofs and all the other pieces of the prevention work. I had been learning that the spatial reading I did was not something I performed on a site but something I arrived at by being in the site with sufficient quality of attention. The site spoke its language. The reading was the being-there, not the subsequent analysis.

The Garden spoke.

Not in language. Not in images or impressions. In the way a site speaks when you read it correctly: through the accumulation of what is there, which becomes, under sustained attention, legible as a grammar. The lantern-plants in their distribution, which I had been charting for months, began to make sense not as individual data points but as a pattern — the pattern of something that had been growing for a very long time in response to the conditions of the between-place, which were not the conditions of either world but were their own conditions, consistent and old. The plants were old. The substrate they grew in was old. The Garden had been here long before the crossing was a concept, long before Liling walked into it in 1972, long before anyone with the crossing capacity had found it and understood what it was.

The Garden was not waiting for crossers. It had been doing its own work all along. The crossers were an event in its history, not its purpose.

I wrote this in the notebook.

Then I wrote: *The Garden is a forest. Not in the botanical sense — it has no trees, no canopy, no fungal network in the soil the way Jun's forest has. But it has the logic of a forest: a self-sustaining system, ancient, responding to conditions that are internal to itself rather than to the conditions of either world at its coordinates. The lantern-plants are its trees. They grow where they grow because of the Garden's conditions, not the park's or the forest's. The luminescence is not bioluminescence in the standard sense — it is the Garden's light, native to this substrate, as natural here as the amber-haze is natural to Jun's world's sky.*

I wrote: *The crossing capacity finds the Garden the way water finds its drainage. Not because the Garden directs it. Because the spatial intelligence and the Garden's spatial nature are the same kind of thing. We recognize each other.*

I wrote: *My grandmother was here. Kenji was here. Park Jinhee was here, three years before she went home to her children. Whoever was here before them — whoever had the capacity in the generations before the colonial records started, before the institutional archives, before the documents that Jing-wei can find — they were here too. The Garden does not archive its visitors. But the Garden is the same Garden they found. They moved through it the same way I move through it. They recognized the threshold light.*

I sat for a long time with the notebook in my lap and the pen not moving.

The luminescence of the lantern-plants had reached the level I had come to think of as the Garden's full presence — not dramatic, not performative, just the specific glow of a place that is being seen correctly. I had described this to Jun once, trying to find the scientific equivalent, and he had said: *it sounds like what happens to the monitoring data when the network node is above threshold. The signal is there, you can read it, it's doing what it's supposed to do.*

It was like that. The Garden above threshold. The Garden being itself.

I thought about Liling, in her last days, going to this place without her body — in the between that her capacity still found even as the dementia took the rest. Sitting, or the equivalent of sitting. In the quiet that she had described: *It's quiet there.*

I thought: the quiet was not absence. The quiet was the sound of a very old system doing its very old work, which was not the same as silence. The forest was not silent. The Garden was not silent. They were both full of the specific noise of things living toward their purposes, which, from outside, could be read as quiet, but from inside was the most present noise there was.

I had been inside it, in every form, for two years.

I closed the notebook.

The Garden did what it always did when I indicated I was about to leave: it did not change. The plants in their luminescence. The between-light. The space larger than its visible edges. It was going to be here when I came back. It was going to be here when whoever came after me — the next person with the capacity, the next spatial reader who found the clearing in the park and walked in because the plants were unfamiliar and they were curious — found it and sat in it and understood.

The network was larger than the two forests. The network included this.

I rose.

I stood in the Garden for the last time before the testimony began — not the last time in the garden, the last time in the posture of someone who was not yet sure what she was or where she was from or what she owed the institution that was waiting to ask her questions. That posture was finished. I was not standing in the Garden as a person with a problem to resolve. I was standing in it as what I was: its resident, its mapper, the current holder of the cartographic project that Liling had begun in 1972 and Kenji had maintained and Park Jinhee had contributed to and that would outlast all three of them and outlast me and continue as long as there were people with the capacity to find the Garden and sit still in it long enough to read what it was doing.

The crossing is not a problem to be resolved.

It is what we are.

I crossed back to the park. The Vancouver November received me — the grey of it, the wet, the specific smell of the firs doing their patient work. I stood for a moment with the ordinary world completing itself around me: the dog-walkers on the path, the child on the swing in the playground at the park's south end, the city doing its Sunday business, entirely itself.

I walked home. I made tea. I sat at the kitchen table with the notebook and read what I had written.

Then I called the Tribunal's administrative office and confirmed my appearance for the following Monday.

There were things I was going to say. They were ready.
