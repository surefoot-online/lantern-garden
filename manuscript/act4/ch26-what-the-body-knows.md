### Chapter Twenty-Six · What the Body Knows

I had not told anyone when the final session was scheduled.

My mother did not know. Jun did not know — or rather, Jun knew I was giving testimony and had known since the sessions began, but he had not asked for dates, which was his form of respect for the process's integrity: the testimony had to be mine, given without the weight of someone else's stake in it sitting in my body while I spoke. I had been grateful for this. It was the kind of consideration that required no discussion to extend and no acknowledgment to receive.

After the session I took a taxi to the short-term rental where I'd been staying — a city I had chosen precisely because it was not Vancouver and not Taipei and not Jun's world. A city that was mine only for the duration of the testimony. A temporary home, which was the only kind I had made for myself in the months since the Garden had been named correctly.

I lay down on the rental apartment's bed without taking my coat off.

I had been holding the testimony for six months. Not as a burden — I had learned to be careful about that word. As a weight. The weight of a thing that has mass and needs to be carried with the correct distribution of effort, the correct understanding of where the load is, the correct posture for the carrying. I had been in the correct posture for six months and now the thing had been set down and my body was recalibrating.

The recalibration had a specific quality. I had experienced versions of it before — after every crossing in the first months, the heaviness that was not quite physical, the particular fatigue of something more essential than muscle having been worked. This was different: not the fatigue of the crossing itself but the fatigue of six months of sustained argument. Of holding everything in its correct position for the time it took to build the case. Of being, for six months, the version of myself that knew what to say and when to say it and what to withhold and why.

That version had done its job. I could put it down now.

I lay on the bed and let the recalibration happen.

The ceiling of the rental apartment was plain. Not architecturally interesting. Not doing anything except being there, which was what ceilings were for, and I had spent enough of the previous months in rooms that required reading — the Tribunal's manufactured grey, the coalition's map-covered walls, the corridors of the care facility with their institutional logic — that a plain ceiling was its own relief. I looked at it without trying to read anything into it. It was a ceiling. That was enough.

I thought about what I had said.

Not reviewing it for errors — the testimony was given, the record was made, the window for revision was closed. I was not a person who revised things after the revision window closed. That habit was part of the discipline: you brought everything you had to the work while the work was open, and when it closed, you released it. Priya had this quality. Jun had it in his own form. The coalition ran on it: the morning briefing was a live document; when the session ended and the day's work began, the briefing was past tense and you worked with what you had decided.

I thought about the testimony not to revise it but to inhabit it for the first time without the tension of preparation. The argument I had made was complete. It was the argument the situation required. I had presented the Garden's nature, the ecological work's significance, the continuity from Liling to the coalition to the corridor. I had presented Mei's nature — my nature — in the language of the terrain. The jurisdictional philosopher had stopped writing and said the open question was better than the false answer.

I believed this was true. I had believed it since the night I sat at the kitchen table in Vancouver with the closed manila folder in front of me and understood that what I had been building for six months was not a case for the Tribunal but a case for myself. The Tribunal was a vessel for what the case required saying. The saying of it out loud was the point.

And now it had been said.

I thought about Jun.

Not with the managed distance I'd been maintaining for months — the professional respect with its careful perimeter, the love that I had been treating as a thing to be documented and classified before it was permitted to be felt. That management was what I had been putting down, slowly, since the conversation at the monitoring station. The management had been necessary. It was not necessary anymore.

I thought about him with the full attention I had been rationing.

He was in his world, at the monitoring station, running the morning briefing with whatever the overnight data had produced. He had the map in front of him and the coalition around him and the specific exhaustion of someone who had been running a response for too long, which he carried with the steadiness that was not stubbornness but architecture — the way a load-bearing element carries weight: structurally, not dramatically, because the drama is not what's needed.

I thought about the morning in the old-growth section when he'd said *I've stopped asking how you get here.* The deliberateness of saying it aloud so it was no longer only in the room between them. He had been doing that throughout: making explicit the things that would otherwise be only ambient. The care in that. The understanding that some things required saying to exist in a form that could be received.

I had not been doing the same. I had been making him wait for the explicit thing — making him hold the knowledge of what we were in the same managed-distance form I'd been maintaining with everything else. The management had been necessary. I had been learning. But the learning was done, or was done enough, and the explicit thing was overdue.

I called him.

He picked up on the second ring. I could hear the station in the background — the specific acoustic signature of the briefing room.

"How did it go?" he said.

"I said what I came to say," I said. "The Tribunal is deliberating."

"Good." The good of someone receiving information they've been holding a question about. A brief pause. "How are you?"

"Lying on a bed in a rental apartment with my coat still on," I said.

"That sounds accurate."

"I wanted to tell you something," I said. "I should have said it earlier. I've been saying it in every form except the correct one."

He was quiet. The station background was very faint — he'd moved somewhere quieter, or was holding the phone close.

"I love you," I said. "I've been managing the distance between that and saying it because I was managing everything else and the management was necessary but it's not necessary now. I love you. I am going to continue crossing between worlds. I am going to continue doing the work in both. I am not going to ask you to be patient with an arrangement that is not what you wanted. But I wanted to say it specifically, out loud, on the record as it were."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I know," he said. His voice had the quality it had when he was saying a precise thing rather than a comfortable one. "I knew. I've been trying not to need you to say it until you were ready to say it."

"That's a considerable amount of patience."

"It's a considerable amount of believing you."

I looked at the rental apartment's ceiling. Plain. Doing its job.

"The corridor," I said. "Tell me."

"The culture deployment window is confirmed for early autumn," he said. "Priya ran the final viability assessment yesterday. All seven corridor strains are maintaining growth rate. She wants to start the ridge substrate preparation next week." A pause. "The eastern re-route is still uncertain — the alternate corridors are reading mixed substrate in the second survey. We may need to go further north."

"How far north?"

"Thomas is running options. I want you to look at the topology with me when you come back."

"I'll come back as soon as the Tribunal closes the deliberation period. Thirty days."

"Thirty days," he said. The two words carried the quality of someone marking a distance as manageable rather than comfortable. Thirty days was not nothing. Thirty days was what was needed.

"Call me if the substrate surveys come back before that. I can look at the models from here."

"I know you can," he said. "Mei."

"What."

"Thank you," he said. "For going. For saying what you said. For building the argument the way you built it." A pause. "You were describing the work in language the Tribunal could receive. I've been reading the transcripts. The way you translated the corridor's significance into a spatial argument. The way you described Liling's methodology." He paused again. "She would have been — she was the correct scientist to have done what she did. And you were the correct person to describe what she did."

I held the phone and looked at the ceiling.

"Go sleep," he said. "You've been awake for most of a week."

"I know."

"The models will still be there when you wake up."

"Mei." His voice shifted — not the monitoring-station voice, not the coalition-director voice. The voice I had heard in the old-growth section, in the dark, at the edge of the forest. "I can see it. What you're describing. I still don't fully have a framework for it. But I can see what you mean."

"That's enough," I said. "For now, that's enough."

After we hung up, I lay still for a while with the phone on my chest. The city outside was making its usual sounds — the traffic, the ambient hum of a place that had its own continuousness, entirely confident in its own proceeding. The rental apartment held the temporary quality of all temporary spaces: not quite belonging to anyone, available to be inhabited fully only if you were willing to be temporary in it.

I was good at temporary. I had been living in it for years.

I had lived in the between for years — had been living in it before I understood that the between was a home rather than an interval. The rental apartment with its plain ceiling was not where I lived, but I could inhabit it fully for the thirty days of the deliberation. This was the skill: not the belonging, which required permanence, but the full inhabitation of what was available. The tourist who walks a city with their whole attention, who sees what the resident misses by repetition. The crossing-person who enters each world fresh, carrying what the other world knows, which means she never quite stops seeing either one as extraordinary.

I thought about this and then I stopped thinking and let the recalibration do what recalibrations did.

I closed my eyes.

I slept for eleven hours without gaps, which was something I had not done since before the testimony began. The body, having been thanked, doing its work.
