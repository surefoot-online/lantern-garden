### Chapter Twenty-Four · What the Record Will Show

*From the testimony record, Session Three: "I want to address the chair's question directly before I continue. You asked, at the close of yesterday's session, whether my presence in Jun's world constituted a willful violation of interworld sovereignty protocols. I want to answer that question with the same precision I have brought to everything else I've told this body. The answer is: no. For the following reasons. In the order I'll give them."*

---

The Tribunal convened on a Monday.

I had prepared for this in the way I prepared for everything: by building the spatial model first. The testimony had a geometry — a sequence of disclosures in a specific order, each one load-bearing, each one positioned to support what came after. You did not begin with the conclusion. You began with the terrain.

The room was the room I'd been shown in advance: the manufactured grey, the laminate table, the two flags in the corners. I had looked at those flags enough times in the preceding weeks that I had developed a theory about them. They were not national flags — they were Tribunal flags, belonging to an institution rather than a jurisdiction, which was either reassuring or ominous depending on how you read institutional symbolism, and I had spent more than my professionally appropriate amount of time reading it.

The chair was the same woman I'd met at the preliminary session six weeks ago. She had the face of a person who had been listening to testimony for most of her professional life — not unkindly, not moved, exactly the right distance from everything. I had decided, in those six weeks, that this distance was not coldness. It was the professional form of patience: the particular neutrality that a person develops when they understand that their function is to receive rather than to respond. She was a vessel for testimony. She took its shape.

There were four other members of the panel. I had been given their professional backgrounds in advance: one environmental law specialist, one ecologist, one jurisdictional philosopher — a title I had not encountered before and which had taken three days of preparation to understand — and one policy administrator who said nothing in any session I attended and whose function I never fully determined. The administrator had a green thermos and a legal pad and the expression of someone who had made peace with the idea that most of what they would hear was beyond their existing frameworks.

I found this more reassuring than the others.

The court reporter was at a machine in the far corner. The recording light went red when the session began. I watched it go red and thought: now.

"Ms. Chen," the chair said. "We've reviewed the previous sessions' records, and we have a number of questions that remain open from that material. I'd like to begin, if you're willing, with the question of your first intentional crossing — not the involuntary crossings that began the testimony, but the first one where you understood what you were doing."

"I'll describe it," I said.

"For the record, what was your purpose in crossing that first time with full knowledge of the mechanism?"

I had prepared for this question. The preparation had taught me something I had not known when I started it: that the question was not actually about the purpose of a single crossing. It was the frame for the question the Tribunal had been building toward since Session One. *What was your purpose?* The institutional grammar of the question. The assumption embedded in it — that a purpose could be assigned to a thing that had been, at its beginning, more like a breath than a decision.

"My purpose," I said carefully, "in the first intentional crossing was to understand what my grandmother had found."

I paused. The court reporter's hands were over the keys.

"I want to be precise about what I mean by that," I said. "I was not crossing to conduct ecological research. I was not crossing to assist the coalition. I was not crossing because I had determined that assistance was my obligation. I was crossing because the photograph of my grandmother at the edge of the Garden had changed what the Garden was — from an involuntary phenomenon happening to me into something I had been preceded by. The photograph established lineage. I wanted to follow it."

The environmental law specialist leaned forward. He had the narrow attentiveness of someone who had been trained to locate the point in a statement where the argument's vulnerability appeared. "And the ecological work you subsequently performed in Jun's world — was that incidental to that purpose, or instrumental to it?"

"At first, incidental," I said. "Over time, instrumental. The work was the mechanism by which I understood what my grandmother had been doing there. She was a mycorrhizal scientist. The forest in Jun's world is where mycorrhizal science matters most immediately. To understand what she had found and what she had done, I had to do the work. Not to validate the work — it didn't need my validation. To understand its scale."

The chair made a note. The court reporter's keys went briefly.

"I want to address the jurisdictional question," I said. "Not because the Tribunal has raised it explicitly yet — though I expect it will — but because I think the order matters. The jurisdictional question cannot be answered correctly if it's asked before the ecological question. And the ecological question cannot be answered correctly if it's separated from the question of the Garden's nature. I'd like to take those in order."

The chair looked at me for a moment. She had the quality I had been watching since the first session: the complete receptivity, the holding-without-responding. She was receiving this.

"Proceed," she said.

---

I described the Lantern Garden.

Not as a crossing point. Not as a spatial anomaly. As a place — with its own topography, its own plant community, its own quality of light that could be mapped and described if the mapper was willing to sit still in it rather than move through it. I described the distribution of the lantern-plants, the acoustic quality of the space, the specific way that the Garden's dimensions exceeded what a transit-oriented visit would reveal.

I was a landscape architect. The description was precise. The chair had the expression of someone receiving precise description as a form of evidence and filing it accordingly.

"The Garden is not," I said, "a door between two worlds. That is the transit metaphor, and it is accurate enough for practical purposes but not for jurisdictional ones. The Garden is a space that exists in relation to both worlds without being contained by either. It occupies the same terrain in both — at identical coordinates in both — but it is not of either world's legal geography."

The jurisdictional philosopher looked up. He had been writing continuously since the session began, in a notebook that appeared to be running out of pages. "How do you establish that characterization?" he said. His voice was very precise, professionally so, the voice of someone who had spent his career interrogating the load-bearing elements of concepts.

"By the same methodology I use for any site characterization," I said. "Observation. Mapping. The relationship between the site's properties and its context. The Garden does not comply with the boundary conditions of either world. It persists — I've now verified this across multiple visits — regardless of the condition of either world's landscape at those coordinates. In my world, the corresponding coordinates are in a city park. In Jun's world, they are at the edge of a contested forest. The Garden exists at those coordinates in both conditions simultaneously, unchanged by either."

"That's a description of persistence," the philosopher said. "Not of jurisdictional independence."

"Persistence that precedes any jurisdiction either world has exercised is a form of independence," I said. "Not a philosophical claim — a fact about sequence. The Garden was there before the Tribunal existed. The Tribunal's jurisdiction is constructed; the Garden's existence is prior to the construction."

He was writing.

The chair said: "Ms. Chen. You said you wanted to address the ecological question before the jurisdictional one."

"Yes."

"Proceed."

---

I described the corridor.

I described it the way I had described it in the coalition's briefings: with the numbers, the propagation models, the substrate analysis, the sequence of interventions. But this was not a technical briefing. The Tribunal was not made of ecologists — one of them was, and she was leaning forward and I was speaking slightly toward her, using her attention as the anchor that kept the technical material from floating free. The others I spoke to in the spatial language: I gave them the geometry of the problem rather than the biology of it, because the geometry was what they had the frames to hold.

"The corridor," I said, "is the primary mechanism by which a temperate rainforest ecosystem in Jun's world has a viable path to recovery. Not a guaranteed path. The coalition's ecologists have been precise about this and I have been equally precise in my analysis: the corridor is necessary but not sufficient. It requires the matrix deployment, the beetle-front holding at the current elevation, and the connectivity between the established nodes and the new seeding to achieve threshold. Three conditionals."

"And the matrix," the chair said. "The seed bank you've described."

"Yes."

"You're asking this body to weigh the ecological significance of a preservation strategy that was executed fifty years ago by someone who is now deceased, in a world that has no institutional connection to this Tribunal's remit."

"I'm not asking this body to weigh it," I said. "I'm asking this body to receive it as evidence. The weighting is yours to do; the evidence is mine to present."

A slight movement in the chair's expression. The almost-expression that had been present in every session: something that was not humor and not admiration and was perhaps simply the recognition of a kind of precision she rarely received.

"My grandmother," I said, "was Dr. Wǎng Liling. She was a mycorrhizal scientist affiliated with the Taiwan Forestry Research Institute. She crossed into Jun's world in 1972, before this Tribunal existed, before any institutional framework for the crossings existed. She spent three years in the Cascade zone. She understood, from her training and from what she observed, that the mycorrhizal network of that forest was entering a fragmentation process that would, without intervention, result in the cascade failure the coalition is now fighting."

I paused.

"She did the only thing available to her. She prepared a mycorrhizal matrix — a preserved, sealed package of fungal cultures selected from the healthy equivalent ecosystems in her own world — and buried it at a specific site in the forest. She chose the site with the precision of a trained botanist: the elevation, the mineral substrate, the moisture gradient. She chose the preservation method with equal precision: birch bark, pine-resin seal, below frost depth, in a low-nutrient substrate that would suppress metabolic activity for decades. She wrote field notes and left them sealed with the package."

I looked at the panel.

"The package was found six weeks before I began this testimony. The cultures are viable. The coalition is in the process of deploying the matrix as the source inoculant for the corridor's northern bypass. Fifty-two years after my grandmother buried it. Fifty-two years after she crossed back to her world, had a child, and spent the rest of her career in the literature of mycorrhizal science that would eventually, two decades later, catch up to what she had understood in 1974."

The room was quiet.

The ecologist on the panel — Dr. Mäkelä, Finnish, the one I had been speaking toward — had stopped writing. She was looking at me with the expression of someone who has been processing a claim and has arrived at her assessment of its credibility.

"The viability assessment on the cultures," she said. "Who conducted it?"

"Priya Krishnamurthy, mycologist, coalition ecology department. Her analysis is in the supplemental materials filed with the Tribunal."

"I've read it," Mäkelä said.

"Then you know the characterization: seven of the eleven preserved strains are identifiable as historic northern Cascade corridor species, now absent from or severely reduced in the test zone. The preservation methodology was technically sound. Fifty-two years of suspended viability is within the documented range for ectomycorrhizal species preserved under optimal conditions." I paused. "My grandmother was twenty years ahead of her field. She preserved cultures using a method the field would not fully understand for another two decades. She got it right."

Mäkelä picked up her pen again. "I want to note for the record," she said, addressing the chair, "that the technical claim in the testimony is consistent with the supplemental analysis and is not, in my professional assessment, overstated."

The chair nodded.

"Continue," she said to me.

---

I told them about Kenji.

Not everything. There were things about Kenji that belonged to the space between Liling's memory and her dementia, to the region of what she had chosen to say and what she had chosen to hold. But there were things about Kenji that were material to the ecological record, and I gave those.

"Tanaka Kenji," I said, "was a Japanese forest ecologist. He crossed into Jun's world approximately four months before my grandmother. He stayed. He spent thirty-four years monitoring the preservation site — the cedar on the north ridge where my grandmother buried the matrix. He died in 2009."

The environmental law specialist looked up. "He stayed for thirty-four years."

"Yes."

"He was crossing from Japan."

"His original crossing was through the same Garden. Whether his continued presence in Jun's world involved additional crossings or whether he remained there continuously, I don't know. The journals don't specify. He was there from the early 1970s until his death."

"And he monitored the site without knowing what was in it."

"He knew what was in it in the general sense: that my grandmother had preserved something significant, something that a future researcher with the right training would know what to do with. He maintained the site because he trusted the pattern. He walked to the cedar twice a week for thirty years because that was what the trust required."

The law specialist wrote something in his notes.

"What I want the Tribunal to understand," I said, "is that the ecological intervention in Jun's world did not begin with me. It began with Tanaka Kenji, who crossed in 1972 and stayed, who had no institutional backing and no organizational structure and no framework except his own training and his own understanding of what the forest needed. And with my grandmother, who crossed the same year and did the same work and came back because she was pregnant and then spent fifty more years in her own world keeping the science alive until someone could find its application."

I looked at the chair.

"Neither of them was operating under an institutional directive. Neither of them had a legal framework for what they were doing. They were scientists who found a situation that their training told them they could address, and they addressed it. The ecological work I have been doing in Jun's world is their continuation, not its origin."

The chair was quiet for a moment.

"Ms. Chen," she said. "I want to return to the question of the Garden's nature. You've described it as a space that exists prior to institutional jurisdiction. I want to understand what you mean when you say you are — " she referred to her notes — " 'of the between.' That formulation appeared in your written submission. I want you to explain it in this session for the record."

I had been waiting for this.

---

"I am a landscape architect," I said. "The thing that makes me good at my work is the same thing that makes the crossings possible for me. I read space. Not metaphorically — literally. I read the spatial grammar of a site the way other people read the emotional grammar of a face. I see the argument a terrain is making in its arrangement, the history encoded in the distribution of things across a surface, the hidden structure that produces the visible form."

I looked at the two flags in the corners. "The crossing capacity is the same organ doing a different kind of reading. The Lantern Garden is a spatial phenomenon. A person with the spatial intelligence that I have — that my grandmother had, that Kenji had — encounters the Garden and reads it. The reading activates the crossing. This is not mysticism. It is the application of a perceptual faculty to a specific kind of space."

"And 'of the between,'" the chair said.

"I spent three years trying to understand the crossing as a problem to be resolved," I said. "Which world do I live in. Which world is mine. Where is my place of residence. I was asking a locational question about an ontological condition." I paused. "I am a spatial reader. The Garden is a spatial phenomenon. The between-place is where a spatial reader is most fully herself. I have never been fully in either world — I have always been reading both from some perspective that was not quite either of them. The crossing didn't create this condition. It revealed it. I am, in the most literal sense I know how to use, a person who is native to the space between fixed locations."

The room held this.

"Not homeless," I said. "Differently homed. The Garden is my home. The crossings are not expeditions from a home to a foreign territory. They are the full range of the home itself."

The jurisdictional philosopher had stopped writing. He was looking at me with the expression of someone whose professional life had prepared him for exactly this kind of claim and who was now determining whether this instance met the threshold for taking seriously.

"I want to ask you something," he said. "Not for the record — a philosophical clarification."

"For the record is fine," I said.

He looked at the chair. She nodded.

"You're describing a category of being," he said, "that the Tribunal's frameworks do not accommodate. You're not stateless — stateless persons have no national affiliation, but they are unambiguously of a specific world. You're not dual-national — dual nationals belong to two states but remain singular beings attached to single-world geography. You're describing something that has no institutional analogue." He paused. "My question is: do you think the appropriate response to that absence is for the category to be created, or for the category to remain outside institutional recognition?"

I thought about this carefully.

"I think," I said, "that the appropriate response is for institutions to acknowledge that the category exists before they decide what to do about it. The decision is yours. The existence is not."

He made a long note.

The session went on for another two hours. I answered the questions that had answers. For the ones that didn't — the ones that were asking me to produce certainties that the situation did not contain — I said precisely what the evidence supported and nothing more. I described the corridor's propagation timeline. I described the northern bypass. I described Priya's culture deployment plan and its three conditionals. I was careful about the conditionals. I did not promise what the data did not promise.

At the end of the session, the chair said: "We'll adjourn until Thursday. The panel will review the supplemental documentation and prepare questions for the final session."

The recording light went out.

I sat for a moment in the manufactured grey of the room, in the silence that followed the end of institutional proceedings, which had a specific quality — the silence of a room that has held a great deal and has now been emptied and is waiting to be used again for something else.

Then I stood. I gathered my materials — the annotated maps, the translated field notes, the photographs of the cedar on the ridge. I put them into the case I'd been carrying since the first session and walked out of the room, past the two Tribunal flags, down the corridor with its institutional carpet, out the main doors into the afternoon.

I went to find a café that had good tea and a window.

I had three days before the final session. Three days to understand what the testimony had built so far and what it needed to finish.

I took out my notebook and drew the corridor. Not the legal argument — the spatial one. The corridor as terrain, with its established nodes and its proposed bypass and the cedar at the north ridge and the fourteen viable points of the mycorrhizal network now confirmed and growing. I drew the shape of what was possible if all three conditionals held.

Then I drew the shape of what was possible if they didn't.

I held both shapes in mind, the way I held any site analysis before a critical presentation: the full range, without the sentimentality of pretending the failure mode was less likely than it was. The failure mode was never less likely than it was. The correct response to the failure mode was not to deny it. The correct response was to have it in the room, visible, accounted for, alongside the success case — so that whatever choice was made was made with the whole picture.

The corridor might hold. It might not. Priya's culture would deploy by mid-autumn if the ridge substrate preparation completed on schedule, which it would if the coalition could free the equipment from the eastern re-route assessment, which it could if the alternate corridors were confirmed viable in the next survey window, which Jun's team was currently running.

Three conditionals was better than five.

I drank my tea and watched the afternoon do what afternoons do: fill up with the ordinary proceeding of a city that did not know a Tribunal was sitting in one of its institutional rooms, adjudicating the nature of a space that most of the city would never see.

I thought about Liling, thirty-five years old, arriving at the Vancouver conference with her notebooks and the certainty that mycorrhizal networks were more intelligent than any published literature yet described. The city around her that did not know what she was. The city that had its own continuousness, which she moved through, carrying what she carried.

I thought about what it meant to carry something for fifty years. Not a burden — she had not said it was a burden. The weight of a tool that finally fits the hand. The particular weight of a thing whose mass is distributed correctly along the bones.

I closed my notebook and went back to the apartment.
