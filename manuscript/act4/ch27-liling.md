### Chapter Twenty-Seven · Liling

My mother called on a Thursday.

Not the final call. Not yet. But the call that preceded the final call by a specific interval — the call that meant: soon.

"She's not in distress," my mother said. "The doctor says her systems are slowing. She's comfortable. She's sleeping most of the time." A pause. "I've been here since yesterday morning."

"I'm coming," I said.

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." I was already reaching for the case I'd been living out of for the past month. "I'll be there this evening."

The flight was three hours with a connection. I sat by the window and watched the clouds below the plane and thought about what I was carrying.

The testimony was given. The Tribunal was deliberating. In Jun's world, the ridge substrate preparation had begun — Priya had sent me the protocol the previous week and I had reviewed it and confirmed the seeding sequence, my portion of the work done remotely with the maps and the substrate models on my screen in the rental apartment. The eastern re-route had found its northern option; Thomas had confirmed viable substrate in a survey the day before. The corridor was narrowing toward one path, which was not the path they had planned, but was the path available, which was the same as the right path given the actual conditions.

The forest was doing its invisible work.

I thought about the difference between being a person going to sit with someone who was dying and being a person who had understood, for perhaps the first time, that dying was not the same as the end. Liling had been telling me this for months — in the confused windows and the lucid ones, in the precise language of a scientist who had been in the between-place more times than she had language left to describe. *The Garden doesn't require full consciousness. It seems to seek her.*

What she had said, in the last lucid window, about the between-place: *It's quiet there. I think I'll stay there for a while. When I go.*

I had told Jun about this. He had been quiet for a long time. *She hasn't lost it,* he'd said. *Whatever the dementia has taken. She hasn't lost the capacity.*

I had chosen to believe this. Not because I could verify it — I couldn't — but because belief was the correct response to a thing that was real but not fully available to evidence. I had been trained to be precise about the distinction between evidence and belief. The distinction was important. But the trained precision could become its own evasion: the refusal to hold something as true because you could not hold it as proven was not rigor. It was the fear of the thing itself.

I believed that wherever Liling went when she drifted — in the long interior periods, in the confusion that was something other than confusion — it was somewhere she recognized. Somewhere she had been. Somewhere she had planted something and watched it take.

---

My mother met me at the entrance.

She had the look of someone who had been in a particular emotional state for so long that the state had become the context rather than the content — the specific quality of a long vigil, which produces a particular kind of calm that is not the absence of feeling but its long accommodation. She had been here since the day before. She was tired and she was very present.

We did not speak in the corridor. There was nothing to say yet; the saying would come later, in the order things came. We walked to Liling's room and went in.

She was smaller.

This was the thing that was not in any medical description of the dying process but that was real: the person becomes smaller. Not dramatically — not visibly diminished in any measurable way. But somehow less space-filling. As if the body, in its last period, was returning something to the air.

She was in the bed — not the sea-glass chair, not the position of someone attending to something. She was in the bed, which was its own statement about the shift. Her hands were on the blanket. Her breathing was the slow, deliberate breathing of a body that was attending carefully to the act.

I sat in the chair by the bed. My mother sat in the second chair. We had done this before. The positions were familiar.

Liling's eyes were closed. Her breathing was even — not labored, the nurse had said, not in distress. Simply slow. The body's own pace, unhurried.

I took her hand.

The hand was warm. The hands of a botanist: the long fingers, the decades of callus. I knew these hands. I had been studying them since childhood with the spatial attention I brought to things that mattered, that attention doing its patient work of learning the grammar of the object — where the weight was, where the history showed in the surface, what the form said about the history of use.

Her hands said: soil, tools, close attention to small things. Fifty years of soil and tools and close attention. Twenty years in the literature that the field hadn't caught up to. Three years in a different world, burying something in the ground that would wait for fifty-two years for someone to find it.

My grandmother's hands.

She did not open her eyes. But her hand, in mine, tightened slightly — the specific quality of the slight tightening that was its own form of acknowledgment. Not the confirming gesture; she did not have the coordination for that now. But the pressure. The signal through the hands that said: received.

My mother was looking at those joined hands.

We sat.

The room did what rooms did in this situation: it held time differently. Not slowly, not the stretched quality of anticipatory waiting. Just differently — the room organized itself around the single fact of the person in it who was in the last stage of the last process, and everything else — the institutional sounds of the corridor, the light through the window, the small sounds of the city outside — arranged itself in relation to that fact, not interrupting it, maintaining its distance.

An hour passed. My mother and I did not speak. She held Liling's other hand. Liling's breathing continued its slow deliberate work.

Then — not dramatically, not with the quality of a specific moment beginning — her breathing changed.

The nurse had told us what to listen for. We had been listening. The breathing had been changing gradually for the previous hour, the intervals slightly longer, the depth slightly shallower, the pattern that the nurse had described as the body's own accounting: going through its reserves, doing the thing it had always done with great efficiency.

I held her hand.

My mother held her hand.

The light in the room was the late-afternoon light — the rectangle on the wall that had moved through its arc over the hours we'd been there, the specific angle of a Taipei late afternoon coming through a northwest-facing window, a quality of light that had no regional character and also had exactly the regional character of Taipei at this time of day in this season. A warm, dry light that did not perform anything. It was simply there.

Liling's eyes opened.

Not fully. Partway — the specific opening of eyes that are not quite registering the room's visual content but are attending to something. She looked at a point in the middle distance, at a slight angle from either of us, in the direction that was not toward the window and not toward the door.

In the direction of the space between.

Her face changed.

Not her expression — not the movement of facial muscles that produces expressions. Something beneath that: the shift in a face when the person occupying it arrives at something. The way a face looks when it has found what it was looking for.

Something she had been expecting. Someone she had been expecting.

I looked at her face and I understood.

Then she was gone.

It was not slow; it was not sudden. It was simply the transition from breathing to not breathing, from presence to the specific quality of a room from which the animating thing has departed and left the room in the particular silence of its absence. The silence was not empty. It was full of what had just been there, still present in the way of things that have occupied a space for a long time: the specific gravity of a person's having been, which persists for a while, which you can feel if you are paying attention.

I held her hand. The warmth was still there. The calluses. The long fingers.

My mother's breath caught once, quietly, and then she was still. She held Liling's other hand with both of hers.

Outside, the city continued. The corridor sounds continued. The light on the wall made its slow movement as the sun descended toward the evening.

I did not think. I attended.

After a while I became aware of a quality in the space that I recognized — not the Garden itself, not the threshold light, not the liminal presence I encountered when I crossed. Something quieter than that. A resonance. The quality of a room that has just been visited by something the room's ordinary physics did not require.

She had known where she was going. She had been practicing.

*It's quiet there. I think I'll stay there for a while.*

I stayed in the quality of that for some time, sitting with the stillness that had replaced her, not trying to name it, not trying to verify it. Some realizations arrive in language and some arrive in a form that language would diminish. I let this one be what it was: received, confirmed, held.

After a while my mother lifted my grandmother's hand and held it against her own cheek for a moment. The gesture of someone saying the last thing in the language of the body when the body-language is what's left. Then she set it down carefully on the blanket.

She looked at me.

I looked at her.

There was nothing to say. We were two people who had been given, by the same woman, the thing she had to give, and the giving was done, and the receiving was done, and what remained was the life that followed. My mother knew a version of the truth that was real and true and enough. I knew a version that was larger and that included hers.

Both versions ended the same way: with a woman who had crossed the worlds and planted something and come back and been present for sixty years and known exactly, at the end, where she was going.

After a long time, my mother said: "She found what she was looking for."

"Yes," I said.

"Not Kenji. Not the forest." She looked at the window. "She was looking for the place where she could stop being between things."

"Yes," I said again.

"And she found it."

"I think she'd been there already," I said. "I think she'd been practicing."

My mother looked at me. There was something in her face — the specific something I had come to recognize as the expression she produced when she was about to not ask the question that was adjacent to the askable one. The wisdom of her: knowing when not to ask. Knowing that some things would become available in their own time and that asking before they were ready would not produce them.

She stood. She pressed her hand against my shoulder once, briefly. The same gesture. Still not knowing it was the same gesture.

"Tell me," she said, "when you have the language for what you know. When it's ready."

"I will," I said.

She went to find the nurse.

I stayed.

---

The light on the wall moved another degree. The corridor sounds continued. The city outside ran its evening, into its night.

I sat with my grandmother's hand for a while longer.

She had been precise about everything. The seed bank coordinates. The preservation methodology. The species selection. The choice to come back. The choice to speak to me in the long window and tell me what she'd held for fifty years and never told her daughter, because she'd been waiting for the right vessel — not her daughter, who would not have had the frames to hold it, but me, who did, who had arrived at the frames through a combination of professional training and the crossing itself and the specific attention I paid to spatial things.

She had been precise about the leaving, too. I understood this now, sitting with the stillness that had replaced her. The leaving was not a failure of the crossing capacity, not a loss of what she was. She was still what she was. The capacity didn't end because the body did. The Garden didn't lose its residents when they ceased to occupy it in the physical sense.

*The crossing is not a problem to be resolved. It is what we are.*

What we are does not end.

I stood. I arranged her hands on the blanket with the care I would give to any important spatial decision: with attention to what was already there, adding nothing unnecessary, leaving everything in its correct position.

Her hands. The long fingers. The calluses of fifty years of close attention to small things.

I leaned down and kissed her forehead.

"Good," I said, in her word, in the word she always used for findings confirmed. Then I went to find my mother.
