### Chapter Twenty-Two · The Hana Park Question

The professional crisis arrived on a Thursday.

I had been managing it for two months — managing it the way I had been managing the rest of my Vancouver life, with the competence of someone who understood the situation and was doing the work required to prevent it from tipping over. Hana Park had been patient. The firm had been patient. The Burrard corridor project had been patient in the way that large urban projects are patient: slowly, expensively, with occasional emails that were professional in tone and urgent in subtext.

On Thursday, Hana Park called rather than emailed.

She called in the late morning, which was when she made calls that were not comfortable in email. I was in my apartment, working on the substrate model update that I owed Jun's coalition by the end of the week. I saw her name on the screen and I felt the specific quality of a reckoning that has been deferred long enough to become unavoidable.

"Mei," she said. "I need to see you this week."

"Yes," I said. "What day works?"

There was a brief pause — the pause of someone who had prepared for more negotiation and has received agreement. "Friday," she said. "Morning. I want to do a proper site visit on the Burrard project. There are things I need to see in person."

"I'll be there," I said.

The call was seven minutes long. The site visit the following day was three hours. Hana Park walked the Burrard corridor with me and asked the questions she had been waiting to ask in person: the drainage resolution in the northeast corner, which I had specified in the documents but which required a physical explanation of why the specification was correct, because the specification was counterintuitive and the contractors had been asking questions that she had been relaying without my full presence to back them up. The treatment of the transition zone at the south end, which I had designed to accommodate a microclimate condition that the original brief had not specified but that my reading of the site had identified as significant.

I answered every question. I was present. I was there in the full professional sense — not managing my presence, not allocating the attention the way I had been allocating it for months, but actually there, in the physical space of the project, reading it the way I read spaces.

Hana Park watched me do this and did not say anything for a long time.

Then, at the end of the site visit, standing at the transition zone's south edge with the view of the water and the mountains that the project had been designed to frame, she said: "You've been somewhere else for months."

It was not an accusation. She was stating a fact she had been observing.

"Yes," I said.

"I don't need to know where," she said. "I need to know that you're here now."

"I'm here now," I said.

She looked at me with the assessment expression — the one she used when she was determining whether a statement was professionally reliable or personally true. Hana Park had been running this firm for twenty-three years. She had learned to distinguish the two.

"You're here now," she said, "and you're going to be somewhere else again."

"Yes."

"Will you still be able to do the work?"

I thought about this carefully. "The work in this world," I said, "is the other half of the work I'm doing somewhere else. It's not separate from it. It's — the prevention side of the same problem. The emergency version is somewhere else. The prevention work is here." I paused. "I don't think I can stop doing either. I think I need to do both or neither will be done correctly."

She was quiet for a long time, looking at the view the project had been designed to frame.

"Is the somewhere else," she said, "something you can bring to this office?"

"Not directly," I said. "But what I understand from being there — the scale of what can be lost, the specific mechanisms of how a forest fails when its network fails — that's in my work. It's been in my work for six months. You've seen it."

"I've seen it," she said. The tone of someone confirming a finding they had been forming for some time. "The Burrard corridor has a mycorrhizal connectivity element that was not in the original brief. The clients love it. The ecologists they consulted love it. I couldn't account for where it came from."

"From somewhere else," I said.

She looked at me. "All right," she said. "I need you present for client-facing work. For the technical work, I need you reliable about timelines. For the design work — " She paused. "For the design work, I think whatever you've been doing has made you better at the design work. So go do it, and come back when the design work needs you."

This was Hana Park's version of the single confirming pat. It was the most that she gave, and it was enough.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me," she said. "Deliver the drainage specification by Monday." She turned and walked back toward the north entrance. "And fix the northeast corner. The contractors are right that it looks wrong even if it isn't."

I looked at the northeast corner.

The drainage specification was correct. The visual line was misleading in the way that correct drainage specifications often are — the surface expression of an underground logic that only made sense if you could read the subterranean conditions that determined the surface form. I could explain it. I had been explaining it in emails for two months.

But I also understood, looking at it now with the full attention of someone who was actually present, that there was a way to adjust the surface treatment to make the underlying logic legible without compromising the drainage. You did not have to choose between the correct solution and the visible solution. You could often find the correct solution's visible form, if you looked at it long enough.

I spent two hours at the northeast corner, adjusting the specification.

When I left the site, I walked home along the water. The specific quality of Vancouver in late spring, the water and the mountains and the gray-green city and the Douglas firs in the parks doing their patient work. My world's version of the Cascade: intact, threatened at its edges, requiring the kind of sustained attention that prevented the emergency rather than responding to it. The prevention work.

I thought about Kenji, walking to the cedar twice a week. I thought about the monitoring without knowing why. I thought about the trust required to maintain a thing you do not fully understand, because you understand enough to know that the maintaining matters.

I walked home. I wrote the drainage specification. I sent it to the contractors and copied Hana Park.

Then I opened the substrate model and finished the update I owed Jun's coalition.

Then I sat at my kitchen table and looked at the manila folder with the accumulated archive of the past six months. The neighbor's cat was on the fence in the alley — still there, same fence, same position, the anchor of ordinary continuity that had been maintaining its post through the entire six months. I had not yet taken the photograph I had promised myself.

I took the photograph.
