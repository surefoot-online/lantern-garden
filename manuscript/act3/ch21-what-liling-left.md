### Chapter Twenty-One · What Liling Left

The call from the care facility came on a Wednesday.

Not the final call. The nurse's voice was calm — the specific calm of someone delivering information that is difficult but that is her professional task to deliver clearly: Liling had had a difficult night, some respiratory distress, the doctor had adjusted her medications, she was comfortable now but the family should know.

"Should I come?" I asked.

"It might be a good time," the nurse said. Which meant: yes.

I called my mother. She was already at the facility — she had arrived, she said, an hour after the night staff had called her, and she was sitting in the common room because Liling was sleeping and the room was too small for two people to sit beside a sleeping person without the second person's presence becoming an intrusion.

"I'll come today," I said.

"Yes," my mother said. "Come today."

I flew Taipei on the afternoon flight.

The facility in the evening had the smell. My body knows it now — the specific layered quality of it — and responds to it the way a body responds to a smell that carries meaning: a slight heightening of attention, a preparing of something. Not dread. Something more neutral than dread and more engaged than neutrality.

Liling was awake.

She was in the sea-glass chair — not looking at the wall this time but at her hands. Her hands in her lap. Her hands were still the hands of a botanist: long fingers, careful evidence of a life spent in close attention to small things. The joints swollen now, the skin thin in the way that age makes skin thin, but the hands themselves still recognizably the hands that had held a field notebook for fifty years and a daughter for sixty and a granddaughter for thirty-four.

I sat in the family chair. My mother was in the doorway behind me.

Liling looked up. She looked at me, and then past me at my mother.

"Ah-Mei," she said. My name, clearly. Then: "Huimei."

My mother came and sat in the second chair — the one usually against the wall, that the staff moved in for occasions with multiple visitors. She sat and she took her mother's hand. Not tentatively. Fully.

Liling looked at her hand in my mother's hand. The expression on her face: not the confused look, not the lucid-window clarity, something in between and past both of them. The expression of someone who has arrived somewhere they were going.

She spoke then. Not continuously — this was not a lucid window in the sense of the long window that had given me ninety minutes. This was brief, specific, the clarity that surfaces when the person has something specific they need to say and rises to say it.

"I need to tell you," she said to my mother. "About the matrix."

My mother glanced at me. I shook my head slightly — *let her.*

"The matrix is in the ground," Liling said. "On the ridge. The cedar." She was looking at my mother's hand in hers. "It has what it needs. I gave it what it needs. It's been waiting." A pause. "It's been found. Ah-Mei found it." She looked at me briefly — confirming — then back at my mother's hand. "It will grow now. The network will grow."

"Yes, Nǎi Nai," I said.

"Good." She settled. Then, in a different register — not the field researcher but the woman: "Huimei. You smell like your father."

My mother was very still.

Liling was looking at their joined hands, and her face had the quality of someone inside a memory, inside the room of the memory, not remembering it from outside but occupying it. "He smelled like cedar," she said. "When he came from the forest. He always smelled like the forest." A pause. "He was patient. He was patient with everything. The way Miyake was patient — not with people, Miyake was very impatient with people — but with the work. With the process of understanding something difficult." She looked at my mother's hand. "He had a quality like that. Like he could wait as long as necessary for the forest to speak."

My mother's hand tightened on Liling's.

"I loved him," Liling said. Simply, as she always states findings. Without apology, without embellishment, without the weight of fifty years of silence around the statement, as though the silence had not been, as though this was simply the fact, finally spoken aloud, and the air received it without incident. "I loved him very much."

My mother said nothing. She was crying — the quiet way she cries, the way that is more feeling than performance.

"I loved you more," Liling said. Still simple. "You were already becoming. I couldn't leave."

"I know," my mother said. Her voice was steady, the way voices are steady when the person has passed through the shaking part and come out the other side. "I know that now."

"Good," Liling said. The confirming gesture — she could not quite manage the single pat with her hand in my mother's, so she pressed instead, a slight pressure that was its own confirmation. "Good."

She drifted then. Softly. Into the interior mode — the attending-to-something-only-she-could-see. Her hand stayed in my mother's hand and she did not seem to need or not need anything; she was simply somewhere, in the particular way of wherever she went when she went.

My mother and I sat on either side of her in the room's evening light.

After a while I excused myself and went and sat in the common room with the snake plant and the corridor sounds and I called Jun.

He picked up on the second ring. I could hear the monitoring station's background.

"She's declining," I said. "The facility called this morning. I'm here now."

"I'm sorry," he said. And then: "Is she — is she present?"

"In and out. She said some things tonight. To my mother." I paused. "Important things."

"Good," he said. It was the same word Liling used — the same specific meaning, the confirmation of satisfactory outcome. I registered this without comment.

"I need to tell you something," I said. "About what she said in the long window — the second part, the part I didn't include in the substrate briefing." I had told him the science, the coordinates, the burial protocol. I had not told him the rest. "She mentioned Miyake. Her supervisor at Nagoya. She said Kenji had a quality like Miyake — patient with the work, not with the people." I paused. "She was describing the two most important scientific relationships in her life in the same breath. As the same category of thing."

Jun was quiet on the line for a long time.

"She's been crossing in her dementia," I said. "She told me. She said the Garden doesn't require full consciousness — that it seeks her. She's been spending time in the between-place. She said: it's quiet there. I think I'll stay there for a while, when I go."

Jun said nothing. I could hear him breathing.

"I don't know what to do with that," I said. "I don't know if it's literally true — if she's actually been in the liminal space while what we see is a woman in a chair in a care facility. Or if it's a way of understanding her own diminishment that gives it a frame she can inhabit." I paused. "I've decided it doesn't matter which. What matters is that she's not frightened. What matters is that wherever she goes when she goes far away, it's somewhere she's been before."

"Mei," Jun said. His voice had the quality it had when he was working through something that the ecological framework didn't quite capture. "If what she describes is real — if the Garden exists in a state that is accessible to her even now — then the crossing isn't contingent on full cognitive function. It's in the body. In the structure."

"Yes."

"Then she hasn't lost it," he said. "Whatever the dementia has taken. She hasn't lost the capacity."

"No," I said. "I don't think she has."

He was quiet again. "That's not nothing," he said. And then, more quietly: "Is she going to be all right?"

I looked at the corridor. The snake plant in its pot. The sound of someone moving at the far end of the hall. "No," I said. "She's going to die. Not tonight, I think. But soon."

"Then I'm sorry," he said again. "I'm genuinely sorry."

"I know." I held the phone and looked at the common room's evening light. "She asked me to tell you it matters that you keep going. That's not — she said that in the long window. I'm telling you again because I think she'd want it said again."

Jun was quiet for a long time. "Tell her—" He stopped. "You don't need to tell her anything from me. She knows."

"She probably does," I said.

"Mei." His voice shifted. "The culture rate is still holding. Priya thinks mid-autumn deployment is possible if we move on the substrate preparation now. I want to brief the team tomorrow morning and get the ridge survey scheduled."

"Good," I said.

"She deserves to know that the work is moving. If you can tell her—"

"She already knows," I said. "She said so tonight. She said the network will grow." I paused. "She's not asking for updates. She's past needing them. She planted the thing fifty-two years ago and she knows it's doing what she designed it to do. That's enough for her."

He was quiet. "That's a particular kind of trust," he said.

"It's a particular kind of science," I said. "Trusting the data you set in motion to do what data does."

I went back to the room after a while. My mother was still sitting with Liling, still holding her hand. Liling was in the interior mode. The room was quiet.

We sat that way for an hour, my mother and I, on either side of the woman in the sea-glass chair. After a while the night nurse came to do the evening round and gently, professionally, indicated the visiting hours. We rose.

In the corridor, my mother and I walked toward the exit.

She did not say anything for a long time. We were at the outer door when she said: "She loved him."

"Yes."

"She actually loved him." She said it the way she had said it on the phone — placing the statement in its correct position, registering the arrival of something that had needed to arrive. "I always thought the grief was the proof that something had gone wrong. Something had been lost. But it was the proof of the thing itself." She looked at the door. "If you love something, you grieve it. You can't grieve what you didn't have."

"No," I said.

"The grief was the fullness," she said again. I let her say it again; she needed to say it again. "Not the absence of it."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what it was."

She pushed the door open and we walked into the New Taipei evening: the warmth of it, the specific ambient sound of a city that runs late, the smell of the night market somewhere two blocks over, the particular quality of the light that comes from a city that doesn't fully go dark.

"Tell me about Jun," she said.

And I did — the version I could tell her, which was real and true and left out the parts that required a framework she didn't have. I told her about the forest, and the coalition, and the spatial modeling, and what it felt like to have a skill find its correct use. I told her about the ridge corridor and Priya's culture timeline and the northern bypass that might hold if everything held.

She listened, and she said, when I paused: "He's fighting for something that might not happen in his lifetime."

"Yes."

"Like she was," my mother said. "Like what she planted."

"Yes," I said. "Like exactly that."

We walked to the station together. The night market was three streets over, its noise finding us intermittently — the specific bass note of a city in its evening mode, the register of a city that stays awake late and is comfortable with the fact of its own continuousness.

I had not been in Taipei at night since the visit for the long window, the ninety minutes. Since before the cedar. The city had continued in my absence, as cities do — the same streets, the same smell of the stalls and the oil and the specific mineral note that came up from the wet pavement after dark. The same quality of light from the shopfronts and the apartment windows and the neon that New Taipei used so much more than Taipei proper. The city entirely itself.

My mother walked with her usual deliberateness — not a hurried person, not a slow person, a person who moves at the pace of the thing she is attending to. She was attending to the walk. She was processing, in the way she processed, without speaking.

I did not fill the silence. She would say what she was going to say when she had organized it in the order she wanted to say it. This was a quality she shared with her mother, which I noted without saying, because it was the kind of thing that would feel like an observation to me and might feel like a comparison she hadn't asked for.

At the station she stopped and looked at me with the expression she uses when she has decided to say the thing she was going to say rather than the easier thing.

"When she goes," my mother said, "you will tell me what she meant. About the matrix. About the network growing." She paused. "You don't have to tell me everything. Tell me what I can hold."

"I will," I said.

"Good." She pressed her hand against my arm once, briefly, in the confirming gesture. The same gesture. She didn't know it was the same gesture; she had never seen Liling do it with me. And yet there it was, running forward through the line, the way knowledge runs when it has no other vehicle — through the body, through the hands, through the specific timing of a touch that means: received, confirmed, enough.

I watched her go through the turnstile. I stood on the street for a moment in the New Taipei evening, in the warm ordinary night, with the weight of everything I was carrying and the specific quality of a weight that you have learned to carry correctly — distributed, balanced, held close but not gripped.

I thought about my grandmother, fifty-two years ago, returning from the Canadian fieldwork and standing outside the NTU department's staff entrance with the seed samples and the preserved matrix and the pregnancy she had not yet told anyone about. Standing in the Taipei evening — a different Taipei, less neon, less MRT, the same fundamental quality of a city that had lived long enough to become comfortable with itself — carrying everything and being, in that moment, between two versions of her life.

She had walked in. She had filed the summary report and deposited the specimens and worked with Professor Huang on the preservation protocol and then formally returned to the Institute and begun the next phase of the work.

The network is talking. In all its forms.

I went to find a taxi.
