### Chapter Twenty-Two · The Collapse Accelerates

Three days after the ridge, the worst models began to look like the optimistic ones.

Sera brought the updated beetle-front data to the morning briefing with the specific expression she uses when she has information that she has been hoping will be refuted by the overnight readings and has not been. The expression is not despair — Sera does not do despair, or rather, she has no use for it in the field and has made a practice of keeping it out of the briefing room. It is something more controlled than despair: the expression of someone who has been in this fight long enough to know that the next piece of bad news is always available, and who has developed a methodology for receiving it without losing forward motion.

"The eastern front has merged with the northern vector across the Pemberton corridor," she said. She had the updated maps on the display: the beetle infestation zones overlaid on the die-off data, overlaid on the topographic model, overlaid on the fungal network substrate analysis I had provided. The composite was, in every color that meant loss, extensive. "As of the weekend's survey, the two fronts have established a continuous line across a corridor we had been planning to use for the eastern network propagation. The corridor is effectively closed."

The room was quiet in the particular way that coalition meetings are quiet when the news is bad: not shocked, because nothing is a shock here anymore, but absorbing. The specific silence of people updating their models.

"How long for the eastern propagation to re-route?" Jun asked.

"Six months minimum, and that assumes we can identify a viable alternate corridor that doesn't cross the beetle line." Sera looked at the map. "There are two candidate corridors. Both have substrate issues. Neither is what we wanted."

"What are the nodes we lose in the eastern merger?"

She listed them. Seven nodes — four of them from the test site's eastern edge that she had already predicted losing, the other three from a secondary network we had been building in the previous two weeks. The secondary network was newer, less established, and more vulnerable to the microclimate disruption that preceded the beetle-front's advance.

Henriksen, in the front row: "What about the southern cluster? The seven nodes?"

"Holding." Sera's voice shifted slightly on this — the precise shift of someone delivering news that is genuinely good and wants to be careful not to color it with the context of the bad news around it. "The seven central nodes are above establishment threshold. The possible eighth confirmed last week. The southern cluster is intact."

Jun moved on. He presented the two alternate corridors for the eastern re-route. He presented the revised monitoring schedule. He presented the legal update on the clearcut injunction, which had produced a fine that the coalition's lawyers considered inadequate and were appealing. He was, throughout all of this, the person he has been since the first meeting I attended: presenting the worst conditions without performing the tragedy of them, because the tragedy performs itself.

Two of the coalition's senior members stood up at the end of the briefing.

Their names were Henriksen and Yiu. I have mentioned them before — Henriksen the long-committed, Yiu the careful newcomer whose caution I had characterized, in an earlier session, as not wrong. They stood together, which was not a thing I had seen before, and Henriksen spoke for both of them.

"I want to say something that I want the record to reflect," he said. His voice had the quality of a man who has prepared something and is going to deliver it whether or not it is welcome. "I've been in this coalition for eleven years. I've seen four directors, three funding crises, two legal battles that we lost and one that we won. I've seen the beetle-front advance across territory that we had models saying we could hold." He looked at the map. "I came into this thinking we were going to save the forest. I think what we're doing is witnessing the forest's worst period and refusing to look away from it. Those are different things and I needed to understand the difference before I could do the second one correctly."

He sat down.

Yiu, beside him, said nothing for a moment. Then: "What he said."

Into the silence that followed, one of the newer coalition members spoke. Her name was Priya — a mycologist from the university ecology department, the most technically focused member of the team in the two days since Jun had briefed the coalition on the matrix.

"And the ridge sample?" she said. "What's the status?"

"Still being cultured," Sera said. "Four weeks minimum before we know viability at scale. Priya, you have the culture parameters?"

Priya had them. She listed them precisely, in the language of a mycologist who was also a careful scientist: the ectomycorrhizal species identified from the matrix, their compatibility with the southern cluster's existing network, the projected timeline for culture expansion, the conditions required for field deployment. It was the language Liling would have used, I thought. The same precision. The same trust in data to speak if presented correctly.

"If the culture is viable at scale," Priya said, "and if we can deploy by early winter — that's a tight window, but it's possible — we may be able to extend the southern cluster along the ridge corridor that runs northeast. Which is exactly the corridor the eastern merger has closed off."

Jun looked at the map. "The ridge corridor," he said.

"The one with the cedar," Priya confirmed. She said it neutrally — she was too careful a scientist to editorialize, but I could hear in the neutrality the specific quality of a person who has understood the significance without overstating it. "If the matrix deploys there successfully, the ridge corridor becomes a propagation vector the beetle-front can't close. The substrate at that elevation is too cold for the beetle's primary cycle. We would have a northern bypass."

The room absorbed this.

"Four weeks," Jun said. "Until we know."

"Four weeks," Sera confirmed.

Jun looked at both of them — at Henriksen and Yiu, at Priya, at the room — with the expression of someone who has been carrying a weight alone for long enough that having someone else acknowledge the weight is its own form of relief. He nodded.

"We are not here because we believe we will win," he said then, to the room. Not performed — stated, in the flat tone of someone reporting a condition that is simply true. "We are here because we know what it would mean to stop."

I watched him say this and I understood, in the specific way that I understand things that arrive from inside the space of something rather than from outside it, why I loved him.

He was refusing the comfort of meaning. He was not telling the coalition that their work would succeed. He was not telling them that the forest would survive, that the beetle-front would be contained, that the matrix would save the southern corridor and the ridge would hold and the network would knit itself back together. He was telling them only that stopping had its own cost and that they had all independently decided, for their own reasons, that the cost was too high.

This is the only kind of leadership I can respect entirely: the kind that does not promise more than it can deliver and does not pretend the situation is better than it is.

After the briefing, the room cleared in the complicated choreography of people who need coffee and fresh air and a moment not to be in the crisis. I stayed. Jun stayed. We stood in the briefing room with the maps on the display and the coffee going cold in its urn.

"The ridge," he said.

"The ridge," I said.

He looked at the display, at the merged beetle-front, at the southern cluster's orange confirmation dots, at the ridge corridor running northeast in the direction that was, if everything held, a possible path.

"I've been doing the math on the eastern re-route," he said. "Even if the alternate corridors work — even if both of them establish — we lose eighteen months. Minimum. The eastern merger costs us eighteen months of propagation time that we don't have."

"I know."

"The ridge is the thing," he said. "If Priya's culture works. If we can deploy by early winter. If the beetle-front doesn't advance up-elevation before we can establish."

"Three conditionals," I said.

"Three conditionals is better than five."

This is also something I love about him: the precise calibration of hope. Not optimism — optimism is cheap, available without evidence. This was something more careful. The acknowledgment that three conditionals is better than five is better than seven, and that this narrowing, slow as it is, represents something real.

"I need to go back to my world for a few days," I said. "I have — I have things I need to do there. Professional things I can't keep deferring." This was true. Hana Park's patience had been extraordinary and was not infinite, and I needed to go to the office and be present in it in a way that did not require explanation. "But I'll come back for the culture update. In four weeks."

He looked at me. "Will you?"

"Yes."

He nodded. The single nod that means: received, not questioned. The nod I have come to understand as a form of trust, which is — for Jun, whose trust is not easily given — the most important acknowledgment available.

"Tell me something before you go," he said.

"What?"

"The matrix. What Liling prepared. The species she chose." He paused. "She chose them in 1974. For conditions she projected the forest would face fifty years later. Based on whatever data she had access to in 1974." He looked at the map. "How did she know what to choose?"

I had thought about this on the drive back from the ridge, with the sample containers in the truck's bed and the hayloft window of the monitoring station in my mind as a destination. I thought about it again now.

"She understood the network's intelligence better than anyone in her field," I said. "She understood that a resilient network needs diversity — not monoculture, not the single best-performing species, but a community of species that support each other in the way that a real ecosystem does. She chose for community, not for individual performance."

"She chose for relationship."

"Yes."

"That's what your analysis does," he said. "When you model the propagation vectors. You don't optimize for individual nodes. You model the relationships between them."

I had not thought of it this way. I thought of it now. "Yes," I said. "I suppose I do."

"It's the same methodology," he said. "Fifty years apart. Same methodology."

I stood with this for a moment.

"She would have recognized your work," Jun said. "She would have understood exactly what you were doing and why it was the right approach."

I thought about Liling in the sea-glass chair. *You always know what a space is doing.* The specific way she had said it — as a confirmation, not a compliment. As the statement of a fact that she had known for a long time.

"Yes," I said. "She would have."

I left the next morning. I crossed back from the Lantern Garden in the early light, the lantern-plants pale in the dawn, and I arrived in Vancouver's wet gray morning and walked to my apartment and sat down and thought about what it meant that my grandmother's methodology and my methodology were, in their essential architecture, the same.
