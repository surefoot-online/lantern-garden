### Chapter Twenty · The Collapse Accelerates

Three days after the northern corridor visit, the worst models began to look like the optimistic ones.

Sera brought the updated beetle-front data to the morning briefing with the specific expression she uses when she has information that she has been hoping will be refuted by the overnight readings and has not been. The expression is not despair — Sera does not do despair, or rather, she has no use for it in the field and has made a practice of keeping it out of the briefing room. It is something more controlled than despair: the expression of someone who has been in this fight long enough to know that the next piece of bad news is always available, and who has developed a methodology for receiving it without losing forward motion.

"The eastern front has merged with the northern vector across the Pemberton corridor," she said. She had the updated maps on the display: the beetle infestation zones overlaid on the die-off data, overlaid on the topographic model, overlaid on the fungal network substrate analysis I had provided. The composite was, in every color that meant loss, extensive. "As of the weekend's survey, the two fronts have established a continuous line across a corridor we had been planning to use for the eastern network propagation. That corridor is effectively closed."

The room was quiet in the particular way that coalition meetings are quiet when the news is bad: not shocked, because nothing is a shock here anymore, but absorbing. The specific silence of people updating their models.

"How long for the eastern propagation to re-route?" Jun asked.

"Six months minimum, and that assumes we can identify a viable alternate corridor that doesn't cross the beetle line." Sera looked at the map. "There are two candidate corridors. Both have substrate issues. Neither is what we wanted."

"What are the nodes we lose in the eastern merger?"

She listed them. Seven nodes — four of them from the test site's eastern edge that she had already predicted losing, the other three from a secondary network we had been building in the previous two weeks. The secondary network was newer, less established, and more vulnerable to the microclimate disruption that preceded the beetle-front's advance.

Henriksen, in the front row: "What about the southern cluster? The established nodes?"

"Holding." Sera's voice shifted slightly on this — the precise shift of someone delivering news that is genuinely good and wants to be careful not to color it with the context of the bad news around it. "The central established nodes are above threshold. The southern cluster is intact."

"And the new nodes in the northern corridor?" Yiu said. He was looking at me, not at Sera — he had been at the site two days before, he knew I had been there.

"Sixteen now," I said. "The two new ones from the overnight monitoring are reading connectivity on the northern substrate. The network is reaching north."

The room absorbed this differently than it had absorbed the eastern merger. Not with relief — the coalition had learned to be careful with relief — but with the specific recalibration that comes from holding two opposing facts simultaneously. The eastern corridor was closed. The northern network was reaching. Both things were true.

Jun moved on. He presented the two alternate corridors for the eastern re-route. He presented the revised monitoring schedule. He presented the legal update on the clearcut injunction, which had produced a fine that the coalition's lawyers considered inadequate and were appealing. He was, throughout all of this, the person he has been since the first meeting I attended: presenting the worst conditions without performing the tragedy of them, because the tragedy performs itself.

Two of the coalition's senior members stood up at the end of the briefing.

Their names were Henriksen and Yiu. I have mentioned them before — Henriksen the long-committed, Yiu the careful newcomer whose caution I had characterized, in an earlier session, as not wrong. They stood together, which was not a thing I had seen before, and Henriksen spoke for both of them.

"I want to say something that I want the record to reflect," he said. His voice had the quality of a man who has prepared something and is going to deliver it whether or not it is welcome. "I've been in this coalition for eleven years. I've seen four directors, three funding crises, two legal battles that we lost and one that we won. I've seen the beetle-front advance across territory that we had models saying we could hold." He looked at the map. "I came into this thinking we were going to save the forest. I think what we're doing is witnessing the forest's worst period and refusing to look away from it. Those are different things and I needed to understand the difference before I could do the second one correctly."

He sat down.

Yiu, beside him, said nothing for a moment. Then: "What he said."

Into the silence that followed, Priya spoke. She was the mycologist from the university ecology department, the most technically focused member of the team since Jun had briefed the coalition on the matrix. She had Liling's burial protocol committed to memory within twenty-four hours of my translating the field notes.

"The ridge culture status," she said. Not a question — a statement that invited a response. She was looking at Jun.

"Viable at six weeks," Jun said. "Early indicators stronger than projected. The ectomycorrhizal strains from the matrix are establishing in the culture medium faster than the native strains we've been working with."

"Why faster?" I asked.

Priya turned to me. "My hypothesis is that the matrix was preserved under conditions that maintained the fungi in a state of metabolic readiness. The mineral substrate suppressed activity without causing cellular degradation. When we restored the culture conditions, the network essentially resumed from where it left off." She paused. "Fifty-two years of suspended readiness. The biology was waiting."

I thought about Liling's hands, holding the sealed vessel in both hands for a moment before placing it in the ground. *It has what it needs. I gave it what it needs.*

"If the culture maintains this rate," Priya continued, "deployment by early winter is realistic. Possibly mid-autumn if we can prepare the ridge substrate during the summer window."

"The ridge corridor," Jun said. "If we deploy there — what's the propagation vector?"

She had the site model up on the display before he finished the sentence. "Northeast," she said. "The ridge runs at the elevation where the beetle cycle doesn't establish. If the matrix deploys successfully at the cedar and propagates along the ridge, we have a corridor that the eastern merger can't close. The substrate at that elevation — the cold, the mineral composition — it's unfavorable to the beetle but favorable to the ectomycorrhizal community the matrix contains." She paused. "We would have a northern bypass."

The room absorbed this.

The northern bypass. The corridor the eastern merger had closed, re-routed through the ridge. Not the same path Liling had imagined — she could not have known, in 1974, exactly what shape the collapse would take, which corridors would fail and which would hold. But the substrate she had chosen, the ridge she had selected, the species she had paired with that specific mineral environment — all of it was exactly positioned for the route the collapse had forced.

"She didn't know," I said. I was thinking aloud and I stopped when I realized I was doing it. Everyone was looking at me.

"She chose the cedar for the mycorrhizal compatibility," I said. "For the substrate, the elevation, the cold-tolerance of the species she selected. She chose it because it was the right site for the preservation. But it's also the right site for the bypass because the conditions that make it right for the preservation make it right for the corridor."

"Same conditions," Priya said. "Different application."

"Yes."

"She chose correctly," Priya said. The tone of someone who has been doing the quality assessment on another scientist's work and has found it sound. "She made the right call in 1974 with fifty-two years of lead time. That's extraordinary selection."

Jun was looking at the map with the working face. "She was twenty years ahead of the field on the network science," he said. "But she understood this landscape specifically — the elevation range, the beetle cycle's upper limit, the substrate conditions at the ridge. She read this forest."

"She had three years of field work," I said. "And Kenji, who had four more months than she did. Between them, they understood this forest better than anyone in this room."

Henriksen said, from the front row: "Except you."

I looked at him. He was looking at me with the expression of someone who has made a precise statement and is watching to see if it lands correctly.

"Possibly," I said. "I don't know yet. I've been working here for six months. Kenji worked here for thirty-four years." I paused. "I'm carrying what they learned. That's different from being the one who learned it."

After the briefing, the room cleared. I stayed. Jun stayed. Priya stayed, which was less usual — she typically left immediately after briefings to return to the culture lab, where she kept a schedule that the rest of the coalition had stopped trying to map because it did not correspond to the standard working hours that the coalition tried, with variable success, to maintain.

Priya sat down at the table and opened her laptop without asking whether she was wanted. This was characteristic. She had determined, at some point in the weeks since Jun had briefed the team on the matrix, that she was a core member of whatever configuration was now making decisions, and she was simply present in whatever space the decisions were being made. The coalition had learned to accept this. It was the right call.

Jun and I stood in the briefing room with the maps on the display and the coffee going cold in its urn.

"The ridge," he said.

"The ridge," I said.

He looked at the display, at the merged beetle-front, at the southern cluster's confirmation indicators, at the ridge corridor running northeast in the direction that was, if everything held, a possible path through the disaster. The map had the specific quality of maps made under bad conditions: very precise, very complete, very careful about what the data showed and did not show. Jun had trained the coalition to treat the map as a record of what was known rather than a prediction of what would happen. The map showed the situation. It did not promise anything about the situation's resolution.

"I've been doing the math on the eastern re-route," he said. "Even if the alternate corridors work — even if both of them establish — we lose eighteen months. Minimum. The eastern merger costs us eighteen months of propagation time that we don't have."

"I know."

"The ridge is the thing," he said. "If Priya's culture maintains the rate. If we can deploy by mid-autumn. If the beetle-front doesn't advance up-elevation before we can establish."

"Three conditionals," I said.

"Three conditionals is better than five."

This is also something I love about him: the precise calibration of possibility. Not optimism — optimism is cheap, available without evidence. This was something more careful. The acknowledgment that three conditionals is better than five is better than seven, and that this narrowing, slow as it is, represents something real.

Priya, from the table: "The culture rate is going to hold. I want to be clear about the basis for that — it's not confidence, it's the establishment curve. The ectomycorrhizal community from the matrix has already moved through the establishment phase and is in active network formation. The curve doesn't reverse at this stage. What can go wrong is the deployment — substrate preparation at the ridge, timing of the inoculation, beetle-front movement during the establishment window. Those are the conditionals. Not the culture itself."

Jun looked at her. "You're saying two conditionals."

"I'm saying the culture conditional is effectively resolved," she said. "I wouldn't stake it on a projection — I'm staking it on an established growth curve that shows a community behaving as expected. The culture is viable. The question is what we do with it."

She went back to her laptop. Jun looked at me.

"Two conditionals," he said.

"Two conditionals is better than three," I said.

He almost smiled. This was the rare expression I had been waiting to see — the movement in his face that was not the working face and not the grief of the situation but something else, something that surfaced very occasionally, when the data moved slightly in the direction of possible.

"I need to go back to my world for a few days," I said. "Professional things I can't keep deferring." This was true. Hana Park's patience had been extraordinary and was not infinite. The Burrard corridor project was at a stage that required my presence; I had been managing it from a distance with a level of technical accuracy that had made the distance invisible, but the client was going to need a site visit, and I could not explain that my site visits had been complicated by the fact that I had been spending time in a parallel world. "But I'll come back for the culture update."

He looked at me. "Will you?"

"Yes."

He nodded. The single nod that means: received, not questioned. The nod I have come to understand as a form of trust, which is — for Jun, whose trust is not easily given and is not performative when it is — the most important acknowledgment available.

"Tell me something before you go," he said.

"What?"

"The matrix. What Liling prepared. The species she chose." He paused. "She chose them in 1974. For conditions she projected the forest would face fifty years later. Based on whatever data she had access to in 1974." He looked at the map. "How did she know what to choose?"

I had thought about this on the drive back from the northern corridor. "She understood the network's intelligence better than anyone in her field," I said. "She understood that a resilient network needs diversity — not monoculture, not the single best-performing species, but a community of species that support each other the way a real ecosystem does. She chose for community, not for individual performance."

"She chose for relationship."

"Yes."

"That's what your analysis does," he said. "When you model the propagation vectors. You don't optimize for individual nodes. You model the relationships between them."

I had not thought of it this way. I thought of it now. "Yes," I said. "I suppose I do."

"It's the same methodology," he said. "Fifty years apart. Same methodology."

I stood with this for a moment.

"She would have recognized your work," Jun said. "She would have understood exactly what you were doing and why it was the right approach."

I thought about Liling in the sea-glass chair. *You always know what a space is doing.* The specific way she had said it — as a confirmation, not a compliment. As the statement of a fact that she had known for a long time.

"Yes," I said. "She would have."

Priya, from the table: "The field notes mention a Japanese ecologist named Tanaka Kenji." She was reading something on her screen. "He's in your translation."

"Yes."

"He stayed in this world. He died in 2009, according to what you've told Jun." She paused. "Did he continue to monitor the culture conditions at the cedar after Liling left?"

"He monitored the site," I said. "He walked to the cedar twice a week for thirty years. He didn't know about the culture conditions in the technical sense — Liling hadn't given him the full burial protocol when she left, because she had prepared it precisely for someone who would understand the methodology. But he knew the cedar was significant and he kept monitoring it."

"He was maintaining the site without knowing why," Priya said.

"He knew why in the broad sense. He knew she had left something there that mattered. He trusted that if the crossing brought someone with the right skills, they would find it and know what to do with it."

Priya looked at me steadily. "He trusted you," she said. "Thirty years before you arrived. He was maintaining the site for you."

I had not framed it this way. I sat with it.

"For whoever came," I said. "Not for me specifically."

"It was you," she said. Not insisting — simply stating the fact as it had resolved. "It was you who came. So it was you he was maintaining it for."

She closed her laptop and stood. "The culture update will be ready in three weeks," she said. "Come back then." She left the briefing room.

Jun and I stood with the map.

"She's right," he said.

"I know," I said.

I left the next morning. I crossed back from the Lantern Garden in the early light, the lantern-plants pale in the dawn, and I arrived in Vancouver's wet gray morning and walked to my apartment and sat down and thought about what it meant that my grandmother's methodology and my methodology were, in their essential architecture, the same.

I thought about Miyake Fumiko at Nagoya University, teaching a young Taiwanese botanist to read a site the way a carpenter reads the grain. I thought about whatever unnamed professor at UBC had taught my design professor, who had taught me. I thought about the chain of perception passing forward through the generations, through different countries and different languages and different methodologies for describing the same underlying reality: that a living system can be read in its surface, if you have learned to look.

I thought about Kenji, walking to the cedar twice a week for thirty years, maintaining the site for someone who had not yet arrived. Trusting the pattern. Doing the work.

The network is talking. In both worlds. Through all the generations of people who were taught to listen.
