### Chapter Twenty-One · Huimei

The call happened the evening after the ridge. After Jun and I had driven back in the truck in the specific silence of people who have been present at something significant together and are not yet ready to reduce it to words. After I had eaten the bowl of rice and pickled vegetables at the coalition kitchen with the mechanical hunger of a body that has spent its energy on something other than eating. After I had sat in the monitoring station for an hour reviewing the documentation — the photographs we had taken, the field notes in Liling's handwriting that I had transcribed on site, the sample containers sealed with the coalition's identification codes, all of it properly filed before I allowed myself to stop.

I called my mother from the small room that Jun had offered me, the one with the hayloft window, the one I had come to think of as the room I used in this world.

She picked up on the third ring. I could hear, in the background, the sounds of her evening — a television program she'd muted, the particular acoustic of her Taipei apartment, its high ceilings and the road outside that ran toward the night market.

"Ah-Mei," she said. "You sound strange."

"I'm all right," I said. "I have something to tell you."

"Tell me."

I had thought about what to tell her during the drive back. What to include, what to hold back. The full truth was not available to her — the full truth included two worlds and a liminal garden and a crossing that I could not hand her without a container to put it in, and she did not have the container. But the partial truth that was available was real, and important, and she had been living without it for sixty-two years.

"Grandmother had a life," I said. "In those three years. A real life. She was working in a forest — a forest research zone in British Columbia — with two colleagues. One of them was a Korean botanist named Park Jinhee. One of them was a Japanese ecologist named Tanaka Kenji."

Silence.

"She worked in the forest for three years," I said. "She did significant scientific work. She planted something — a scientific preservation — that may, right now, help the forest she was working in. The work mattered. The three years mattered."

"And Kenji," my mother said.

"Yes," I said. "She loved him. It was real. She was there for three years and she loved him and the work and the place." I paused. "She came back because she discovered she was pregnant. She came back because she couldn't raise you in a place where you would have no framework for who you were. She came back because you were already becoming, and she couldn't leave you to become without her."

My mother was very quiet.

"She didn't come back because she stopped loving the other life," I said. "She came back because she loved you more than she loved anything else. Not instead of the other things. More." I paused again. "She made one choice that contained all the other choices. She chose you. Everything else followed from that."

I heard my mother breathe. The specific quality of the breath of someone who is not going to cry immediately but who is standing at the edge of something.

"I always felt," my mother said, after a long time, "like I was the reason she couldn't be happy."

I had known she would say this. I had known it the way you know the shape of a room before you walk into it — had known it from the quiet she brought to conversations about Liling, from the way she phrased things about her mother's far-away presence, from the particular quality of her love for Liling which was full and also careful, the love of someone who had learned to hold something without gripping it.

"You were the reason she could be," I said.

I heard my mother stop breathing for a moment.

"She had the other life, and she had you, and she came back to you," I said. "She was different after — she was always a little far away, you've said that, she was always partly elsewhere. That was real. She was carrying things you couldn't see and she never put them down. But she chose you. That was a whole and complete choice. Not partial. Not compromise." I paused. "You were not a consolation prize. You were what she came back for."

My mother cried, then. Not loudly — I have never heard my mother cry loudly. She cried in the way she does everything: with care, without excess, the emotion precise in its expression. The way people cry when they have been holding something for decades and something small finally moves, and the thing that had been held moves with it.

I sat with the hayloft window's view of the Cascade forest in the dark and held the phone and did not say anything for a while.

Then she said: "She loved him."

"Yes."

"She actually loved him." The quality of the statement: something settling, something arriving in its correct place. "I always knew — I always felt — there was a grief in her that she wouldn't name. I thought it was something done to her. A loss she couldn't speak. But it was a love she wouldn't speak. That's different."

"Very different," I said.

"The grief was the fullness," my mother said. "Not the absence of it."

I thought about Liling in the sea-glass chair. The way she said *he stayed, I came back* — not with regret, but with the specificity of a fact she had lived inside for fifty years and had learned to carry without being crushed. "Yes," I said. "That's exactly what it was."

My mother was quiet for a moment. "Where are you?" she said. "You sound — far away."

"I'm in British Columbia," I said. Which was true.

"With Jun?"

"Yes."

She was quiet again. "He's important to you."

"Yes," I said. "He is."

I heard her consider this and not ask the question that was adjacent to it — the question of what Jun and I were to each other, the question that would require an answer I couldn't give without a framework she didn't have. My mother has always known when not to ask the question that is adjacent to the one that can be answered. This is the version of wisdom that does not require understanding everything.

"Ah-Mei," she said. "When can I come with you to see her?"

"Soon," I said. "Soon, I think."

"I want to — I want to look at her differently. Now." A pause. "I want to look at her and see what I see now."

"We'll go," I said. "When I come back."

After I hung up I sat for a long time with the hayloft window. The Cascade zone in Jun's world, at night, in the season between winter and summer: the tree line visible against the sky, which had the particular dark of a sky that is clear but never fully clears, always carrying something, smoke or moisture or the weight of what the trees beneath it are holding. The forest making its invisible sounds. The network in the ground, at the test site and now, fifty years older than we had known, at the cedar on the ridge.

I thought about my mother saying *the grief was the fullness.* I thought about Liling in her sea-glass chair, watching the light on the wall, attending to something neither I nor my mother could see.

I wrote in my notebook: *Told Huimei a version of the truth. The version that was available. She received it.*

Then I wrote: *The version that was available was enough. It was real and it was true and it was enough.*

Then I went to sleep, in the hayloft room, in Jun's world, with the forest outside, and I slept without gaps.
