### Chapter Eighteen · Liling Speaks

I want to be precise about time.

The Tribunal has asked me, in various forms, to establish timelines: when did I return to Taipei after the Ch.17 events, how long between the phone call on the Zhonghe street and my next crossing, what was the interval between Liling's window of clarity and what came after. I have given the Tribunal dates. The dates are accurate. But dates are not the same as time, and what I am trying to describe is something that happened in time in a way that dates do not capture. The session with Liling that I am about to describe lasted ninety minutes by the clock on the care facility's wall. It has occupied a different kind of duration in me ever since. I cannot give the Tribunal a number for that.

I returned to Taipei the following morning.

I had not planned to. I had planned to go back to Vancouver, cross to Jun's world Thursday as I had said, make the trip to the ridge. But when I woke in the hotel in Zhonghe that I had booked for the one night — the hotel that my mother had suggested when I called her that evening, the hotel where she had stayed herself during Liling's first difficult month — I understood that I was not going to Vancouver. Not yet. What had happened in Liling's room was not finished.

I walked to the care facility in the morning. The walk was twenty minutes from the hotel, through the market street and then along the quieter road that curved up toward the hill where the facility sat, and I was aware during the walk of something that I can only describe as a quality of the air: the particular stillness of a morning that has a specific weight inside it, the way certain kinds of light feel like they are holding something that will be significant. I am a person who reads space. I understood that the space of that morning was telling me something.

She was in the chair again. The sea-glass chair.

The morning light was the same rectangle on the wall, warmer than the previous day because the cloud cover had cleared overnight, and she was in the chair and she was looking at the light and I could tell before I crossed the threshold — I have learned this, in the months of visiting her, to read the quality of her posture from the door — I could tell that she was in a window.

Not just any window. Not the ordinary lucid-interval presence that she achieves sometimes, where her eyes clear and she tracks conversation and she knows who I am and can hold a thread. This was something more. Her posture had the field-station quality — the quality I had seen only once before, briefly, in the April visit when she had grabbed my wrist and said *you smell like the crossing.* The chin angle. The particular stillness of someone who is not drifting but attending, specifically, to this.

She turned when I came in.

"Ah-Mei," she said. My name, exactly, with the specific inflection that belongs to Liling and not to the dementia-version of her that sometimes produces the name as a floating signifier detached from the person it addresses. This was the name addressed to me, precisely me, the version of me she had been waiting for.

I sat in the family chair. I did not speak. I understood that she was going to.

"I've been thinking about what to tell you," she said. "Since yesterday. I have been — I have been here, thinking about what order to put it in."

Her Mandarin was the formal register of her generation and education — the clean diction of the mid-century TFRI scientist, precise vowels, no elision. Not the way she speaks in the confused periods, which is fragmentary and often in the Taiwanese that was the language of her childhood, before the Mandarin of her professional life. This was professional Liling. This was the woman who had written the 1972 paper.

"Start anywhere," I said.

"No." She moved her head slightly — the negation of someone who has organized something and intends to deliver it in the order it was organized. "There is a correct sequence. Let me use it."

I opened my notebook. She watched me open it.

"Good," she said. "Write it down. Your memory will not hold everything the way it holds the spatial things. Mine didn't either. Write it."

I wrote.

---

She began with the capacity. Not the crossing itself — she would come to that — but the thing that makes the crossing possible, which she described in the language of a botanist and which I have tried to render here in a way that is faithful to how she said it.

"The capacity is a structural feature," she said. "Like being left-handed. Like synesthesia. It is not a gift and it is not a curse; it is a trait. It runs in the maternal line of our family. I don't know how far back — I discovered this after the fact, by looking at the records the Garden keeps, which are not records the way our institutions keep records, but which are legible if you learn to read them." She paused. "My mother had the capacity. I know this now. She never crossed — or if she crossed, she didn't understand what she was doing, and the experiences registered as something else. Dreams. Visions. The kind of thing that women of her generation and her circumstances were expected to keep quiet about."

She looked at me directly. "You are not an anomaly. You are the most recent iteration of something that has been in this family for at least three generations. Possibly more. I am the only one who crossed with full understanding of what I was doing — because I was a scientist, and because I had colleagues who were doing the same thing, which meant I had people to compare notes with. You have the benefit of knowing about me. I had the benefit of Kenji and Park Jinhee."

I wrote: *Capacity is inherited, maternal line. Grandmother's mother had it — uncrossed or unconscious crossing. Liling: first conscious crosser in family.*

"The capacity activated for me in 1972," she said. "At the Vancouver conference. I was thirty-five. There was no warning — it doesn't announce itself. I walked into a park near the conference hotel and I found the Garden and I walked through it because it seemed like the thing to do." She paused, and something crossed her face that was neither the confused expression nor the lucid one — something wry, almost amused. "I was a botanist. A garden that appeared in a park and contained species I had never seen was professionally interesting. I went in."

"You weren't frightened?"

"Later I was frightened," she said. "In the moment I was interested. This is the difference between a scientist and a sensible person." The wry quality again. Then, more seriously: "Kenji had been there for four months already. He found me in Jun's world on my second day. He understood what was happening before I did and he explained it to me with great patience and very little drama, which is how I prefer to receive information."

*Kenji arrived in Jun's world approximately four months before Liling. He understood the crossing before she did. He helped her understand it.*

"We worked well together," she said. "The three of us — Jinhee arrived in January 1972, she and Kenji had already been working in the zone for some months. She is the most practically competent person I have ever met and she was a counterweight to Kenji's tendency to theorize before he had confirmed the data." A pause. "She went home in 1974. Her children. She made the right choice."

I thought about Jing-wei's summary. *Some of us came back. Some of us stayed.* "And Kenji?" I asked.

She was quiet for a long moment. The light on the wall moved its fraction of an inch.

"Kenji stayed," she said. "He had no children. He had a career he did not love the way he loved the work he was doing in the Cascade zone. He had colleagues he respected but no one in his world who understood the forest the way the people in Jun's world understood it." She paused. "He had reasons. I understood his reasons." Another pause. "I had my own reasons for coming back."

"Tell me," I said.

"I discovered I was pregnant in September 1974." She said it with the directness that characterized all of her self-disclosure — not as confession, not as explanation, but as finding. The way you say *I discovered the connectivity gradient.* "I knew immediately that this changed the calculation. Not because I was not capable of raising a child in Jun's world — I was, Kenji would have been a good father, the coalition of researchers and local botanists had the kind of community that raises children well. But because the child was going to carry what I carry. The capacity is maternal line. If my daughter was born in Jun's world, she would begin her life there, and her crossings would take her back to my world — a world she had never lived in — and she would have no framework for it. She would have no Liling."

I looked at my notebook. *Liling pregnant September 1974. Returns to her world to raise Huimei with the capacity properly contextualized.*

"I came back in November 1974," she said. "Not the early 1975 that the TFRI records show — I arrived in Taiwan in November and spent several weeks at the TFRI's outlying research station in Nantou, recovering, preparing the specimens, working with Professor Huang on the matrix's preservation protocol before I formally returned to the Institute." She paused. "I needed those weeks before I could face the institutional questions."

"The extended research period," I said.

"Someone at the TFRI was kind," she said. "More than kind. I think someone understood something, without understanding the specific thing. There is a kind of understanding that doesn't require details. A willingness to receive someone back without making the return conditional on explanation." She looked at the window. "I have been grateful for that my whole life. The world contains people who will do that — who will hold the door open without needing to know what you were doing in the cold."

*Someone at TFRI facilitated her re-entry. Liling doesn't know who. Grateful for undocumented kindness.*

"What I left behind," she said. The tone shifted — not heavier, but more deliberate. The tone of someone coming to the part of the account they have been preparing most carefully. "I knew, before I left Jun's world, that the forest was in worse condition than the scientists in that world had fully measured. I could see it. Kenji could see it. The collapse was going to be severe and it was going to be slow, and the thing it needed most was what I understood: the network, not the individual trees. Intervention at the level of the fungal matrix."

"So you planted the matrix."

"I planted what I could prepare in the time I had," she said. "Fifty years' worth. The species I chose were from the Pacific Rim conifer populations of my world — healthier, more resilient, already adapted to the climate conditions that Jun's world was going to face as the collapse progressed. I selected for cold tolerance and drought tolerance and deep-network propagation capacity. I prepared the matrix at the Cascade zone's optimal substrate location — the cedar, which sits on a ridge with the right elevation, the right moisture gradient, the right mineral substrate for the ectomycorrhizal species. The birch bark holds moisture and keeps the matrix at the right acidity. The pine-resin seal excludes oxygen and prevents desiccation." She said this with the careful diction of someone reciting a protocol that has been held in memory for fifty years and has not been degraded by the holding. "Buried below frost depth. The species I chose — *Rhizopogon* and *Pisolithus* — were selected specifically because they form deep mineral associations in nature; they are already adapted to the substrate conditions at that elevation. The nitrogen-poor mineral substrate was not incidental: it suppresses metabolic rate. In a low-nutrient environment the fungi do not grow, they sleep — and sleeping, they remain viable not for months but for decades. The ectomycorrhizal species I selected can remain viable in a dormant state for far longer than fifty years under those conditions — I confirmed this with the available science in 1974 and I believe it remains true. The science on mycorrhizal viability in preserved matrices has only improved."

I wrote all of it. I wrote quickly and completely, the way she had told me to.

"It was not a solution," she said. "I want to be clear about that. It was a tool. The best tool I could leave. What they do with it, how they culture and deploy it, what the outcome is — I couldn't know that. I didn't try to know it. I left what I could leave and I trusted the future to have someone who would know what to do."

"Jun's coalition will know what to do," I said.

"Yes." She was very still. "I know. Because you are here."

---

She rested for a while. I did not try to fill the silence. The morning had advanced enough that the light on the wall had moved to a different part of the wall — the rectangle had shifted, as it does, tracking the sun across the window's angle. I sat with my notebook and reviewed what I had written and thought about the precision of her: the way she had organized the information, the order she had chosen, the things she had said and not said. The wry quality when she had talked about walking into the Garden because she was a botanist. The deliberateness when she had talked about the matrix.

She was not performing these things for me. She was delivering them. There is a difference.

After a while she said: "I want to tell you something that is not information. Something different."

"Tell me."

She turned from the window to face me fully. This was unusual — she usually looks somewhat to the side of the person she's addressing, as though reading them in peripheral vision rather than head-on. This was head-on.

"I was wrong about one thing," she said. "Not the science. The science was right. I was wrong about what coming back meant."

"What did you think it meant?"

"I thought it meant I had chosen," she said. "That I had made the choosing and it was done. My world, my daughter, my work — I had chosen this over the other thing, and the other thing was finished." She paused. "But that's not how it worked. I never left Jun's world, not really. I brought it back with me. Kenji went with me in the way that the important people always go with you — not as a ghost, not as a regret, but as a part of what you understand. My work, after I came back, was better because of those three years. The papers I published in the eighties — the ones that the field finally caught up to — those came from what I learned in Jun's world. The things he and I understood together." She looked at her hands. "The worlds are not separate. Not after you cross. They are in relation. You carry one into the other and back again."

I was writing, but I was also listening in the way that you listen to something you will need to understand later.

"The crossing is not a problem to be resolved," she said. "I spent too long thinking it was. Thinking I had resolved it by coming home. The resolution I was looking for didn't exist because the crossing is not a disruption. It is what we are." She looked at me. "You and I. What we are."

"Nǎi Nai," I said. My voice was careful. "What do I do?"

She made the small gesture again — fingers spreading, the rooting motion. "What you've been doing," she said. "Keep going. Cross as long as crossing is what the work requires. Come back as long as coming back is what the people you love require. Let it be both. It can be both." She paused. "I came back because I could not leave your mother to become without me. But I never stopped going. The Garden finds you in your sleep sometimes, and in the work, and in the way you read a space. The crossing doesn't end. It changes form."

The light on the wall. The morning.

"I knew," she said quietly, "when you started showing the gaps. The temporal losses. The specific kind of tiredness that comes after the liminal interval. I recognized it because I lived it. And I thought — I thought that I had not been able to give you what you needed, because I was silent about it for so long. I thought: I should have told your mother. She would have told you. You would have known, from childhood, that this was something to look for, something to understand, rather than something to fear."

"I don't think I would have believed it," I said.

She considered this. "Perhaps not," she said. "Perhaps the crossing is always a surprise. Perhaps it needs to be." A pause. "But I could have tried. I chose silence and I don't think it was the right choice." She looked at me directly again. "I am telling you now. I am sorry I didn't tell you sooner. I am telling you now."

I held her hand. I did not say anything for a moment.

"You told me now," I said. "That's what we have."

She patted my hand again. The single pat — the confirming gesture. "Yes," she said. "That's what we have."

Then she said: "Go find the cedar. Take Jun. Do it together."

"I will."

"And—" She paused. The lucid quality was still there but had begun its slow softening, the way it does, the window beginning to narrow. "Tell your mother I love her. Tell her in a way that she can hear. Not an announcement. The way you tell someone something you have always known, when they need to hear it said."

"Yes."

"Good." She settled back. The field-station angle of her chin relaxed. Her eyes went to the light on the wall. "The light is very good today," she said, to herself or to me or to neither of us. "It does something interesting at this angle."

I stayed for the rest of the hour. She drifted — gently, not into the agitated confusion but into the interior mode, the one I no longer read as absence. Before I left she said, without looking at me: "The Garden is beautiful when it glows. I haven't seen it glow in a long time. You will."

I did not ask what she meant. I wrote it in my notebook.

Then I left, and walked down the corridor, and went into the warm New Taipei morning, and stood on the sidewalk for a while holding everything I had been given.
