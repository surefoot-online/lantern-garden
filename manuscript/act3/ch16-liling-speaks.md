### Chapter Sixteen · Liling Speaks

I want to be precise about time.

The Tribunal has asked me, in various forms, to establish timelines: when did I return to Taipei, how long between the phone call on the Zhonghe street and my next crossing, what was the interval between Liling's window of clarity and what followed. I have given the Tribunal dates. The dates are accurate. But dates are not the same as time, and what I am trying to describe is something that happened in time in a way that dates cannot capture. The session with Liling that I am about to describe lasted ninety minutes by the clock on the care facility's wall. It has occupied a different kind of duration in me ever since. I cannot give the Tribunal a number for that.

I had not planned to return to the care facility that morning.

I had planned to go back to Vancouver, cross to Jun's world Thursday as I had said, make the trip to the ridge for the formal acknowledgment. But when I woke in the Zhonghe hotel that my mother had suggested — the hotel where she herself had stayed during Liling's first difficult month, which was a piece of continuity I had not registered until I lay in the unfamiliar bed and recognized the particular smell of the corridor outside — I understood that I was not going to Vancouver. Not yet. What had happened in Liling's room the previous day was not finished.

There are things you learn to read about your own knowledge when you work as a landscape architect. Specifically: the difference between having walked a site and having understood it. You can walk a site with perfect professional attention and leave with excellent technical data and still be missing the thing the site was trying to tell you. The missing is not a failure of observation. It is a failure of sitting still. You learn, if you do this long enough, that some sites require you to return. To stand in them again, differently. To be slower.

Liling was that kind of site.

I walked to the care facility in the morning. The walk was twenty minutes from the hotel — through the market street and then along the quieter road that curved toward the hill where the facility sat. I was aware, during the walk, of something that I can only describe as a quality of the air: the particular stillness of a morning that holds a specific weight inside it, the way certain kinds of light feel like they are containing something that will be significant. I read spaces the way other people read faces. I understood, that morning, that the space of it was telling me something.

She was in the chair again.

The sea-glass chair. The morning light made the same rectangle on the wall, warmer than the previous day because the cloud cover had cleared overnight, and she was looking at the light and I could tell before I crossed the threshold — I have learned to read the quality of her posture from the door — I could tell that she was in a window.

Not just any window. Not the ordinary lucid-interval presence she achieves sometimes, where her eyes clear and she tracks conversation and she knows who I am and can hold a thread. This was something more. Her posture had the field-station quality — the quality I had seen only once before, briefly, in the April visit when she had grabbed my wrist and said *you smell like the crossing.* The chin angle. The particular stillness of someone who is not drifting but attending, specifically, to this.

She turned when I came in.

"Ah-Mei," she said. My name, exactly, with the inflection that belongs to Liling and not to the dementia-version of her that sometimes produces the name as a floating signifier detached from the person it addresses. This was the name given to me, precisely me, the version of me she had been waiting for.

I sat in the family chair. I did not speak. I understood that she was going to.

"I've been thinking," she said, "about what to tell you. Since yesterday. I've been here, thinking about the order to put it in."

Her Mandarin was the formal register of her generation and education — the clean diction of a mid-century scientist, precise vowels, no elision. Not the way she speaks in the confused periods, which is fragmentary and often in the Taiwanese that was the language of her childhood, before the Mandarin of her professional life. This was professional Liling. This was the woman who had written the 1972 paper.

"Start anywhere," I said.

"No." She moved her head slightly — the negation of someone who has organized something and intends to deliver it in the correct sequence. "There is a right order. Let me use it."

I opened my notebook. She watched me open it.

"Good," she said. "Write it down. Your memory will not hold everything the way it holds the spatial things. Mine didn't either. Write it."

I wrote.

---

She began with the capacity. Not the crossing itself — she would come to that — but the thing that makes the crossing possible, which she described in the language of a botanist.

"The capacity is a structural feature," she said. "Like being left-handed. Like synesthesia. It is not a gift and it is not a curse; it is a trait. It runs in the maternal line of our family. I don't know how far back — I discovered this after the fact, by looking at the records the Garden keeps, which are not records the way our institutions keep records, but which are legible if you learn to read them." She paused. "My mother had the capacity. I know this now. She never crossed — or if she crossed, she didn't understand what she was doing, and the experiences registered as something else. Dreams. Visions. The kind of thing that women of her generation and circumstances were expected to keep quiet about."

She looked at me directly. "You are not an anomaly. You are the most recent iteration of something that has been in this family for at least three generations. Possibly more. I am the only one who crossed with full understanding of what I was doing — because I was a scientist, and because I had colleagues doing the same thing, which meant I had people to compare notes with. You have the benefit of knowing about me. I had the benefit of Kenji and Park Jinhee."

I wrote: *Capacity is inherited, maternal line. Grandmother's mother had it — uncrossed or unconscious. Liling: first conscious crosser in family.*

"The capacity activated for me in 1972," she said. "At the Vancouver conference. I was thirty-five. There was no warning — it doesn't announce itself. I walked into a park near the conference hotel and I found the Garden and I walked through it because it seemed like the thing to do." She paused, and something crossed her face that was neither the confused expression nor the lucid one — something wry, almost amused. "I was a botanist. A garden that appeared in a park and contained species I had never seen was professionally interesting. I went in."

"You weren't frightened?"

"Later I was frightened," she said. "In the moment I was interested. This is the difference between a scientist and a sensible person." The wry quality again. Then, more seriously: "Kenji had been there for four months already. He found me in Jun's world on my second day. He understood what was happening before I did and he explained it with great patience and very little drama, which is how I prefer to receive information."

*Kenji arrived approximately four months before Liling. He understood the crossing before she did. He helped her understand it.*

"We worked well together," she said. "The three of us — Jinhee arrived in January. She and Kenji had already been working in the zone for some months. She is the most practically competent person I have ever met and she was a counterweight to Kenji's tendency to theorize before he had confirmed the data." A pause. "She went home in 1974. Her children. She made the right choice."

I thought about Jing-wei's summary. *Some of us came back. Some of us stayed.* "And Kenji?" I asked.

She was quiet for a long moment. The light on the wall moved its fraction of an inch.

"Kenji stayed," she said. "He had no children. He had a career he did not love the way he loved the work in the Cascade zone. He had colleagues he respected but no one in his world who understood the forest the way the people in Jun's world understood it." She paused. "He had reasons. I understood his reasons." Another pause. "I had my own reasons for coming back."

"Tell me," I said.

"I discovered I was pregnant in September 1974." She said it with the directness that characterized all of her self-disclosure — not as confession, not as explanation, but as finding. The way you say *I discovered the connectivity gradient.* "I knew immediately that this changed the calculation. Not because I was incapable of raising a child in Jun's world — I was, Kenji would have been a good father, the coalition of researchers and local botanists had the kind of community that raises children well. But because the child was going to carry what I carry. The capacity is maternal line. If my daughter was born in Jun's world, she would begin her life there, and her crossings would take her back to my world — a world she had never lived in — and she would have no framework for it. She would have no Liling."

I looked at my notebook. *Liling pregnant September 1974. Returns to her world to raise Huimei with the capacity properly contextualized.*

"I came back in November 1974," she said. "Not the early 1975 that the NTU records show — I arrived in Taiwan in November and spent several weeks at the department's outlying station in Nantou, recovering, preparing the specimens, working with Professor Huang on the matrix's preservation protocol before I formally returned to the Institute." She paused. "I needed those weeks before I could face the institutional questions."

"The extended research period," I said.

"Someone at the department was kind," she said. "More than kind. I think someone understood something, without understanding the specific thing. There is a kind of understanding that doesn't require details. A willingness to receive someone back without making the return conditional on explanation." She looked at the window. "I have been grateful for that my whole life. The world contains people who will do that — who will hold the door open without needing to know what you were doing in the cold."

*Someone at NTU facilitated her re-entry. Liling doesn't know who. Grateful for undocumented kindness.*

"What I left behind." The tone shifted — not heavier, but more deliberate. The tone of someone coming to the part of the account they have been preparing most carefully. "I knew, before I left Jun's world, that the forest was in worse condition than the scientists in that world had fully measured. I could see it. Kenji could see it. The collapse was going to be severe and it was going to be slow, and the thing it needed most was what I understood: the network, not the individual trees. Intervention at the level of the fungal matrix."

"So you planted the matrix."

"I planted what I could prepare in the time I had," she said. "Fifty years' worth. The species I chose were from the Pacific Rim conifer populations of my world — healthier, more resilient, already adapted to the climate conditions that Jun's world was going to face as the collapse progressed. I selected for cold tolerance and drought tolerance and deep-network propagation capacity." She said this with the careful diction of someone reciting a protocol held in memory for fifty years and not degraded by the holding. "The cedar sits on a ridge with the right elevation, the right moisture gradient, the right mineral substrate for the ectomycorrhizal species. The birch bark holds moisture and keeps the matrix at the right acidity. The pine-resin seal excludes oxygen and prevents desiccation. Buried below frost depth. The species I chose — *Rhizopogon* and *Pisolithus* — were selected specifically because they form deep mineral associations; they are already adapted to the substrate conditions at that elevation. The nitrogen-poor mineral substrate suppresses metabolic rate. In a low-nutrient environment the fungi do not grow, they sleep — and sleeping, they remain viable not for months but for decades. I confirmed this with the available science in 1974 and I believe it remains true."

I wrote all of it. I wrote quickly and completely, the way she had told me to.

"It was not a solution," she said. "I want to be clear. It was a tool. The best tool I could leave. What they do with it, how they culture and deploy it, what the outcome is — I couldn't know that. I didn't try to know it. I left what I could leave and I trusted the future to have someone who would know what to do."

"Jun's coalition will know what to do," I said.

"Yes." She was very still. "I know. Because you are here."

---

She rested for a while. The morning had advanced enough that the light on the wall had shifted — the rectangle had moved, tracking the sun across the window's angle. I sat with my notebook and reviewed what I had written and thought about the precision of her: the way she had organized the information, the order she had chosen, the things she had said and not said. The wry quality when she talked about walking into the Garden because she was a botanist. The deliberateness when she talked about the matrix. She was not performing these things for me. She was delivering them. There is a difference.

After a while she said: "I want to tell you something about Kenji. Not the science. Something different."

"Tell me."

She looked at me — the direct look, not the peripheral-vision read she usually uses. Head-on.

"He understood the network before any of us understood the network," she said. "Not in my language — in his. He had a different framework, from the Japanese ecology tradition, a different way of reading the connection between things. He used the word *satoyama* to describe what he was trying to restore — not a translation, not a Western concept, something specific to how his tradition understood the relationship between human-managed land and forest. When we compared notes we found that we were describing the same reality in different grammars." She paused. "This is where I learned that the truth about a system has to be larger than any single methodology for understanding it."

"That's in the methodology section of the 1972 paper," I said.

A pause. "Yes," she said. "It is. People read it as a theoretical preamble. It was not theoretical. It was a record of what I learned from Kenji in six months of field work."

I wrote: *1972 paper's methodology section — not theoretical. Empirical record of Kenji's approach and their integration. He used satoyama framework. She adapted her mycorrhizal connectivity model to incorporate it.*

"There is something I want you to know," she said, "about where he was trained. It is relevant to something you may encounter."

I waited.

"Kenji did his graduate work at Kyoto University. Ecology faculty. His supervisor was a man named Professor Matsuda Hiroaki — a forest ecologist, old-school, not interested in the mycorrhizal questions that were just beginning to emerge in the late sixties. Kenji worked against the grain of his training. He was a student of Matsuda's in the formal sense and his own scientist in every other sense." She paused. "He told me this in the second month. He said: my supervisor believed that individual trees competed for resources. I believe they share them. He said it without rancor — he was not angry about Matsuda, he simply understood that he had learned what he needed to learn at Kyoto and then moved past it. This is the correct relationship to have with one's training."

I wrote: *Kenji: Kyoto University ecology faculty, late 1960s. Supervisor Matsuda Hiroaki. Mycorrhizal network sharing model — developed against the grain of his training. Moved past competition-model without rejecting the rigor.*

"And you?" I asked. "Where did you train?"

She looked at me with the expression of someone who has been asked a question they expected and finds it reasonable. "Nagoya," she said. "I did my doctorate at Nagoya University. 1963 to 1967. The botany faculty there had a strong relationship with the Taiwan botanical community — it was a natural pathway for Taiwanese women in STEM at that time, given the language access and the institutional connections that predated the war. My supervisor was Professor Miyake Fumiko. She was the only woman on the faculty and she had no patience for any student who was not rigorous, male or female." A pause — the wry quality again. "I learned to be rigorous. I had no choice."

*Liling: doctorate at Nagoya University, 1963–1967. Botany faculty. Supervisor Miyake Fumiko — first woman on faculty, high standards, institutional connection with Taiwan botanical community via pre-war academic networks. Japanese language access was the pathway.*

I held my pen.

"I did not mention Nagoya in the published work," she said. "I said 'private correspondence with colleagues in Japan' in the methodology notes — which was accurate, Miyake and I continued to correspond until she died. But I was trying, in those years, to be legible as a Taiwanese scientist working in Taiwanese institutions. The NTU affiliation was what mattered for my standing at the TFRI. I did not want my work to be dismissed because of where I had trained." She paused. "In retrospect this was overcautious. The work stood on its own. But I was thirty-one when the paper was published and I was still learning which battles needed to be fought and which could be avoided."

This was the longest she had talked about herself that was not the crossing. I registered it as significant.

"It matters," I said, "for something Kenji wrote."

She waited.

"In the journals. He wrote about you reading the moisture gradient in the terrain — identifying the buried drainage from the surface vegetation. He said you and he had different windows into the same room." I paused. "I've been reading those journals for two weeks and I kept wondering how you learned that skill. Whether it was Nagoya or the TFRI or whether it was simply yours."

She considered this. "Miyake taught me to read a site," she said. "She was a plant ecologist and she used the terrain the way a carpenter uses the grain of wood — reading the way the living things were responding to the underlying conditions. I adapted that to the mycorrhizal questions. Kenji had the same skill from a different teacher." She looked at her hands. "We recognized each other immediately. Not as potential colleagues — as people who read the world the same way. This is, I have found, rarer than it should be."

I wrote: *The site-reading skill has a pedagogy. Miyake at Nagoya. Kenji's parallel development at Kyoto. Both adapted to mycorrhizal questions independently — recognized each other across the difference in their national training traditions.*

"I want to tell you something that is not information," she said. "Something different."

"Tell me."

She turned to face me fully. "I was wrong about one thing," she said. "Not the science. The science was right. I was wrong about what coming back meant."

"What did you think it meant?"

"I thought it meant I had chosen," she said. "That I had made the choosing and it was done. My world, my daughter, my work — I had chosen this over the other thing, and the other thing was finished." She paused. "But that's not how it worked. I never left Jun's world, not really. I brought it back with me. Kenji went with me in the way that the important people always go with you — not as a ghost, not as a regret, but as a part of what you understand. My work after I came back was better because of those three years. The papers I published in the eighties — the ones that the field finally caught up to — those came from what I learned in Jun's world. The things he and I understood together." She looked at her hands. "The worlds are not separate. Not after you cross. They are in relation. You carry one into the other and back again."

I was writing, but I was also listening in the way you listen to something you will need to understand later.

"The crossing is not a problem to be resolved," she said. "I spent too long thinking it was. Thinking I had resolved it by coming home. The resolution I was looking for didn't exist because the crossing is not a disruption. It is what we are." She looked at me. "You and I. What we are."

"Nǎi Nai," I said. My voice was careful. "What do I do?"

She made the small gesture — fingers spreading, the rooting motion. "What you've been doing," she said. "Cross as long as crossing is what the work requires. Come back as long as coming back is what the people you love require. Let it be both. It can be both." She paused. "I came back because I could not leave your mother to become without me. But I never stopped going. The Garden finds you in your sleep sometimes, and in the work, and in the way you read a space. The crossing doesn't end. It changes form."

The light on the wall. The morning.

"I knew," she said quietly, "when you started showing the gaps. The temporal losses. The specific tiredness that comes after the liminal interval. I recognized it because I lived it. And I thought — I thought that I had not been able to give you what you needed, because I was silent about it for so long. I thought: I should have told your mother. She would have told you. You would have known, from childhood, that this was something to look for, something to understand, rather than something to fear."

"I don't think I would have believed it," I said.

She considered this. "Perhaps not," she said. "Perhaps the crossing is always a surprise. Perhaps it needs to be." A pause. "But I could have tried. I chose silence and I don't think it was the right choice." She looked at me directly again. "I am telling you now. I am sorry I didn't tell you sooner. I am telling you now."

I held her hand. I did not say anything for a moment.

"You told me now," I said. "That's what we have."

She patted my hand — the single pat, the confirming gesture. "Yes," she said. "That's what we have."

Then she said: "Go find the cedar. Take Jun. Do it together."

"We already did," I said. "Last Saturday. Thomas prepared an acknowledgment. The matrix is being cultured."

She was very still. Something moved through her expression that I can only describe as the relief of someone who has been holding a breath for fifty years and has been told, finally, that they can stop.

"Good," she said. The word was barely audible. The specific good of a finding confirmed.

"And—" She paused. The lucid quality was still there but had begun its slow softening. "Tell your mother I love her. Tell her in a way that she can hear. Not an announcement. The way you tell someone something you have always known, when they need to hear it said."

"Yes."

"Good." She settled back. The field-station angle of her chin relaxed. Her eyes went to the light on the wall. "The light is very good today," she said, to herself or to me or to neither of us. "It does something interesting at this angle."

I stayed for the rest of the hour. She drifted — gently, not into the agitated confusion but into the interior mode, the one I no longer read as absence. Before I left she said, without looking at me: "The Garden is beautiful when it glows. I haven't seen it glow in a long time. You will."

I did not ask what she meant. I wrote it in my notebook.

Then I left, and walked down the corridor, and went into the warm New Taipei morning, and stood on the sidewalk for a while holding everything I had been given.
