### Chapter Twenty-Three · What Liling Left

The call from the care facility came on a Wednesday.

Not the call. Not the final call. The nurse's voice was calm — the specific calm of someone delivering information that is difficult but that is her professional task to deliver clearly: Liling had had a difficult night, some respiratory distress, the doctor had adjusted her medications, she was comfortable now but the family should know.

"Should I come?" I asked.

"It might be a good time," the nurse said. Which meant: yes.

I called my mother. She was already at the facility — she had arrived, she said, an hour after the night staff had called her, and she was sitting in the common room because Liling was sleeping and the room was too small for two people to sit beside a sleeping person without the second person's presence becoming an intrusion.

"I'll come today," I said.

"Yes," my mother said. "Come today."

I flew Taipei on the afternoon flight.

The facility in the evening had the smell. My body knows it now — the specific layered quality of it — and responds to it the way a body responds to a smell that carries meaning: a slight heightening of attention, a preparing of something. Not dread. Something more neutral than dread and more engaged than neutrality.

Liling was awake.

She was in the chair — the sea-glass chair — not looking at the wall this time but at her hands. Her hands in her lap. Her hands were still the hands of a botanist: long fingers, careful evidence of a life spent in close attention to small things. The joints swollen now, the skin thin in the way that age makes skin thin, but the hands themselves still recognizably the hands that had held a field notebook for fifty years and a daughter for sixty and a granddaughter for thirty-four.

I sat in the family chair. My mother was in the doorway behind me.

Liling looked up. She looked at me, and then past me at my mother.

"Ah-Mei," she said. My name, clearly. Then: "Huimei."

My mother came and sat in the second chair — the chair that was usually against the wall, that the staff moved in for occasions with multiple visitors. She sat and she took her mother's hand. Not tentatively. Fully.

Liling looked at her hand in my mother's hand. The expression on her face: not the confused look, not the lucid-window clarity, something in between and past both of them. The expression of someone who has arrived somewhere they were going.

She spoke then. Not continuously — this was not a lucid window in the sense of the long window that had given me ninety minutes. This was brief, specific, the clarity that surfaces when the person has something specific they need to say and rises to say it.

"I need to tell you," she said to my mother. "About the matrix."

My mother glanced at me. I shook my head slightly — *let her.*

"The matrix is in the ground," Liling said. "On the ridge. The cedar." She was looking at my mother's hand in hers. "It has what it needs. I gave it what it needs. It's been waiting." A pause. "It's been found. Ah-Mei found it." She looked at me briefly — confirming — then back at my mother's hand. "It will grow now. The network will grow."

"Yes, Nǎi Nai," I said.

"Good." She settled. Then, in a different register — not the field researcher but the woman: "Huimei. You smell like your father."

My mother was very still.

Liling was looking at their joined hands, and her face had the quality of someone inside a memory, inside the room of the memory, not remembering it from outside but occupying it. "He smelled like cedar," she said. "When he came from the forest. He always smelled like the forest." A pause. "He was patient. He was patient with everything."

My mother's hand tightened on Liling's.

"I loved him," Liling said. Simply, as she always states findings. Without apology, without embellishment, without the weight of fifty years of silence around the statement, as though the silence had not been, as though this was simply the fact, finally spoken aloud, and the air received it without incident. "I loved him very much."

My mother said nothing. She was crying — the quiet way she cries, the way that is more feeling than performance.

"I loved you more," Liling said. Still simple. "You were already becoming. I couldn't leave."

"I know," my mother said. Her voice was steady, the way voices are steady when the person has passed through the shaking part and come out the other side. "I know that now."

"Good," Liling said. The confirming gesture — she could not quite manage the single pat with her hand in my mother's, so she pressed instead, a slight pressure that was its own confirmation. "Good."

She drifted then. Softly. Into the interior mode — the attending-to-something-only-she-could-see. Her hand stayed in my mother's hand and she did not seem to need or not need anything; she was simply somewhere, in the particular way of wherever she went when she went.

My mother and I sat on either side of her in the room's evening light.

After a while I excused myself — I said I would be in the common room, I said take your time — and I went and sat in the common room with the snake plant and the corridor sounds and I called Jun.

He picked up on the second ring. I could hear the monitoring station's background.

"She's declining," I said. "The facility called this morning. I'm here now."

"I'm sorry," he said. And then: "Is she — is she present?"

"In and out. She said some things tonight. To my mother." I paused. "Important things."

"Good," he said. It was the same word Liling used — the same specific meaning, the confirmation of satisfactory outcome. I registered this without comment.

"I need to tell you something," I said. "About the second window." I had told him about the ninety minutes — the science, the coordinates, the burial protocol. I had not told him everything else. I had told him only what was directly relevant to the ridge and to the matrix and to the coalition's work. Now I told him the rest.

I told him about the capacity's inheritance. The maternal line. The way Liling had described the crossing as structural — as what they were, not what had happened to them.

He was quiet on the line for a long time.

"She's been crossing in her dementia," I said. "She told me. She said the Garden doesn't require full consciousness — that it seeks her. She's been spending time in the between-place. She said: it's quiet there. I think I'll stay there for a while, when I go."

Jun said nothing. I could hear him breathing.

"I don't know what to do with that," I said. "I don't know if it's literally true — if she's actually been in the liminal space while what we see is a woman in a chair in a care facility. Or if it's a way of understanding her own diminishment that gives it a frame she can inhabit." I paused. "I've decided it doesn't matter which. What matters is that she's not frightened. What matters is that wherever she goes when she goes far away, it's somewhere she's been before."

"Mei," Jun said. His voice had the quality it had when he's working through something that the ecological framework doesn't quite capture — when he hits the edges of the methodology and is deciding whether to respect the edges or press past them. "If what she describes is real — if the Garden exists in a state that is accessible to her even now — then the crossing isn't contingent on full cognitive function. It's in the body. In the structure."

"Yes."

"Then she hasn't lost it," he said. "Whatever the dementia has taken. She hasn't lost the capacity."

"No," I said. "I don't think she has."

He was quiet again. "That's not nothing," he said. And then he said, more quietly: "Is she going to be all right?"

I looked at the corridor. The snake plant in its pot. The sound of someone moving at the far end of the hall. "No," I said. "She's going to die. Not tonight, I think. But soon."

"Then I'm sorry," he said again. "I'm genuinely sorry."

"I know." I held the phone and looked at the common room's evening light. "She asked me to tell you it matters that you keep going. That's not — she said that in the first window. I'm telling you again because I think she'd want it said again."

Jun was quiet for a long time. "Tell her — " He stopped. "You don't need to tell her anything from me. She knows."

"She probably does," I said.

"Mei." His voice shifted again — the working-through quality. "The matrix. What she planted. It's being cultured correctly?"

"Priya has it. Yes."

"And the protocol — the birch bark, the pine resin, the mineral substrate — Priya understood the preservation conditions?"

"She did. She had questions, and I answered them from the field notes. She's confident."

"Good." A pause. "It's extraordinary, what she did. Fifty years. She trusted that fifty years was not too long."

"She checked the science," I said. "She trusted data, not faith."

"Sometimes they arrive at the same place," he said.

I thought about this. "Sometimes," I said. "Yes."

I went back to the room after a while. My mother was still sitting with Liling, still holding her hand. Liling was in the interior mode. The room was quiet.

We sat that way for an hour, my mother and I, on either side of the woman in the sea-glass chair. After a while the night nurse came to do the evening round and gently, professionally, indicated the visiting hours. We rose.

In the corridor, my mother and I walked toward the exit.

She did not say anything for a long time. We were at the outer door when she said: "She loved him."

"Yes."

"She actually loved him." She said it the way she had said it on the phone — placing the statement in its correct position, registering the arrival of something that had needed to arrive. "I always thought the grief was the proof that something had gone wrong. Something had been lost. But it was the proof of the thing itself." She looked at the door. "If you love something, you grieve it. You can't grieve what you didn't have."

"No," I said.

"The grief was the fullness," she said again. I let her say it again; she needed to say it again. "Not the absence of it."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what it was."

She pushed the door open and we walked into the New Taipei evening: the warmth of it, the specific ambient sound of a city that runs late, the smell of the night market somewhere two blocks over, the particular quality of the light that comes from a city that doesn't fully go dark.

"Tell me about Jun," she said.

And I did — the version I could tell her, which was real and true and left out the parts that required a framework she didn't have. I told her about the forest, and the coalition, and the spatial modeling, and what it felt like to have a skill find its correct use. I told her about the southern cluster's seven orange monitoring flags. I told her about Priya and the culture conditions and the ridge corridor that might be a northern bypass if everything held.

She listened, and she said, when I paused: "He's fighting for something that might not happen in his lifetime."

"Yes."

"Like she was," my mother said. "Like what she planted."

"Yes," I said. "Like exactly that."
