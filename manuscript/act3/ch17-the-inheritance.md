### Chapter Seventeen · The Inheritance

I did not cross for three days.

This was the longest I had gone without crossing since the crossings began. I did not plan it as a deliberate pause — it was not a decision, exactly, so much as a recognition that what Liling had given me needed time before it could be carried. The way a graft needs to rest before it will take. The way certain conversations continue after the conversation ends, in the body, in the quality of attention you bring to ordinary things, in the dreams that are not quite dreams.

I went back to my Vancouver apartment. I slept. I ran the substrate models for the coalition and sent them to Jun with a message that said only: *The acknowledgment was documented. Working on the substrate update — will explain everything when I cross.* He wrote back: *Take the time you need.*

I did not explain more. I needed the three days to think in my own world's light, in my own apartment's sounds, in the particular quality of Vancouver's late-spring weather — gray and wet and mine in the way that few things still felt mine.

The apartment had accumulated the small evidence of my double life: an extra coat I had not planned to buy, purchased during the week I'd spent embedded with the coalition, that hung in the closet next to my own coats with the specific smell of the Cascade zone's wood smoke still faintly in it. A pair of boots by the door that I wore in Jun's world because they were better for the terrain. The spatial models of the test zone on my work monitor, which Hana Park's firm had not asked about because they were filed under a client code that was not technically false — I had been doing spatial analysis, and I had been doing it for a consortium of some kind. The fact that the consortium existed in a different world was a level of detail I had not been required to specify.

The apartment also had, on the kitchen table, a folder of materials I had been printing and annotating over the course of the six months: Liling's published papers, Jing-wei's institutional findings, the scanned pages of the field reports with my own marginal notes in pencil. I had been building a physical archive in the apartment the same way I built physical archives for client sites — the habit of making the project legible in material form, not just in files, not just in the model on the screen. Something about paper. Something about the specific friction of turning a page that the spatial sense responds to.

I sat at the kitchen table with the folder and I did not open it. I sat with it closed in front of me and I looked at the cover — the manila folder, the handwriting I'd used to label it, the dates — and I understood something about what I had been doing for six months that I had not understood while I was doing it.

I had been building a case.

Not a legal case — not what the Tribunal would later understand as a case, with its evidential structure and its jurisdictional claims. A case in the older sense: the thing you build when you are trying to understand something that resists understanding. The way scientists build cases: accumulating observations, testing them against each other, looking for the point where the evidence stops being interpretable as coincidence and becomes describable as structure.

The case I had been building was about my grandmother. Or it had started as being about my grandmother and it had become something else — had become, I understood now, looking at the closed folder, a case about me. About what I was. About the structure of the thing I had been calling a disruption and that Liling had called, in the clean diction of a mid-century scientist: a trait.

I sat at my desk and looked at Liling's words in my notebook.

*The capacity is structural. Maternal line. What we are.*

*The worlds are not separate. You carry one into the other and back again.*

*The crossing is not a problem to be resolved. It is what we are.*

I read these the way you read a diagnosis you have been waiting for: not with surprise, but with the specific relief of having a name for the thing you have been living. The relief was not clean. It was mixed with something I had to sit with carefully before I could name it.

The something was this: I had spent six months — longer, really, the ten weeks of crossings that preceded the novel's starting point, and the weeks before that when the crossings were beginning and I was interpreting them as symptoms of something wrong with me — I had spent all of that time in a posture of defensive management. Managing the crossings so they didn't cost too much. Managing the time so no one noticed the absences. Managing my professional life so it didn't collapse while I was somewhere else. Managing Jun so that my eventual exit from his world would not cause disproportionate damage. Managing my mother so that the questions she didn't know to ask would not be asked.

Management is exhausting in a specific way. It is exhausting because it requires you to continuously relate to your own life as a problem to be contained. You do not live a managed life; you administer it.

What Liling had told me was that I had been managing the wrong thing.

That self had always felt slightly wrong to me, in a way I had never been able to specify. Not unhappy — unhappy is too simple, unhappy has causes and addresses. Wrong in the way that a poorly fitted thing is wrong: functional, serviceable, nothing you can name without sounding ungrateful, but wrong in the body's knowledge in a way that surfaces as tiredness, as vague dissatisfaction, as the sense that the most important thing you are capable of feeling is not being felt.

I thought about what Liling had said: *I have always known what a space is doing.*

I had been reading spaces since I was a child. The way other children read faces, I read the spatial grammar of rooms and paths and gardens — the movement a space invited, the emotion a threshold created, the silence a particular arrangement of planted things produced in the person who walked through it. I had made this into a profession, which was the most obvious thing to do with it, and the profession was good, and I was good at the profession, and none of it had ever reached the register where the capacity felt fully used.

Until Jun's forest.

Until the first time I looked at the die-off pattern in the south plot and saw the collapse vector that the coalition's models had missed — saw it spatially, in three dimensions across actual terrain, in the way that Liling had looked at the mycorrhizal connectivity gradient in 1971 and seen the forest's intelligence in a way that the field would not name for two more decades. Until I felt my skill find its correct use, finally, in the specific context where it was needed.

And now I had another piece: Liling had learned to read a site from Miyake Fumiko at Nagoya. Kenji had learned a parallel skill at Kyoto, from a supervisor who believed trees competed for resources and a student who believed they shared them. Two scientists trained in Japan in the late 1960s, two different traditions converging on the same understanding, carrying that understanding across the Garden into a world that needed it.

I had studied in Vancouver. I had learned the spatial read from a professor at UBC who had trained in Copenhagen, which had trained under a German landscape architect who had written the foundational text of the movement in 1947. Different chains of transmission. The same underlying perception.

*Different windows into the same room.*

I thought about the chain of transmission in that phrase. Miyake at Nagoya, teaching a young Taiwanese botanist to read vegetation as the surface expression of the ground's conditions. Whoever had taught Miyake. The unnamed scientists in the earlier generations of ecology who had been the first to understand that a forest's surface life was a legible record of everything happening beneath it. The Indigenous land stewards who had understood this before the scientists named it, in a tradition that didn't require a published methodology section to make the knowledge transmissible.

Thomas, at the cedar, speaking in two languages. The acknowledgment. The way knowledge travels through time.

The second day, I was still in Taipei.

I had not booked a flight home. I had not told Jun this — his message said *take the time you need* and I was taking the time I needed in a hotel room in New Taipei, which was not something I had planned when I flew in for the care facility visit but which was, I was coming to understand, exactly where I needed to be.

I walked through the botanical garden near the National Taiwan University campus. Late afternoon, the closing light through the old-growth trees at the angle that turns late spring gold, families and students and one older man feeding the turtles in the pond at the garden's center.

I sat on a bench and watched a space doing what it was doing.

The path through this garden — I had walked it once before, years ago, a visit to the university library for a project that had required access to their historical planning documents. I remembered the path differently than it was. I had remembered it as shorter, as more direct, as less complex in its routing. Returning to it now, after six months of reading spaces in a different world, under different conditions of urgency, for different stakes — the path was doing things I had not noticed the first time. The way it curved slightly away from the pond before approaching it, creating a moment of anticipation before the reveal. The way the planting on the south side of the path was denser, creating a sense of enclosure that released when you reached the open lawn section near the turtles. A sequence, carefully choreographed, that you experienced as simply enjoying a garden but that was actually guiding you through a specific set of emotional states in a specific order.

Someone had designed this garden with great care. Someone had understood the spatial grammar well enough to make the emotional argument through arrangement rather than instruction.

I thought: *I have been reading spaces my whole life. This is the organ. The crossing is not the organ; the spatial intelligence is. The crossing is what the organ does when it encounters something that needs what it has.*

I thought: *Liling had it. The organ. She understood the mycorrhizal network before anyone else in her field because she could read the forest's spatial grammar — the garden's underground language. She crossed because she found the Garden and walked into it, and she ended up in the Cascade zone, and the Cascade zone needed what she had.*

I thought: *I found the Garden and walked into it, and I ended up in the Cascade zone, and the Cascade zone needed what I have.*

Not because the Garden chose me. Not because I was fated for this. Because I am a spatial reader and the Garden is a spatial phenomenon and the Cascade zone is a spatial problem — and spatial readers and spatial problems find each other the way water finds its level. Not magic. Physics. Not fate. Ecology.

I thought: *My grandmother is an ecologist and so am I and so is the crossing itself, finding the path through the substrate toward the next available connection.*

I sat in the botanical garden for an hour and watched the space do its work: moving the people through it, giving them the experience it was designed to give them, without any of them knowing they were being guided. This is what good design does. It is invisible. It makes its argument through the arrangement of things in space rather than through explicit instruction, and the argument is received in the body before the mind registers that an argument has been made.

I thought about how I had been making an argument to myself for six months — the argument that the crossing was a problem, that it was temporary, that it would resolve into a decision. The argument had been received in the body before the mind registered it, which is why I had been so tired: managing the argument's constant presence alongside everything else.

The argument was wrong. I had been receiving the wrong argument and acting on it.

On the third day I walked to the MRT and took the line toward the care facility and then, on the train, sitting with the tunnel's moving dark outside the window, I stopped and thought: not today. She has said what she needed to say. I have received what I need to receive. Going back today would be my need, not hers, and the difference matters.

I rode to the end of the line and back. I watched the other passengers with the specific attentiveness that I bring to spaces and that extends, in the right conditions, to the people in them: the woman with the plastic bag of vegetables from the night market, her face with the specific tiredness of someone who has been performing competence all day and is now, in transit, at rest. The young man with the headphones, his foot keeping time to something only he could hear. The old man with the cane who navigated the train's movement with the body-knowledge of someone who had been navigating it for decades.

All of them in their crossings. All of them between one place and another.

The inheritance was not the crossing. The inheritance was the capacity to be fully present in the between.

I thought about writing this down and decided not to. Some realizations arrive in language and some arrive in a form that language would diminish. This was the second kind. I let it sit in me without words, the way the network Liling had planted sat below the frost line for fifty years — not growing, not diminishing, maintaining the state of readiness until the conditions were right for the next thing.

When I got back to the hotel I booked a flight to Vancouver, packed my bag, and sat at the small desk by the window to write Jun a message. I started three versions. The first was too technical — a report on the substrate models. The second was too much the opposite — I stopped it before I sent it because I was not sure yet what I meant by what I was trying to say. The third said: *Ready to cross Thursday. I have something to tell you about the capacity. Will tell you on site.*

He wrote back in eleven minutes: *I'll have the equipment ready. Also: the overnight monitoring came back with two new nodes in the northern corridor. We should look at them together.*

I read his message and I thought: the equipment. The trowels and the testing kits and the sampling containers and the documentation cameras. Jun's coalition, with its practical readiness, its insistence on doing the thing rather than only understanding the thing.

I loved him for this. Not in the way I had been trying to classify my feeling for him — the professional respect with the romantic undertow, the careful characterization I had been using to keep it at a manageable distance. I loved him, straightforwardly, in the way that you love someone who has the same orientation to the world that you have: facing toward the work, doing the work, refusing to let the work be merely theoretical. I loved him the way you love a person who will stand next to you in a dying forest and read the monitoring data without flinching and then say: two new nodes. We should look at them together.

I did not write any of this in my message. I put my phone down and I sat in the hotel room with the weight of the inheritance — which was not a burden, which was the wrong word for it, which was something more like the weight of a tool that finally fits the hand, the particular weight of a thing whose mass is now distributed correctly along the bones — and I let the three days close around me.

Then I flew home to Vancouver. And then Thursday came, and I crossed.
