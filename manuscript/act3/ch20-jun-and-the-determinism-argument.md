### Chapter Twenty · Jun and the Determinism Argument

I told him everything in the monitoring station before we went to the ridge.

We had three hours before we were going to need to start the drive to the boulder field, and I needed those three hours to say what I had to say without the urgency of the terrain waiting. We sat across the table with bad coffee and I laid it out: Liling's full account, the ninety minutes, the capacity's inheritance and its mechanism, the choice to come back, the precise details of the matrix's burial protocol — birch bark, pine-resin seal, ectomycorrhizal species selected for the climate conditions, mineral substrate below frost depth, viable for decades beyond what anyone would have expected. I gave him the science as Liling had given it to me, in the language of a botanist who had trusted the data.

Jun listened in the way he listens. Completely. Without moving much. When I stopped talking he was quiet for a while, looking at the coffee cup.

"She planned this in 1974," he said.

"Yes."

"She planned this for fifty years. She chose the species, she chose the substrate, she prepared a matrix that she knew — that she *knew* — would still be viable when someone came to find it." He looked up at me. "That's not — I'm trying to understand the scale of what she did."

"She was the best mycorrhizal scientist of her generation," I said. "Twenty years ahead of the field. She knew what she was doing."

"I know," he said. "I know she knew what she was doing. That's what I'm trying to — the scale of trust involved in that. She left something and she trusted that the future would contain someone who would know what to do with it." He paused. "She trusted you."

"She trusted the pattern," I said. "She'd seen how the crossing works — the capacity, the spatial skill, the way it deposits you where the work is. She knew that someone with the capacity would cross into this zone and eventually find their way to her. She left the matrix for that person."

"She left it for you specifically."

"No. She left it for whoever came. I happened to be the one." I paused. "I happened to be her granddaughter. That was a coincidence of genetics and the maternal line and the fact that the capacity passed to me. The matrix would have been there for anyone who came."

Jun was quiet again. I could see him working through something — the working face, the specific stillness of active calculation.

Then he said: "Then you were always going to find me."

I recognized the shape of what was coming and I felt the resistance arrive before he finished the sentence.

"The crossing always leads to someone fighting for the forest," he said. "Maybe that's what it's for. Maybe the Garden is — maybe it's not random. Maybe it's an ecological mechanism, finding the most effective path between connected points. Liling crossed and she ended up exactly where the forest needed her. You crossed and you ended up exactly where the forest needed you. That's not coincidence, Mei. That's—"

"Determinism," I said.

He blinked. "I was going to say ecology."

"Ecology as determinism," I said. "The idea that the system's logic supersedes the individual's choice. That I was going to arrive here because the system needed me to arrive here."

"Isn't that what happened?"

"No." I was certain of this in a way I hadn't expected — the certainty arrived whole, without needing to be constructed. "That's not what happened and I need you to understand why it matters."

He sat back slightly. The expression of someone who is prepared to be argued with, which is one of the things I respect most about Jun — his willingness to sit still while someone challenges a framework he's been thinking in.

"The Garden exists," I said. "It is a place. It has properties. One of the properties is that people with the crossing capacity can traverse it between worlds. Another property, which you're describing, is that it tends to deposit crossers in locations where their specific skills have relevance. This may be true. I think it probably is true. But that property is a feature of the transit, not a directive." I paused, trying to find the precision. "I walked into the Garden in Vancouver because I found it and I was curious. I did not walk into it because the Garden needed me to. I walked into it because I found it. What happened afterward — arriving in your forest, developing the spatial analysis, finding the coalition — those were things I did. Choices I made with the agency available to me."

"Inside a system that was shaping your choices."

"All choices happen inside a system that shapes them," I said. "That doesn't make them not choices."

He was quiet.

"If I had walked into the Garden and arrived in your world and decided not to engage — decided to cross back immediately, or arrived and walked in the wrong direction, or arrived and been so frightened I couldn't function — the system would not have forced me to help. The system put me in the vicinity of the work. I decided to do the work." I looked at him. "Jun. If I were a mechanism of fate, you would not need to love me. You could simply receive what the mechanism provides."

He looked at me for a long time. Something moved through his expression — a complication of things.

"And if you are a person making choices," he said slowly, "then the help you've given us is — it's a gift. Not an inevitability. It's yours to give."

"Yes."

"And the seed bank. Liling's matrix. She didn't plant it because the system required her to. She planted it because she was a scientist who understood what the forest needed and she made a choice to leave something useful behind."

"Yes."

"That's more—" He stopped. "That's more moving than the determinism version. The determinism version is elegant but it doesn't have anyone in it. Just forces."

"Right."

He picked up the coffee cup and set it down again without drinking. "Then what are you choosing now?" he asked.

I had been waiting for this question. Not because I had a prepared answer — I hadn't, exactly — but because I had been circling toward the answer for three days in Vancouver and I knew approximately where it was.

"I'm choosing what I am," I said.

He looked at me with the expression of someone hearing something they don't yet understand but recognize as significant.

"I'm not choosing your world," I said. "I'm not choosing my world. I'm choosing to be what I am, which is someone who crosses. Who exists in the between as well as on either side. Who can carry what each world knows to the other." I paused. "Your grandmother's generation — in my world — they used to call it code-switching. The way bilingual people move between languages without losing competence in either. The way diaspora people carry multiple cultural grammars simultaneously. It was characterized as a burden, sometimes. Or as a kind of homelessness. But it's not homelessness. It's — it's a form of being that has its own intelligence. Its own logic. You can see things from the between that you cannot see from either side."

"What does that look like practically?" he said. Not dismissively — pragmatically. Jun wanting to understand the operational parameters.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "I know what I can do. I can carry data. I can carry analysis. I can carry knowledge between the two forests — your forest and mine, which are related forests facing related problems at different stages. I can model both and I can understand both and I can tell each what the other knows." I paused. "I can find the cedar and show your coalition what Liling left, and I can translate her field notes in real time, and I can explain the burial protocol so they culture the matrix correctly."

"That's today," he said.

"That's today. Tomorrow I don't know. But I'm not going to close the door on tomorrow by making a permanent declaration about which side of the door I'm on."

He was quiet for a long time. The monitoring station hummed its equipment hum. Outside, the early-morning light of Jun's world was the amber-and-haze that it always is — the quality I have stopped reading as ominous and have started reading as simply *this*, the specific quality of light in a world that is fighting.

"I don't understand everything you're describing," he said finally. "I want to tell you that. I don't have a frame for what you're talking about — I have ecology and I have physics and I have the spatial modeling you've taught me to do, and none of those give me a framework for what it means to choose to be a bridge rather than choosing a side."

"I know."

"But I can see what you mean." He looked at me. "I can't say I like it. I can say I can see it."

"That's enough," I said. "For now, that's enough."

He nodded, slowly. Then he stood up and picked up his jacket from the back of the chair. "Then we should go to the ridge," he said. "Liling's matrix has been waiting fifty years. We shouldn't make it wait longer."

"No," I said. "We shouldn't."

We went.
