### Chapter Eighteen · Jun and the Determinism Argument

I told him everything in the monitoring station before we went to the northern corridor.

We had two hours before we were going to need to start the drive to the monitoring sites, and I needed those two hours to say what I had to say without the urgency of the terrain waiting. I had driven up from the Garden — the crossing, the cool of the early morning, Jun's world's spring advancing in the way it advanced here, slightly behind schedule, the cold holding longer at this elevation than the models had projected.

Jun was at the map table when I came in, which was where he was in the mornings. He looked up, looked at my face, and said: "Coffee first or talking first?"

"Talking," I said. "The coffee will get cold anyway."

We sat across the table and I laid it out: Liling's full account, the ninety minutes, the capacity's inheritance and its mechanism, the choice to come back, the Nagoya years, Miyake Fumiko, the thing I had not been able to tell him before because I had not yet understood it myself.

Jun listened in the way he listens. Completely. Without moving much. When I stopped talking he was quiet for a while, looking at the coffee cup.

"She studied in Japan," he said.

"Yes. Nagoya. 1963 to 1967. Miyake Fumiko was her supervisor."

He was quiet again with the working face — the specific stillness of active calculation. "Taiwan-Japan academic networks in that period. After the occupation." He set the cup down. "The institutional connection would have been complicated."

"She said it was the natural pathway for Taiwanese women in STEM at the time. Language access. Pre-war connections." I paused. "She wasn't sentimental about it. It was the available route, she took it. She learned what she needed to learn and moved past the parts of the training that didn't serve the work."

"Kenji was at Kyoto," Jun said.

"Yes."

"He trained under someone who believed in competition." The working quality in his voice — something being assembled. "She trained under someone who taught her to read a site. They met in the Garden and they had the same perception of the network from two different directions."

"That's exactly what she said. Different windows into the same room."

Jun looked at me with something I would not quite call surprise. "That phrase is in the 1972 paper," he said. "In the methodology section. I've read that paper six times. I always thought it was a translation artifact — a Mandarin idiom for the scientific concept. It was literally descriptive."

"Yes," I said.

He absorbed this. Then: "The supervisor. Miyake. Is she in any historical record? Could the coalition—"

"She died. Sometime in the eighties, from what Liling said. They corresponded until she died." I paused. "I don't know if there's a record. But you could ask Jing-wei. She's still working on the archival materials."

He nodded slowly. The motion of someone who has added a task to an ongoing list. Then he looked at me. "She trusted you."

"She trusted the pattern," I said. "She'd seen how the crossing works — the capacity, the spatial skill, the way it deposits you where the work is. She knew that someone with the capacity would cross into this zone and eventually find their way to her. She left the matrix for that person."

"She left it for you specifically."

"No. She left it for whoever came. I happened to be the one." I paused. "I happened to be her granddaughter. That was a coincidence of genetics and the maternal line and the fact that the capacity passed to me. The matrix would have been there for anyone who came."

Jun was quiet again. I could see him working through something.

Then he said: "Then you were always going to find me."

I recognized the shape of what was coming and I felt the resistance arrive before he finished the sentence.

"The crossing always leads to someone fighting for the forest," he said. "Maybe that's what it's for. Maybe the Garden is — maybe it's not random. Maybe it's an ecological mechanism, finding the most effective path between connected points. Liling crossed and she ended up exactly where the forest needed her. You crossed and you ended up exactly where the forest needed you. That's not coincidence, Mei. That's—"

"Determinism," I said.

He blinked. "I was going to say ecology."

"Ecology as determinism," I said. "The idea that the system's logic supersedes the individual's choice. That I was going to arrive here because the system needed me to arrive here."

"Isn't that what happened?"

"No." I was certain of this in a way I hadn't expected — the certainty arrived whole, without needing to be constructed. "That's not what happened and I need you to understand why it matters."

He sat back slightly. The expression of someone who is prepared to be argued with.

"The Garden exists," I said. "It is a place. It has properties. One of the properties is that people with the crossing capacity can traverse it between worlds. Another property, which you're describing, is that it tends to deposit crossers in locations where their specific skills have relevance. This may be true. I think it probably is true. But that property is a feature of the transit, not a directive." I paused, trying to find the precision. "I walked into the Garden in Vancouver because I found it and I was curious. I did not walk into it because the Garden needed me to. I walked into it because I found it. What happened afterward — arriving in your forest, developing the spatial analysis, finding the coalition — those were things I did. Choices I made with the agency available to me."

"Inside a system that was shaping your choices."

"All choices happen inside a system that shapes them," I said. "That doesn't make them not choices."

He was quiet.

"If I had walked into the Garden and arrived in your world and decided not to engage — decided to cross back immediately, or arrived and walked in the wrong direction, or arrived and been so frightened I couldn't function — the system would not have forced me to help. The system put me in the vicinity of the work. I decided to do the work." I looked at him. "Jun. If I were a mechanism of fate, you would not need to love me. You could simply receive what the mechanism provides."

He looked at me for a long time. Something moved through his expression — a complication of things.

"And if you are a person making choices," he said slowly, "then the help you've given us is — it's a gift. Not an inevitability. It's yours to give."

"Yes."

"And the seed bank. Liling's matrix. She didn't plant it because the system required her to. She planted it because she was a scientist who understood what the forest needed and she made a choice to leave something useful behind."

"Yes."

"That's more—" He stopped. "That's more moving than the determinism version. The determinism version is elegant but it doesn't have anyone in it. Just forces."

"Right."

He picked up the coffee cup and set it down again without drinking. "Then what are you choosing now?" he asked.

I had been waiting for this question. Not because I had a prepared answer — I hadn't, exactly — but because I had been circling toward the answer for three days in Vancouver and I knew approximately where it was.

"I'm choosing what I am," I said.

He looked at me with the expression of someone hearing something they don't yet understand but recognize as significant.

"I'm not choosing your world," I said. "I'm not choosing my world. I'm choosing to be what I am, which is someone who crosses. Who exists in the between as well as on either side. Who can carry what each world knows to the other." I paused. "Your grandmother's generation — in my world — they used to talk about code-switching. The way bilingual people move between languages without losing competence in either. The way diaspora people carry multiple cultural grammars simultaneously. It was characterized as a burden, sometimes. Or as a kind of homelessness. But it's not homelessness. It's a form of being that has its own intelligence. Its own logic. You can see things from the between that you cannot see from either side."

"What does that look like practically?" he said. Not dismissively — pragmatically. Jun wanting to understand the operational parameters.

"I don't know yet in full," I said honestly. "I know what I can do. I can carry data. I can carry analysis. I can carry knowledge between the two forests — your forest and mine, which are related forests facing related problems at different stages. I can model both and I can understand both and I can tell each what the other knows." I paused. "I can translate Liling's field notes in real time. I can explain her burial protocol and the science behind it. I can bring the coalition what she knew fifty years ago and what the science in my world has added since."

"That's today," he said.

"That's today. Tomorrow I don't know. But I'm not going to close the door on tomorrow by making a permanent declaration about which side of the door I'm on."

He was quiet for a long time. The monitoring station hummed its equipment hum. Outside, the early-morning light of Jun's world had the amber-and-haze quality I have stopped reading as ominous and started reading as simply *this* — the specific quality of light in a world that is fighting.

"I don't understand everything you're describing," he said finally. "I want to tell you that. I don't have a frame for what you're talking about — I have ecology and I have physics and I have the spatial modeling you've taught me to do, and none of those give me a framework for what it means to choose to be a bridge rather than choosing a side."

"I know."

"But I can see what you mean." He looked at me. "I can't say I like it. I can say I can see it."

"That's enough," I said. "For now, that's enough."

He nodded, slowly. Then he stood and picked up his jacket from the back of the chair. "Then we should go to the northern corridor," he said. "Two new nodes. We shouldn't make them wait."

"No," I said. "We shouldn't."

We went.

---

The northern corridor was a two-hour drive from the monitoring station — up through the logging roads that the coalition used for access, the roads that were technically closed to civilian traffic but that the coalition's legal team had established a right-of-way for through the conservation designation process. Jun drove. He drove the way he did most things: efficiently, with minimal comment, attending to the road rather than to the driving. The truck made its way through the secondary growth that bordered the logging roads and then, as the elevation gained, into the older growth that was what the coalition was fighting for.

I looked at the forest through the window.

I had been looking at this forest for six months, but looking at it as a problem: reading the collapse vectors, the beetle-front advance markers, the die-off patterns in the canopy that Sera had taught me to read from the truck window the way she read them from the air. I had been, for six months, reading the forest in diagnostic mode. The way a doctor reads a patient: looking for the symptoms, the indicators, the evidence of what is wrong.

Today I looked at it differently.

I looked at it as a site. As a place. As a piece of terrain that had its own spatial grammar, its own organization, its own way of moving you through it if you let it. The way the road curved into the upper elevation's trees and the quality of light changed — the canopy closing overhead, the light filtered and green-tinged, the specific temperature drop of old-growth interior. The way the understory opened and closed as the road passed through areas of different tree density. The way the forest told you, in its spatial language, what was happening in it: the die-off sections visible not just in the dead trees but in the change of light that came through where the canopy had thinned, the slightly warmer, drier air, the absence of the understory that couldn't survive the conditions change.

I had been reading this. I had been reading it in diagnostic mode. Now I was reading it as Kenji had read it — as a place to pay attention to, not only as a place to fix.

"What are you looking at?" Jun said.

I had not realized I was visibly looking. "The forest," I said.

He glanced at me and back at the road. "Something specific?"

"The way it talks," I said. "The spatial grammar. I've been reading it as damage assessment for six months. I'm trying to read it as — as what it is. Not only what it's losing."

He was quiet for a moment. "What does it say?"

I thought about this. "That it's still talking," I said. "That's the remarkable thing. At this level of fragmentation, at this stage of the beetle advance — the forest is still running its language. The mycorrhizal network is still operating in the sections that haven't been fully compromised. You can read it in the understory, in the way the indicator species are distributed." I paused. "Kenji could read that. Liling could read that. That's what the journals mean when he writes about her reading the moisture gradient. She was reading the network's language in the surface vegetation."

"And you."

"I'm learning it," I said. "I'm reading it imperfectly. But I'm learning it."

Jun drove. The road curved into the final approach to the monitoring site — the older section, the big trees, the area where the two new nodes had registered overnight. I could feel, in the quality of the light and the temperature and the forest's specific acoustic signature, the difference between this section and the damaged sections below.

"It's healthier here," I said.

"For now," he said. Not dismissive — factual. "The beetle front is eighteen months from this elevation at current rate of advance. Maybe twenty if the winter comes in cold."

"Eighteen months is enough," I said.

"For what?"

"For Priya's culture to deploy. For the ridge corridor to establish. For the northern network to build enough connectivity that the beetle-front has something to stop at." I paused. "If everything holds."

"Three conditionals," he said.

I had said this to him forty minutes ago, in the monitoring station, and he was using my phrasing back at me in the way that Jun uses your own words: not as mimicry, not to be agreeable, but because he has determined that the formulation was correct and is employing it as a tool.

"Three conditionals," I said.

We parked and walked to the monitoring sites. The two new nodes were in a section of the corridor I had mapped in my second week — a stretch where the substrate analysis had shown potential but where the inoculation had been delayed by the beetle-front's initial advance in the sector below. The nodes were new growth, fragile, but the monitoring data was reading as genuine connectivity rather than isolated activity.

I crouched next to the first monitoring post and looked at the data on the tablet. The connectivity reading was exactly what Priya had described as the baseline for established nodes, with a small upward variation that she would characterize as promising.

"It's working," I said.

"It's working," Jun confirmed. He was crouched next to me, reading the same data, with the specific quality of attention he gave to things that were providing evidence in the direction he needed evidence to go — not relief, not celebration, but the very careful look of someone who does not want to read too much into what is there.

"What do you want it to say?" I asked.

He glanced at me. "What?"

"What do you want these nodes to say? Not what they do say — what do you want them to say?"

He sat back on his heels and looked at the monitoring post and then at the forest around it. The trees doing their patient work. The understory's indicator species in their positions.

"I want them to say the network can be rebuilt," he said. "Not restored — rebuilt. From the matrix Liling left and the culture Priya is running and the nodes the coalition has been establishing for three years. I want them to say that a network that has been destroyed can reconstruct itself from the preserved pieces, given enough time and enough intervention and enough luck." He paused. "I want them to say that the work we've been doing is the right work."

"And?"

"And they're saying something in that direction," he said. "Something. I'm not going to claim more than what's there."

We drove back in the same kind of silence we had driven up in — the silence of people who have been in a significant place together and are not yet ready to reduce it to words. The forest passing outside the windows. The amber-haze quality of Jun's world's light, doing what it did at midday: making the surviving canopy look almost ordinary, almost like a forest that was not in its worst period, almost like the thing it had been before and might, if everything held, approach again.
