### Chapter Twenty-Three · The Wrong Question

I want to tell the Tribunal something before I describe what happened in the Garden.

There is a pre-linguistic stage to any testimony — the moment before the argument is ready to speak, when it is still assembling itself from all the pieces gathered. I was in that stage at the kitchen table. I was building the spatial model of the argument the way I built the spatial model of any project: finding the geometry that made the evidence legible, the arrangement that turned data into form. A landscape architect's preparation. The professional habit, turned on its own history.

By the time I crossed to the Garden, the model was ready. What I want to tell the Tribunal is what I found there.

I want to tell you that there are things I am not going to disclose in this testimony. This is not evasion. I want to be precise about the distinction.

Evasion is the withholding of information that is relevant to the body asking for it, for the purpose of protecting oneself from consequences. What I am doing is different: I am withholding information that would cause damage in the act of being disclosed — not to me, but to the thing itself. There are things that cannot yet be examined without damage. The examination would not reveal the thing; it would disturb it. I have chosen not to disturb it.

I'll tell you what I choose to tell you, and I'll tell you why, and you will have to decide what to do with the parts I keep.

This is the only honest approach I have available.

---

I went to the Lantern Garden on a Tuesday morning, four days after the visit to the care facility.

I had crossed from Vancouver — the city, the park, the ordinary territory of a Tuesday morning walk that ended, as my Tuesday morning walks had been ending for months, at the specific location in the park where the Garden could be found by someone who knew how to find it. I had arrived in Jun's world and I had done a day's work with the coalition — the substrate model update, a call with Priya about the culture conditions, a review of the alternate eastern corridors with Jun and Sera — and I had crossed back in the evening. And then I had slept, and woken, and on Tuesday morning I went back to the Garden.

Not through. Not past.

To.

I want to be precise about this. I have been treating the Garden as a transit point. For the months of crossings, it had been the place I passed through — the interval, the between, the three minutes of liminal space that separated one world from the other. I had developed the habits of transit: passing through with intention, not dawdling, not examining the Garden itself because the Garden was not the destination.

On Tuesday I went to the Garden and I sat down in it.

I want to describe it accurately.

The plants are Physalis-like — lantern-fruited, with the inflated calyx that in my world is orange and papery, but in the Garden is translucent and faintly luminous, as though whatever light the Garden contains is choosing to make itself visible here, in this particular form. The plants grow in loose clusters rather than rows, following what I would call — speaking professionally, speaking as a landscape architect — a naturalistic distribution: not planted, not random, distributed in the specific way of things that have found their positions over time in response to the conditions of the site. The way ferns colonize a hillside. The way moss establishes in the wet corners of a wall.

The light in the Garden is its own. Not the light of my world — not Vancouver's gray-wet quality — and not the light of Jun's world — not that amber-haze. The Garden's light is without color temperature, without the directionality of an external source. It comes from everywhere and is not quite daytime and not quite dusk. I have tried to describe this to the Tribunal and I have found that words for light are inadequate: we borrow from emotion and from physical sensation and from other lights, and none of the borrowings are accurate. It is the light of the Garden. I don't have a better term.

I sat in it.

I had been in the Garden hundreds of times — crossing and crossing, arriving and departing. I had never sat in it. The sitting was strange at first in the way that familiarity in a new mode is always strange: I knew this place and I was experiencing it differently. The difference was the difference between walking through a room and sitting down in it. Between using a space and being in it.

The lantern-plants were not glowing, not in the luminescent way that Liling's phrase had implied. They were faintly luminous, as I have described. Not the full glowing. I registered this and did not try to force it.

I did the thing I do in spaces I am trying to understand: I mapped.

Not with instruments — I don't carry instruments in the Garden, which is a transit-thinking habit I am now revising. With observation. With the spatial sense that is, as Liling said, the organ. I sat and I attended to the Garden the way I would attend to any site I was trying to understand: the distribution of plants, the quality of light, the sense of the space's dimensions, the relationship between the elements.

What I found:

The Garden is larger than I had registered. In transit mode, the Garden is experienced as a corridor: you enter at the point that corresponds to my world's park coordinates, you traverse, you exit at the point that corresponds to Jun's world's forest edge. The corridor is maybe three minutes of experienced time — maybe longer, the time is unreliable there — and it is experienced as a path. What I found, sitting, is that the Garden is not a path. The path is one feature of a larger space.

How much larger I cannot say. The edges of the Garden, when I attended to them, did not resolve. This is not the same as not having edges — I am not claiming the Garden is infinite. I am saying that the edges were not visible from where I was sitting, in the way that the edges of a forest are not visible from the forest's interior.

This is what I had been misunderstanding.

I had been thinking of the Garden as a threshold: the place between the door and the next door. A functional space, defined by what it connects rather than by what it is. But a threshold is a human construct. It is what you make of a piece of architecture when you are oriented toward what is on the other side of it. The door itself is not a threshold. The door is a door: a thing in its own right, with its own properties, which you can open or close or stand in front of or lean against.

The Garden is the door.

Not the threshold. The door.

And I had been walking through it so many times, in such a hurry to arrive at one world or the other, that I had failed to see what the door itself was.

I sat for a long time. I do not know how long — time in the Garden has never corresponded exactly to clock time in either world, and sitting rather than transiting changes the relationship further. My notebook was in my lap. I had not opened it. I was attending to the space, which does not require instruments or documentation — attending is its own methodology.

What I understood, slowly, in the way that some understandings arrive: I had been asking the wrong question.

The question I had been asking, for months, in every form I could find: *which world is mine?* This is the question that everyone around me had been implicitly or explicitly asking as well: Jun, in his *are you staying*; my mother, in her careful not-asking; the Tribunal, in its jurisdictional interest in my *place of residence*. The question assumes that a person is native to a location. That identity is a matter of where you are from.

But I am from a crossing. I am from the between-place.

Not in a mystical sense. In the same sense that a bilingual person is from both languages and neither: not homeless, but differently homed. The crossing is not a space between two homes. The crossing is its own home. I have been living in it for months without recognizing it as home because I was oriented — always — toward what I was going to arrive at next.

Liling had tried to tell me this. *I never left. Not really. And neither will you. That's not weakness, little one. That's what we are.* I had heard it as comfort: the kind thing you say to someone who is struggling with a difficult condition. Now I understood it as description. Empirical. She was not consoling me. She was informing me of a fact.

What we are: people who are from the crossing itself. People whose home is the liminal space. People who exist most fully not at either end of the transit but in the transit itself — in the between, where the two worlds' knowledges can be held simultaneously, where the spatial intelligence has access to both forests at once, where the temporal layers are all somehow concurrent.

I had been suspending myself between two worlds and feeling the suspension as a problem. The suspension was the thing.

I am not a bridge *between* anything. I am the bridge. I am the crossing. I am the Garden.

This is not metaphor. Or it is metaphor in the way that the best ecological descriptions are metaphor: accurate, precise, more true than the literal statement would be.

I opened my notebook.

I had been carrying, since the day I had understood that the Garden was something to be mapped rather than transited, the beginning of a site analysis. A landscape architect's analysis of a site. The professional habit: observe, record, understand the space on its own terms before you try to determine its function.

I began to write.

The distribution of the lantern-plants: naturalistic, self-organized, responsive to conditions I could not fully observe but could infer from the distribution. The quality of substrate — I pressed my hand into the ground and felt it: not earth, exactly, not in the geological sense, but something with the properties of earth. Something that holds and gives and is not nothing. The acoustic quality of the space — not silent, not ambient in the way of either world, but with its own sound that I had been filtering out in transit mode and could now attend to. Something at the edge of hearing. Not threatening. Persistent.

I wrote for what felt like hours. I documented the Garden with the same attention I would give a client site: the micro-topography, the light quality at different angles, the way the lantern-plants created nodes of visual attention in the space. The two entry and exit points — my world and Jun's world — which I could locate by the slight difference in the Garden's quality at those coordinates, a difference of orientation rather than appearance.

I thought about Kenji's journal entry: *I have been in this Garden three dozen times and it is still strange every time — the specific quality of an in-between space that is genuinely between.* He had written that on the day Liling crossed back for the last time. He had stood in the Garden after she had gone and looked at the threshold light and then gone back to the north ridge the next morning.

He had understood the Garden as between. He had not, from what the journals recorded, understood it as a place in its own right. He had the same transit-thinking I had been living in, perhaps because he had always been the one who stayed — the one crossing in only one direction, the one for whom the Garden was always a door to somewhere else rather than a place to inhabit.

Liling had crossed in both directions. She might have been the one to understand. But she had come back and built her professional life on the understanding she'd brought with her, and the dementia had taken the language before I could ask her the right questions.

She had left me the organ instead. The spatial intelligence. The capacity to read a space on its own terms.

I wrote: *The crossing is not a disruption of a coherent self. The crossing is the coherent self operating at full capacity.*

I wrote: *I am a spatial reader. The Garden is a spatial phenomenon. The crossing is what a spatial reader does when she encounters the Garden. This is not fate. This is ecology. The right instrument for the right conditions.*

I wrote: *I have been asking where I live. I live here. I have always lived here. The crossings are not expeditions from a home to a foreign territory; they are the full range of the home itself. I live in a home that extends across two worlds and through the between.*

I wrote: *Liling knew this. She found language for it that I am finding now. The Garden doesn't direct you. It puts you where the work is. If you're paying attention, you find the others who are paying attention.*

I wrote: *Jun is paying attention. I am paying attention. Liling paid attention for fifty years and left what she knew in the ground for the next person who would know to look.*

Then I wrote: *The next person is me. The next person after me is someone I don't know yet. The Garden is larger than I can see from the interior.*

I sat with the notebook in my lap.

The lantern-plants around me did something I had not expected. It was not sudden — it was gradual, the way light changes in the late afternoon, the way you notice it only when it has already shifted. The translucent calyxes, which had been faintly luminous, became more so. Not dramatically. Not the fairy-tale glowing that Liling's phrase had implied. But a deepening of the quality, a brightening of what was already there, as though the plants were responding to something — to sustained attention, perhaps, or to the quality of presence that comes from sitting rather than transiting.

*The Garden is beautiful when it glows. I haven't seen it glow in a long time. You will.*

I had not done this before — had not sat still in the Garden long enough to be present in it rather than passing through it. And the Garden was doing what a garden does when someone actually arrives in it rather than uses it as a path to somewhere else: it was being itself. It was being visible.

I sat in the growing luminescence and I did not move, because moving felt like the wrong response. I sat and I let the Garden show me what it was showing.

What it was showing: this.

The between-place, in its own right. Not the space between my world and Jun's world. The space that both worlds touch. The space that is neither and is both and is itself. The space that a person with the crossing capacity inhabits when she is at full capacity — not in my world, not in Jun's world, but here, in the garden of lantern-lights, in the home that is not a location.

My notebook. My hand on the page. The spatial model I had been building in the corner of my consciousness since I understood the Garden was something to be understood. The specific quality of the light, which was everything and nothing, which was the light of a place that exists in the gap between the described and the describable.

I was here.

This was the correct answer to the question. Not *her world* and not *his world*. Here, in the suspension itself, which was not suspension: it was the most precise location I had ever occupied.

I closed the notebook. I sat for a while longer in the luminescence.

Then I did what I would do in any site I have come to understand: I began to consider what might be built here. Not with timber and concrete — with attention, with repeated presence, with the kind of sustained inhabitation that transforms a space from something observed into something known. The Garden was not finished being what it was. I was not finished learning what it was.

I knew enough, now, to know that I would keep coming back.

Not because the crossing required it. Because this was my home, and a person returns to their home.

I rose. I stood in the Garden for a moment, looking at the lantern-plants in their luminescence, which was — I will say this for the record, to the Tribunal, knowing they will not know what to do with it — beautiful in the way that a thing is beautiful when you understand it. Not the beauty of what is finished or resolved. The beauty of what is exactly and precisely itself.

Then I crossed back.

The garden faded as I moved. Or rather: I moved and the Garden did not fade; I simply moved away from it, and it was still there, as it always is, doing what it does, waiting for the next person who knows to look.

It is a judgment of the speed at which institutional bodies move relative to the speed at which forests die.*

*My relationship with Jun: I have characterized it as collegial. This is partially true. The full truth is private and belongs to the two of us and is not relevant to the Tribunal's jurisdictional mandate.*

*What my grandmother said in the final minutes of her last lucid window: I have not told the Tribunal this. I will not. It belongs to the family.*

*My place of residence: I will answer this question when the chair asks it. I am preparing my answer. It is not an evasion. It is the truth, stated as precisely as I can state it in language that the Tribunal's forms were not designed to receive.*

*I am here. In the between. That is the correct answer.*

*That is the answer I arrived at.*
