### Chapter Seven: The Photograph

I had been carrying the photograph for two weeks.

Not the original — that was on my grandmother's bedside table, and I would not have taken it even if I'd had a reason to, because the photograph was hers and the bedside table was hers and the act of taking it would have been a kind of presumption I wasn't prepared to make, an assertion of priority over something whose ownership I didn't yet understand.

I had the phone photograph of the photograph. I had been, in the two weeks since I'd taken it, not looking at it directly — opening the file and closing it without full attention, looking at the edges of it rather than the center, treating it the way you treat something you're not quite ready to see.

I was in my Vancouver apartment on a Sunday morning when I looked at it properly.

I did not premeditate this. I was at the kitchen table with my second cup of tea, and my phone was in my hand, and I opened the file, and this time I did not close it.

The photograph was, by the standards of photographic quality, an ordinary image taken by an amateur camera at some point in the past — the color had the warmth of analog, which put it in the era of film rather than digital, which put it, I calculated, at least twenty years back and possibly much further. The image was not sharp at the edges. The center was clearer. And the center showed a garden.

I looked at the center.

The lantern-plants were ground-level, fruiting structures with the pendant shape I'd spent six months familiarizing myself with — the specific droop of the physalis-like fruit, the way the papery calyx enclosed the globe. I'd learned to find them in the low light of the Garden, where they emitted the bioluminescence that gave them their character. In the photograph, they were not bioluminescent in any way that photography could have captured, but the shape was unmistakable. The shape was exactly what I'd been walking past for six months, crouching beside, learning the way you learn something you're spending time with.

Behind the lantern-plants: larger growth, the suggestion of canopy, the specific quality of light that was not quite any light I knew how to name by reference to time of day or weather pattern. The in-between light. I'd stopped trying to describe it in the notes I kept about the crossing and had taken to calling it, in my own shorthand, *threshold light.* The light that existed at a liminal point.

This was threshold light. In a photograph that was at least twenty years old.

I looked at the edges of the image. The left edge was the garden itself, the lantern-plants continuing out of frame. The right edge showed trees, the suggestion of larger growth, forest. Not old-growth; the scale was different. But forest.

And at the bottom-right of the frame, just inside the photograph rather than outside it, there was a figure.

A young woman. She was not facing the camera. She was facing into the garden — standing at its edge, looking in, in the specific posture of someone who is about to step into something rather than the posture of someone who has arrived at a place and stopped. She was wearing clothes that were consistent with the 1970s. Her hair was dark and in a practical arrangement, pinned back. She was not tall.

I could not see her face. The young woman was facing away.

But I had, on my phone, a photograph from the dresser in my grandmother's room. A professional portrait, circa fifty years old — my grandmother at approximately forty, holding a small potted plant. The height. The hair. The specific way a person carried their posture, the particular arrangement of a body in space that was as individual as a fingerprint and that I, who read space the way other people read faces, could identify across fifty years and a turned back and a photograph of a photograph.

I sat with this for a long time.

The tea went cold. The Sunday morning continued on the other side of the bad windows — the alley, the neighbor's cat, the ordinary proceeding world. I was aware of these things with the peripheral awareness of someone whose main attention is elsewhere. Inside the apartment, in the specific quality of a Sunday morning that was asking nothing of me, I held the photograph of the photograph and let myself look at it without the deflections I'd been using for two weeks.

I am a landscape architect. I read space. I read a site not only by what is on its surface but by what the surface implies about what was below it — the hidden drainage, the buried history, the structural logic that produced the visible form. I read a terrain by its relationships: what was here because of what was there, what the shape of a ridge told you about the water that had moved through it, what the pattern of vegetation told you about the soil that was invisible beneath it.

I was looking at a photograph of a garden I knew. Not from photographs — from being there. From standing in it, from walking through it, from crossing through it into another world. I knew the way the lantern-plants arranged themselves, knew the specific height of the fruiting structures in relation to the understory behind them, knew the quality of the light that gathered in the space between the plants and the treeline. I knew this place the way I knew sites I had studied for months, because I had studied it for months, though my study had been the study of transit rather than attention.

And in the photograph: the same place. Fifty years ago. With a woman who was the same height and the same build and carried her posture the same way as the grandmother sleeping in a care facility in New Taipei.

The arithmetic was not complicated.

My grandmother had been in the Lantern Garden.

My grandmother had stood at its edge, in the early 1970s, looking in with the posture of someone about to cross — someone who had found the Garden and understood what it was and was in the process of stepping into it for the first time, or perhaps the second, or perhaps the third. Standing at the threshold. Facing the light.

And on the bedside table of a woman with advancing dementia, in a care facility in New Taipei, the photograph had survived the fifty years between then and now. As though it were kept. As though it were not something to be filed in an album or left in a box in a storage unit but something to be close to, something to see from where you lay.

I thought about shoes.

*You mustn't leave your shoes there.*

I thought about the word *crossing.* I thought about the smell that my grandmother had identified on me — the smell of the Garden, of the between-place, carried back on my skin or my clothes or in some register that was not olfactory but that my grandmother's body, which had crossed the same Garden fifty years before, recognized.

I thought about Kenji.

I thought about the three-year gap in the institutional record — the Canadian visa renewed three times, the conference registration with no record of presentation, the missing-persons report that I didn't know yet had been filed but whose shape I could feel in the outline of what my mother had told me. Three years. Three years that my grandmother had simply — been elsewhere.

I thought about seeds. About what a botanist would bring back from three years in another world. About what a mycorrhizal scientist, twenty years ahead of her field, would recognize in a forest ecosystem facing a collapse that hadn't yet arrived in her own world.

I thought about what you could do with fifty years of preparation, if you were a scientist who knew what was coming and understood that knowing was not the same as being helpless.

I set down my phone.

I looked at the bad windows, the alley, the ordinary world.

Then I picked up my phone and opened my calendar and cancelled the client meeting scheduled for Thursday, citing a family matter in Taipei, which was not untrue. I sent the apology, brief and professional. I did not overthink the wording.

Then I booked a flight.

Not to Taipei — I was going to Taipei next week for work in any case. Not to anywhere new. I booked a flight that established an itinerary, a paper trail, a record of a person who moved through one world and whose movements were accountable. I was doing what my grandmother had done for three years, I understood: building the record of someone who was somewhere specific. Maintaining the story.

It occurred to me, sitting at the kitchen table with the cold tea and the phone photograph and the Sunday morning making its indifferent way through the bad windows, that I had not yet begun to understand what it meant that my grandmother had done this before me. That I was not the first. That the Garden had been there for fifty years, in the same coordinates, having its nature — being what it was — waiting for whoever came to it with enough attention to find their way through.

I thought about what it meant to find something that was already there. Not discovered — the Garden was not something I'd created by finding it. It had been there. My grandmother had been there. Whatever my grandmother had left there — in the soil of Jun's world, in some specific location that I did not yet have coordinates for — had been there for fifty years, waiting.

I was not the beginning of this story.

I was not even its second chapter. I was the place where the story became visible again, the way a buried creek becomes visible when the surface conditions change, when enough of the camouflage has eroded to let the actual shape of things through. The story had been running beneath the surface of my family for fifty years. My grandmother's silence had been its containment. My mother's unasked questions had been the pressure it built. And I, who read landscapes for a living, who understood that what was visible was always a product of what was not — I had simply been the person with the capacity to see.

I thought about the word *inheritance.*

I had always understood myself as my grandmother's intellectual descendant — the ecologically-trained designer who'd gotten there via a different route than botany but had ended up working on the same questions. I had understood the professional lineage without understanding what it was built on. You could not build a lineage of mycorrhizal knowledge across fifty years without that knowledge coming from somewhere. I had assumed it came from the same places knowledge always came from: study, observation, rigorous attention to what was in front of you.

But my grandmother had also been in a world where the forest's crisis was fifty years further along. Had seen what was coming. Had done what scientists do when they see what is coming and have the tools to respond: she had responded.

I looked at the photograph.

I looked at the specific angle of the young woman's body — the slight lean forward, the weight on the front foot. It was the posture of someone who had made a decision and was in the process of acting on it. Not hesitation. Not pause. The beginning of motion, captured at the threshold.

My grandmother, at the edge of the Garden, in the early 1970s, stepping in.

I thought about what it meant that the photograph had been kept close for fifty years. Not archived. Not forgotten. Kept on a bedside table in a care facility, visible from the bed where my grandmother slept, there in the hours when she was looking at things that were far away and not visible to anyone else in the room.

I looked at the photograph one more time. The young woman at the edge. Facing the light.

"I knew the photograph was significant," I said aloud, to the apartment. To myself. To the version of myself that would be in this room on some future day, having arrived at the understanding that this photograph had started, and wanting to find a line back to the moment before I had it.

I did not know, yet, what the photograph was.

I would know later — would know, in the full way that knowledge settles into certainty and certainty into action — that the photograph was a door. Not a metaphor for a door. Not a way of understanding the door. The door itself, held at a specific angle, showing the light on the other side.

I didn't know this yet.

I knew the photograph was significant.

I knew my grandmother had crossed before me.

I knew that what I'd been calling a situation I needed to resolve was something older and more specific than that: a shape that ran in the maternal line, that my grandmother had lived inside, that had produced seeds and silence and a child and fifty years of botanical work that was twenty years ahead of its time, and that was now producing me — who was the next in a sequence I hadn't known I was part of.

I went to the Lantern Garden in the late afternoon, as the light changed, as the threshold light became something I could step into.

I stood at the edge of it — the way my grandmother had stood in the photograph, I now understood, the way anyone stands at the edge of something they are about to enter for reasons that are not fully resolved but are sufficient.

I crossed.
