# What the Living Say

<!-- Chapter 6: REVISED per editor-emma review, 2026-05-10 -->

The first thing she noticed in the Zhonghe facility was laundry starch — laid over the antiseptic underneath, and over that something floral she could never quite name. The corridor that led to Liling's wing had the quality of morning light diffused through curtained windows: not warm, not harsh, a flat institutional yellow that flattened the walls and made distances hard to read. She walked it measuring, the way she measured every corridor the second time.

She had not planned to go to Taipei.

The work presentation was scheduled — a client review for the green roof project, which had achieved a fragile and provisional agreement on four of the five towers, which she was treating as a victory rather than a concession, which required some discipline. The client was a development company with offices in both Vancouver and Taipei, which was why she'd been on the plane to begin with: a site walk in the afternoon, a client dinner she couldn't avoid.

The care facility was a twenty-minute taxi ride from the hotel. She had, that morning, no particular plan to visit. She had a work obligation in the afternoon, and before that she'd planned to get food and answer emails and sit in a café near the hotel and try to feel present in her own world, which had been requiring increasing effort since she'd come back from the ranger station.

At eleven o'clock, without having consciously decided, she was in the taxi.

Fang-ling met her at reception with a slight elevation in attention — the professional signal of a care facility worker preparing a family member for a difficult visit. "She's had a harder morning," Fang-ling said. "More confusion than usual. But she was asking for you about an hour ago."

"Asking for me."

"By name, very clearly. She's clearer about you than she is about most things right now."

Her grandmother was not looking at the window when Mei came in. She was looking at the door, as though she'd been watching for it to open.

"Mei-mei," she said.

"Nǎi Nai. I wasn't sure you'd be expecting me."

"I knew you were coming." This was said with perfect matter-of-fact certainty, which could mean many things in her grandmother's current state — could be confabulation, pattern recognition, coincidence retrofitted as prediction, or something else. Mei had learned not to challenge these certainties. "Sit down."

She sat.

Her grandmother was, this morning, visibly more confused than the previous visit — her eyes had the unfocused quality of someone managing a great deal of internal noise, the look of someone trying to hear a conversation in a loud room. She asked Mei twice for her name, which she had not done on previous visits, and each time Mei told her, she nodded with the relief of someone who needed the anchor.

They sat for a while. Mei talked — about the taxi, about the hotel, about the client meeting. She described the hotel room, the view from it, the particular color of the Taipei morning. She gave her grandmother the world in small specifics, which was the thing she'd found worked best: not abstractions, not emotional content, but the precise texture of ordinary experience.

Then — between one breath and the next, without warning and with the quality she'd come to recognize as window — her grandmother's hand came across the space between them and closed around Mei's wrist.

Hard. Not rough, but definite. The grip of someone who needed to make sure the connection was real.

Her grandmother's eyes were focused. Fully, precisely focused, with the look that was different from ordinary lucidity — the look that carried something behind it, something that had been in position and was now ready.

"You smell like it," Liling said.

Mei's breath stopped. Her first instinct was to pull her wrist back — not from alarm but the particular reflex of someone who has learned, in rooms like this one, that you do not follow every thread. You hold still. You wait for it to pass. She let the instinct rise and did not act on it; she kept her hand where it was. That was its own kind of choice.

"Like the crossing." Her grandmother's grip on her wrist was steady. "You've been there recently."

"Nǎi Nai —"

"Don't." Not unkind. Firm. "I know what it smells like. I know what it does to you — the tiredness, the way you hold your shoulders when you've just come back. I've been watching you for three visits." She paused. "I've been waiting for you to say something. You weren't going to say something."

"I didn't know —" She stopped. She hadn't known what her grandmother knew. She hadn't known there was anything to know.

"I know," Liling said. "You didn't know. That's my fault." Her grip didn't loosen. "I should have told your mother. I kept thinking I'd tell her when she was old enough, and then the years — you know how years do."

Mei's hand in her grandmother's grip. The fluorescent light of the room. Outside, the courtyard with its small maple and its ferns.

"Tell me," Mei said.

Her grandmother opened her mouth. Something shifted in her face — the window was still open, she was still there, but there was a weather change inside the focus, something moving through. Her posture changed: the shoulders dropped by a fraction, the chin lifted to an angle that was different from the confused triangulating of the usual visits — an angle that was precise, directed, the posture of a woman who had spent decades looking at field sites and had found one she recognized. Her eyes in this mode were not the searching eyes of someone trying to orient. They were the eyes of someone who had already oriented and knew exactly where they were standing.

"Kenji," Liling said. "You have to —"

And then the window closed.

It was not gradual. One moment her grandmother was present — the grip on her wrist was present, the focused gaze was present, the weight of what was about to be said was present. And then it was as though a light had been switched. The grip loosened to an old woman's ordinary clasp. The eyes drifted. She looked around the room with mild confusion, as though she'd been asleep and was determining where she was.

"I should have lunch soon," Liling said. "Is it lunchtime?"

"Almost," Mei said.

She stayed another half hour, talking about nothing consequential. She held her grandmother's hand. She watched the courtyard maple through the window and told herself she was fine, that she was not shaking, that the specific electrification in her sternum was just the surprise of it, just the sudden presence of a thing she hadn't expected to find.

Her grandmother slept for a few minutes — brief, shallow, the sleep of someone whose cognitive effort was high and whose body took rests where it could. When she woke, she looked at Mei with the particular expression of someone who has surfaced and is triangulating.

"You're still here," Liling said.

"I'm still here, Nǎi Nai."

"Good." A pause. "I don't always know who comes and goes. The days are — there are days when time doesn't stay in order." She said this with a certain dignity, the factual dignity of someone describing a condition they have made a professional assessment of. Even now she was a scientist about her own dissolution. "But I know when you're in the room."

"How do you know?"

Her grandmother looked at her. "You pay attention the way I do. It takes up space." She closed her eyes again, then opened them. "Your mother doesn't do it. She pays attention but it's a different kind — it's a worried kind. Yours is curious. Even when you're worried, you're curious first." A faint smile. "I always liked that about you."

Mei did not trust her voice to do anything particular, so she said nothing. She held her grandmother's hand, which was warm and dry, the calluses of decades under her fingers.

After a few minutes Liling said, to the window: "The light is different there. I've been trying to describe it to the nurses but I don't have good language for it anymore. I used to have good language for everything." A pause. "I planted something there. I want you to know that. I planted something."

"In the courtyard?"

"No." Her grandmother's eyes stayed on the window. "In the place with the light. The place where the garden is." She blinked. "Someone should know."

Mei looked at her grandmother's profile — the strong bones of it, the particular angle that had always reminded her, when she was a child, of a kind of authority she aspired to without being able to name. The bones of a woman who had made decisions and acted on them and carried the consequences with the same matter-of-fact discipline she applied to her research.

"I know, Nǎi Nai," she said. Though she didn't yet. Though what she meant was: I will know.

Her grandmother nodded once, satisfied, and drifted back toward sleep.

She left at twelve-fifteen and sat on a bench in the street outside the care facility, in the May heat, and called her mother.

"I visited Nǎi Nai," she said when Huimei answered.

"I know, Fang-ling texted me. How was she?"

"She was — confused this morning. But she had a clear spell." She paused. "Mā, she mentioned someone named Kenji."

A silence.

"She often asks for Kenji," Huimei said. "It's one of her recurring things. We've asked the staff about it — they say she mentions it fairly regularly. We don't know who it is." Her voice had the specific quality of someone presenting official information and not fully inhabiting it. "Some old friend, probably. A colleague. You know how it is — the mind in that state reaches for old attachments, people from a long time ago."

"Has she ever said anything else about Kenji? Any context?"

Another pause. "No. Just the name. Sometimes she says it like she's calling for someone. Sometimes she just says it quietly, like —" Her mother stopped. "Like she's remembering something pleasant."

Mei looked at the street. A scooter passing. A woman walking with a child. The ordinary specific life of a city going about its ordinary specific business, none of it aware of anything that wasn't itself.

"Mā. Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"When Nǎi Nai came back from Canada — in the 1970s. What was she like? Do you remember?"

"I was very young."

"I know. But do you remember anything? Anything she said about the trip? Why she went, why she came back?"

Huimei was quiet for so long that Mei checked the phone to make sure the call was still connected.

"She didn't talk about it," Huimei said finally. "She came back and she was — she was the same, I think. But quieter in some way. More careful. More... present. But not quite here." Another pause. "Your grandfather said once that she'd come back different. He didn't say different-bad. Just different."

"Was she pregnant when she came back?"

The question left her mouth before she had fully decided to ask it — the thing her mind had been building toward without her permission, the arithmetic that had been running beneath the conversation.

Silence.

"I was born in 1976," Huimei said. Her voice was precise and careful in a way that was different from her ordinary precision. "Nine months after she returned."

Neither of them spoke.

The scooter was long gone. The woman with the child had turned the corner. The street was briefly empty of everything except the heat and the distant sound of traffic on the larger road.

"She brought things back," Huimei said, finally, as though she were continuing from a different place. "Plants. Seeds. She always had seeds."

"Seeds," Mei said.

"She was a botanist. She was always bringing things from her fieldwork. I didn't think anything of it."

Mei sat with the sun on her face. She thought about a mycorrhizal scientist who had been twenty years ahead of her field. She thought about what you would bring back, across a crossing, if you were a botanist who understood what a forest needed before the forest needed it.

"Okay," she said. "Thank you, Mā."

"Is something wrong?"

"No. No, I was just — I've been thinking about her a lot. About her work."

"Her work was remarkable. She was very far ahead of her field, her colleagues used to say." Her mother paused, and Mei heard the specific quality in the pause that meant she was deciding whether to continue. "She had a paper — I found it once, when I was going through her things before the move to the facility. A very early paper, from the late sixties. About underground communication in forests. She was writing about mycorrhizal signaling as if it were established science. But the science didn't catch up to her until the 1990s."

"I know. I've read some of her work."

"Have you?" Her mother sounded surprised. "When?"

"Recently. I've been interested in the mycorrhizal work." This was true and not the whole truth. "The forestry project I've been consulting on — it's relevant."

"She'd have liked knowing that," Huimei said. "That her work was useful. She used to say it was the most frustrating thing — to know something clearly and have no one else see it yet." Her mother's voice had shifted slightly, the way it shifted when she was remembering something she'd been carrying without being certain she should put it down. "She was a difficult person sometimes. Very certain. Not patient with people who didn't keep up. But the certainty — I think now it was because she actually knew. She wasn't guessing."

Mei thought about this for a moment. She thought about the precision of her grandmother's hands. The calluses. The way she'd identified the maple variety in the courtyard with the casual authority of someone stating a fact they'd known for sixty years.

"She said she planted something," Mei said. "When she was confused. She said she planted something somewhere, and she wanted someone to know."

A silence.

"She says things like that," Huimei said finally. "In the confused periods. She has — there are themes that come back. The garden. The planting. Kenji." A pause. "I've been — I try not to interpret it too literally. The doctors say the content of dementia speech often has emotional significance without literal meaning."

"Yes," Mei said.

"But sometimes I wonder." Her mother's voice was careful. "Sometimes I wonder if there are things she never told us. Things she — held." A pause. "She held things. She always did. Even before."

The bench was warm from the sun. A group of schoolchildren passed on the pavement, in uniform, led by a teacher who was counting heads with the practiced efficiency of someone who has been counting heads for twenty years. Ordinary life, going about its ordinary purposes.

"Mā," Mei said. "If I find out something — about that time, about where she was — would you want to know?"

A long pause.

"Yes," her mother said. Then, more quietly: "I think I've always wanted to know. I just didn't know it was something I could find out."

"Okay," Mei said.

She went to the client meeting. She was professionally present. She said the right things about the green roofs, addressed the cost concerns with the patience they required, left the meeting with a signed scope extension. She did all of this with a portion of her mind and spent the rest of it sitting with the word *Kenji*, with the word *seeds*, with the arithmetic of a woman who had returned from somewhere in 1975 and given birth nine months later to a child who would grow up to have a daughter who would, fifty years later, find the same Garden.

At the client dinner she answered questions about urban canopy projections and ate fish and drank one glass of wine and smiled at the right moments and was professionally charming in the way she'd been professionally charming for eight years, which required no more than thirty percent of her available attention. She spent the other seventy percent in a different room entirely: a room that was a care facility in Zhonghe District, smelling of gardenias and institutional cleanliness, where her grandmother had said *you smell like it,* and *I planted something,* and *Kenji,* and then been gone.

Later, at the hotel, she looked at the photograph on her phone again. The garden. The lantern-plants. The quality of light she'd been refusing to name.

She named it, now, to herself. She said it aloud, in the hotel room, to no one.

Then she set the phone face-down on the nightstand, and let the thing she now knew sit beside her in the Taipei evening like a companion she hadn't known she was waiting for.

In the morning she was professional and the client meeting was productive and the flight home was on time.
