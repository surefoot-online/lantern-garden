### Chapter Six: What the Living Say

I had not planned to go to Taipei.

The work presentation was scheduled — a client review for the green roof project, which had achieved a fragile and provisional agreement on four of the five towers, which I was treating as a victory rather than a concession, which required some discipline. The client was a development company with offices in both Vancouver and Taipei, which was why I'd been on the plane to begin with: a site walk in the afternoon, a client dinner I couldn't avoid.

The care facility was a twenty-minute taxi ride from the hotel. I had, that morning, no particular plan to visit. I had a work obligation in the afternoon, and before that I'd planned to get food and answer emails and sit in a café near the hotel and try to feel present in my own world, which had been requiring increasing effort since I'd come back from the ranger station.

At eleven o'clock, without having consciously decided, I was in the taxi.

Fang-ling met me at reception with a slight elevation in attention — the professional signal of a care facility worker preparing a family member for a difficult visit. "She's had a harder morning," Fang-ling said. "More confusion than usual. But she was asking for you about an hour ago."

"Asking for me."

"By name, very clearly. She's clearer about you than she is about most things right now."

The hallway to my grandmother's room. The smell of the facility — not unpleasant, I'd resolved this in my first visits: the smell was the smell of institutional cleanliness, which overlaid the unavoidable smell of a place where people were living through something difficult, but the cleaning was thorough and the food was real food and the staff were people who worked here because they chose to rather than because they'd failed to find something else, which I could read in them the way I read any group of people operating in a space together. The facility's smell was honest. I'd come to find it, in a strange way, reassuring.

My grandmother was not looking at the window when I came in. She was looking at the door, as though she'd been watching for it to open.

"Mei-mei," she said.

"Nǎi Nai. I wasn't sure you'd be expecting me."

"I knew you were coming." This was said with perfect matter-of-fact certainty, which could mean many things in her current state — could be confabulation, pattern recognition, coincidence retrofitted as prediction, or something else. I had learned not to challenge these certainties. "Sit down."

I sat.

My grandmother was, this morning, visibly more confused than the previous visit — her eyes had the unfocused quality of someone managing a great deal of internal noise, the look of someone trying to hear a conversation in a loud room. She asked me twice for my name, which she had not done on previous visits, and each time I told her, she nodded with the relief of someone who needed the anchor.

We sat for a while. I talked — about the taxi, about the hotel, about the client meeting. I described the hotel room, the view from it, the particular color of the Taipei morning. I gave her the world in small specifics, which was the thing I'd found worked best: not abstractions, not emotional content, but the precise texture of ordinary experience.

Then — between one breath and the next, without warning and with the quality I'd come to recognize as window — my grandmother's hand came across the space between us and closed around my wrist.

Hard. Not rough, but definite. The grip of someone who needed to make sure the connection was real.

My grandmother's eyes were focused. Fully, precisely focused, with the look that was different from ordinary lucidity — the look that carried something behind it, something that had been in position and was now ready.

"You smell like it," Liling said.

My breath stopped.

"Like the crossing."

Her grip on my wrist was steady. Her gaze was direct. She was there — fully, briefly, unmistakably there.

Then something shifted in her face. A weather change inside the focus. The window was still open, but it was moving. She opened her mouth as though to continue, and then she was drifting — I could see it happen, the way a lamp gutters.

Her gaze moved past me, toward the window. Quietly, as though speaking to someone just out of frame: "Kenji."

Just the name. Said the way you say the name of someone you have been thinking about for a long time.

I held still. I did not know what to do with it, so I held it the way you hold a sound you aren't sure you heard correctly — not filing it away, not yet; just keeping it.

Then the breath went out of her, slow and audible. The quality of her attention changed, something draining from behind the eyes, the grip on my wrist not loosening all at once but going through a kind of forgetting — first the intention, then the strength. Her face moved through something unnamed, a small passage, and then it was over.

The grip on my wrist loosened to an old woman's ordinary clasp. Her eyes drifted. She looked around the room with mild confusion, as though she'd been asleep and was determining where she was.

"I should have lunch soon," Liling said. "Is it lunchtime?"

"Almost," I said.

I stayed another half hour, talking about nothing consequential. I held her hand. I watched the courtyard maple through the window and told myself I was fine, that I was not shaking, that the specific electrification in my sternum was just the surprise of it, just the sudden presence of a thing I hadn't expected to find.

My grandmother slept for a few minutes — brief, shallow, the sleep of someone whose cognitive effort was high and whose body took rests where it could. When she woke, she looked at me with the particular expression of someone who has surfaced and is triangulating.

"You're still here," Liling said.

"I'm still here, Nǎi Nai."

"Good." A pause. "I don't always know who comes and goes. The days are — there are days when time doesn't stay in order." She said this with a certain dignity, the factual dignity of someone describing a condition they have made a professional assessment of. Even now she was a scientist about her own dissolution. "But I know when you're in the room."

"How do you know?"

My grandmother looked at me. "You pay attention the way I do. It takes up space." She closed her eyes again, then opened them. "Your mother doesn't do it. She pays attention but it's a different kind — it's a worried kind. Yours is curious. Even when you're worried, you're curious first." A faint smile. "I always liked that about you."

I did not trust my voice to do anything particular, so I said nothing. I held my grandmother's hand, which was warm and dry, the calluses of decades under my fingers.

After a few minutes Liling said, to the window: "The light is different there. I've been trying to describe it to the nurses but I don't have good language for it anymore. I used to have good language for everything." A pause. "I planted something there. I want you to know that. I planted something."

"In the courtyard?"

"No." My grandmother's eyes stayed on the window. "In the place with the light. The place where the garden is." She blinked. "Someone should know."

I looked at my grandmother's profile — the strong bones of it, the particular angle that had always reminded me, when I was a child, of a kind of authority I aspired to without being able to name. The bones of a woman who had made decisions and acted on them and carried the consequences with the same matter-of-fact discipline she applied to her research.

"I know, Nǎi Nai," I said. Though I didn't yet. Though what I meant was: I will know.

My grandmother nodded once, satisfied, and drifted back toward sleep.

I left at twelve-fifteen and sat on a bench in the street outside the care facility, in the May heat, and called my mother.

"I visited Nǎi Nai," I said when Huimei answered.

"I know, Fang-ling texted me. How was she?"

"She was — confused this morning. But she had a clear spell." I paused. "Mā, she mentioned someone named Kenji."

A silence.

"She often asks for Kenji," Huimei said. "It's one of her recurring things. We've asked the staff about it — they say she mentions it fairly regularly. We don't know who it is." Her voice had the specific quality of someone presenting official information and not fully inhabiting it. "Some old friend, probably. A colleague. You know how it is — the mind in that state reaches for old attachments, people from a long time ago."

"Has she ever said anything else about Kenji? Any context?"

Another pause. "No. Just the name. Sometimes she says it like she's calling for someone. Sometimes she just says it quietly, like —" My mother stopped. "Like she's remembering something pleasant."

I looked at the street. A scooter passing. A woman walking with a child. The ordinary specific life of a city going about its ordinary specific business, none of it aware of anything that wasn't itself.

I filed the name. I noted it, the way I noted things that didn't have a clear place yet — between the facts and the explanations, in the space where I kept things I wasn't ready to interpret.

"Mā. Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"When Nǎi Nai came back from Canada — in the 1970s. What was she like? Do you remember?"

"I was very young."

"I know. But do you remember anything? Anything she said about the trip? Why she went, why she came back?"

Huimei was quiet for so long that I checked the phone to make sure the call was still connected.

"She didn't talk about it," Huimei said finally. "She came back and she was — she was the same, I think. But quieter in some way. More careful. More... present. But not quite here." Another pause. "Your grandfather said once that she'd come back different. He didn't say different-bad. Just different."

"She brought things back," Huimei said, finally, as though she were continuing from a different place. "Plants. Seeds. She always had seeds."

"Seeds," I said.

"She was a botanist. She was always bringing things from her fieldwork. I didn't think anything of it."

I sat with the sun on my face. I thought about a mycorrhizal scientist who had been twenty years ahead of her field. I thought about what you would bring back, across a crossing, if you were a botanist who understood what a forest needed before the forest needed it.

"Okay," I said. "Thank you, Mā."

"Is something wrong?"

"No. No, I was just — I've been thinking about her a lot. About her work."

"Her work was remarkable. She was very far ahead of her field, her colleagues used to say." My mother paused, and I heard the specific quality in the pause that meant she was deciding whether to continue. "She had a paper — I found it once, when I was going through her things before the move to the facility. A very early paper, from the late sixties. About underground communication in forests. She was writing about mycorrhizal signaling as if it were established science. But the science didn't catch up to her until the 1990s."

"I know. I've read some of her work."

"Have you?" My mother sounded surprised. "When?"

"Recently. I've been interested in the mycorrhizal work." This was true and not the whole truth. "The forestry project I've been consulting on — it's relevant."

"She'd have liked knowing that," Huimei said. "That her work was useful. She used to say it was the most frustrating thing — to know something clearly and have no one else see it yet." My mother's voice had shifted slightly, the way it shifted when she was remembering something she'd been carrying without being certain she should put it down. "She was a difficult person sometimes. Very certain. Not patient with people who didn't keep up. But the certainty — I think now it was because she actually knew. She wasn't guessing."

I thought about this for a moment. I thought about the precision of my grandmother's hands. The calluses. The way she'd identified the maple variety in the courtyard with the casual authority of someone stating a fact they'd known for sixty years.

"She said she planted something," I said. "When she was confused. She said she planted something somewhere, and she wanted someone to know."

A silence.

"She says things like that," Huimei said finally. "In the confused periods. She has — there are themes that come back. The garden. The planting. Kenji." A pause. "I've been — I try not to interpret it too literally. The doctors say the content of dementia speech often has emotional significance without literal meaning."

"Yes," I said.

"But sometimes I wonder." My mother's voice was careful. "Sometimes I wonder if there are things she never told us. Things she — held." A pause. "She held things. She always did. Even before."

The bench was warm from the sun. A group of schoolchildren passed on the pavement, in uniform, led by a teacher who was counting heads with the practiced efficiency of someone who has been counting heads for twenty years. Ordinary life, going about its ordinary purposes.

"Mā," I said. "If I find out something — about that time, about where she was — would you want to know?"

A long pause.

"Yes," my mother said. Then, more quietly: "I think I've always wanted to know. I just didn't know it was something I could find out."

"Okay," I said.

I went to the client meeting. I was professionally present. I said the right things about the green roofs, addressed the cost concerns with the patience they required, left the meeting with a signed scope extension. I did all of this with a portion of my mind and spent the rest of it sitting with the word *Kenji*, with the word *seeds*, with the shape of something I was not yet ready to name.

At the client dinner I answered questions about urban canopy projections and ate fish and drank one glass of wine and smiled at the right moments and was professionally charming in the way I'd been professionally charming for eight years, which required no more than thirty percent of my available attention. I spent the other seventy percent in a different room entirely: a room that was a care facility in Zhonghe District, smelling of gardenias and institutional cleanliness, where my grandmother had said *you smell like it,* and *I planted something,* and *Kenji,* and then been gone.

Later, at the hotel, I looked at the photograph on my phone again. The garden. The lantern-plants. The quality of light I'd been refusing to name.

I named it, now, to myself. I said it aloud, in the hotel room, to no one.

Then I set the phone face-down on the nightstand, and looked at the ceiling, and let the thing I now knew sit beside me in the Taipei evening like a companion I hadn't known I was waiting for.

I did not sleep easily.

In the morning I was professional and the client meeting was productive and the flight home was on time.
