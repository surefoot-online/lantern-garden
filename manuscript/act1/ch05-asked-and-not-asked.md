### Chapter Five: Asked and Not Asked

I found him the next morning at the edge of the test plot, before the rest of the field team arrived.

He was doing what he sometimes did before the day's work began, which I'd taken six weeks to understand: he was memorizing it. Standing at the perimeter of the die-off, not reading data, not taking notes — just looking, with the full attention of someone storing something. The way people look at things they expect to lose.

"Priya's assessment confirms the hydrology," he said, when I was beside him. He hadn't heard me approach — or had heard me and filed it, keeping his attention on the plot. "She went out at five with two of the field technicians. The buried drainage path is intact. The soil composition is what the model needs it to be."

"I know. She sent me the preliminary results last night."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I didn't want you to be woken up by my good news." I had been trying to calibrate this — the space between professional and something else. The space was narrower than I'd planned for it to be, which was itself something I was not ready to examine directly.

He looked at me now. The thing in his face that I had been fielding for ten weeks was clearer this morning than it had been. Or perhaps I was less rigorous this morning. Or perhaps there was a threshold, in how long you stood beside someone at the edge of something they were fighting to save, after which rigor became insufficient.

"The team is going to vote on the full southern corridor commitment today," he said.

"I know."

"The vote is going to pass. Thomas is on board. Anya and Luca are on board. Priya's assessment tipped the last two holdouts."

"Good."

"It's good because of your model."

"It's good because the model is correct and Priya confirmed it."

He made a sound that was not quite a laugh — short, genuine, something between acknowledgment and exasperation. "You do that too," he said.

"What."

"Redirect credit. Precise. Systematic. Every time."

I said nothing.

"Mei." He said my name the way he occasionally said it — not as a form of address but as a form of attention. The way you'd say someone's name to let them know you were speaking to them specifically, not to the room or the situation. "Are you planning to stay?"

I looked at the test plot. The rusted crowns, the too-open light. "I'm here now."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

He waited. He was patient — one of the things about him that I had catalogued without deciding what it meant. He did not use silence as pressure. He created it and then occupied it beside me, as if the question were a problem we were looking at together rather than a demand I was being asked to meet.

"I can't answer that," I said.

"Can't or won't?"

I thought about this carefully, because I'd trained myself to be careful with words in this world — to say, as precisely as I could, what I meant rather than what was easier. "Can't," I said. "Genuinely can't. Not unwillingness. Not evasion. The question doesn't have an answer I can access right now."

"Because you don't know what you want."

"Because what I want may not be the relevant variable."

He absorbed this for a moment. "What's the relevant variable?"

I didn't answer, because I didn't have the answer. I had, at this point, something between a suspicion and a hypothesis, and the hypothesis required more evidence than I currently held.

He nodded, accepting this in the way I'd come to understand he accepted difficult professional information: not closing the discussion, but recognizing the limit of the present session.

We walked the plot edge for a while in silence, I recording angles and distances on my tablet, he with his hands in his jacket pockets doing the memorizing-thing. The field team arrived at seven-thirty — Priya, Luca, three of the field technicians — and the day's work formally began.

---

In the evening, when the work was done and the station had the specific quietness of people who have worked past the point of speech, Jun found me at the map table.

"Come for a walk," he said.

I looked up. He was in his jacket already, which meant outside. "It'll be dark soon."

"I know."

I closed the laptop and went.

The edge of the forest was fifteen minutes from the station on a path that the field team had worn over years of the same walk: up the ridge, through the cleared buffer zone, to the point where the trees began. Not the test plot — the intact forest, the northern sector, the part that was still in that condition called *old growth*, which was not a technical term but a name for something I'd had to re-learn the meaning of in this world where less of it survived.

The trees here were three hundred years old. I'd verified this when I first stood among them, because I hadn't trusted my initial reading — in my world, trees this size existed in protected areas and nowhere else. Here too, technically. The technical protection was more contested than in my world. I'd looked at the trees and felt something I didn't have good language for, and had been feeling it, in various registers, for ten weeks.

The quality of the forest was different from the test plot in every sensory register. The sound was different: the insect conversations were intact here, the birds were the birds of a full ecosystem rather than the birds of an edge, the wind through old needles had a different quality from wind through young canopy. The smell was the smell of an intact system — resin, moisture, the specific earthen note of fungal activity below the leaf litter. I'd started, three weeks ago, to think of this smell as the smell of a working network. The forest communicating with itself.

"My father used to bring me here," Jun said.

I looked at him. He hadn't said much about his father.

"He was a park ranger. In the nineties, when the designation still meant something." He was looking up into the canopy — old-growth canopy, three-hundred-year crowns, doing what they'd been doing since before anyone in the conversation's great-grandparents had been born. "He used to say that old-growth forests had earned their complexity. That you couldn't look at a tree this size and think a hundred-year-old replacement would be the same thing. They're not the same thing."

"No," I said.

"He died before the worst of it started. I used to think that was sad. Now I think —" He stopped.

"You think he didn't have to watch it."

"Yes."

The light was going. The air at this elevation, at this time of day, had the quality I'd noticed on my first visit to this forest: a depth to it, a layering of temperature from ground to crown that you could feel as you moved through it, the warmth settling, the cold descending. In my world, standing in a forest at dusk in May, this was a pleasure. Here it was also a pleasure. I was not so far from myself in this world that I could not feel it as a pleasure, which I thought about sometimes at night: what that meant about the nature of pleasure, or about the nature of me.

"I've stopped asking how you get here," Jun said.

I went still.

"I noticed," he said, "that you don't say. That what you say is technically true and doesn't explain anything. That I've been — I don't know. Not looking at it directly." He was not looking at me. He was still looking at the trees. "I've started asking a different question."

I waited.

"What keeps you from deciding?"

The birds in the canopy were doing the last-light thing — the specific intensification of sound that happened in the hour before dark, as though the forest were accounting for the day. I listened to it.

"I don't think it's a decision," I said.

Now he looked at me. "What else would it be?"

I thought about the garden in the photograph on my grandmother's bedside table. I thought about the word *crossing*, which my grandmother had used. I thought about the quality of the light in the Lantern Garden, and the quality of the light in the photograph, and the relationship between those two qualities and what it might mean that they were the same.

"I don't know yet," I said.

"But when you know —"

"When I know, I'll tell you."

He looked at me for a long moment. The old-growth trees were behind him, doing their ancient, slow work in the soil beneath them, the fungal network doing its messaging in the dark below the root systems. I thought about what it meant to stand in a dying forest that was, in its oldest section, still fully alive — to stand at the edge of two conditions, both of them real.

"I've stopped needing you to explain it," he said quietly. "I don't need that from you. But I think —" He stopped again. "I think I need to know if you're going to leave without telling me."

"I won't," I said.

"You can promise that?"

"That much I can promise."

He looked at me for another moment, then back at the trees. The last birds were settling. The dark was coming down through the canopy in layers, the way it did here — slower than in cities, more deliberate.

"What else would it be," he said again, and this time it wasn't quite a question. It was the thing he was turning over, holding up to examine.

"I don't know," I said. "But I think *decision* is the wrong frame."

"What's the right frame?"

I looked at the trees. "Something older than that."

He didn't say anything. He didn't push. After a while, we walked back along the path to the station, in the dark now, with headlamps, the sound of our footsteps precise and ordinary in the changed evening.

---

I crossed back the following morning, before dawn, while the station was still sleeping.

The crossing was always in the Garden — I'd learned this in the first month. The Lantern Garden existed in both worlds at the same coordinates, or at coordinates that were so close to the same as to be functionally identical. The garden in Jun's world was at the edge of the old-growth zone, near a specific clearing I'd found by accident in week three and had returned to methodically since.

I had not told Jun about the clearing. I had told him nothing about the crossing mechanism. I had told him I flew in from the coast and I had maintained this with the careful consistency of someone who understands that partial truths, once established, require maintenance.

The Garden in the half-dark was the Garden: the lantern-plants low to the ground, the fruiting structures catching whatever light there was and returning something more than they caught, the specific quality of the air that I had never successfully described because it occupied a register that didn't have a ready language — not temperature, not scent, not brightness, but some combination of all three that told my body it was in the between-place. I stood in it for a moment, as I always did, registering what was there. The plants. The light. The silence.

Then I crossed.

The interval was, as always, indescribable in terms that would translate. It felt like minutes. It felt like a specific weight, a specific gravity, operating differently than the world's gravity but not uncomfortably so. I was always aware, during the crossing, that I was moving through something that was not space and not time but was structured like both, and that the structure was not hostile — was, if I were honest, beautiful in the way that an elegantly solved problem is beautiful, the way a site that has been read correctly and responded to correctly has a beauty that goes beyond the aesthetic into something like rightness.

Then I was in my world, in the park, in the grey Vancouver morning, and the birds were the birds I knew, and the smell was the smell I knew, and my body was the specific weight of a body that had just crossed and would need, for the next two days, more sleep than usual.

I walked home. I made tea. I lay down on the floor beside my bed — not in it, not yet — and looked at the ceiling, which was ordinary and correct and belonging to my world.

I thought about Jun's question.

*What else would it be?*

I did not have the answer. I had the photograph on my phone, and the image of the photograph on my grandmother's bedside table, and the word *crossing*, and the word *garden,* and the word *Kenji* that my grandmother had not yet said to me — that I did not yet know was about to be said.

I thought about what decision meant. I thought about what the alternative to decision might be. I thought about a forest doing its slow work below the surface, the network doing its messaging, connecting what had been disconnected, restoring the path that had been interrupted.

I fell asleep on the floor with the tea untouched on the nightstand.
