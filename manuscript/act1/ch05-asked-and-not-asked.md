# Asked and Not Asked

She found him the next morning at the edge of the test plot, before the rest of the field team arrived.

He was doing what he sometimes did before the day's work began, which she'd taken six weeks to understand: he was memorizing it. Standing at the perimeter of the die-off, not reading data, not taking notes — just looking, with the full attention of someone storing something. The way people look at things they expect to lose.

"Priya's assessment confirms the hydrology," he said, when she was beside him. He hadn't heard her approach — or had heard her and filed it, keeping his attention on the plot. "She went out at five with two of the field technicians. The buried drainage path is intact. The soil composition is what the model needs it to be."

"I know. She sent me the preliminary results last night."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I didn't want you to be woken up by my good news." She had been trying to calibrate this — the space between professional and something else. The space was narrower than she'd planned for it to be, which was itself something she was not ready to examine directly.

He looked at her now. The thing in his face that she had been fielding for ten weeks was clearer this morning than it had been. Or perhaps she was less rigorous this morning. Or perhaps there was a threshold, in how long you stood beside someone at the edge of something they were fighting to save, after which rigor became insufficient.

"The team is going to vote on the full southern corridor commitment today," he said.

"I know."

"The vote is going to pass. Thomas is on board. Anya and Luca are on board. Priya's assessment tipped the last two holdouts."

"Good."

"It's good because of your model."

"It's good because the model is correct and Priya confirmed it."

He made a sound that was not quite a laugh — short, genuine, something between acknowledgment and exasperation. "You do that too," he said.

"What."

"Redirect credit. Precise. Systematic. Every time."

She said nothing.

"Mei." He said her name the way he occasionally said it — not as a form of address but as a form of attention. The way you'd say someone's name to let them know you were speaking to them specifically, not to the room or the situation. "Are you planning to stay?"

She looked at the test plot. The rusted crowns, the too-open light. "I'm here now."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

He waited. He was patient — one of the things about him that she had catalogued without deciding what it meant. He did not use silence as pressure. He created it and then occupied it beside her, as if the question were a problem they were looking at together rather than a demand she was being asked to meet.

"I can't answer that," she said.

"Can't or won't?"

She thought about this carefully, because she'd trained herself to be careful with words in this world — to say, as precisely as she could, what she meant rather than what was easier. "Can't," she said. "Genuinely can't. Not unwillingness. Not evasion. The question doesn't have an answer I can access right now."

"Because you don't know what you want."

"Because what I want may not be the relevant variable."

He absorbed this for a moment. "What's the relevant variable?"

She didn't answer, because she didn't have the answer. She had, at this point, something between a suspicion and a hypothesis, and the hypothesis required more evidence than she currently held.

He nodded, accepting this in the way she'd come to understand he accepted difficult professional information: not closing the discussion, but recognizing the limit of the present session.

They walked the plot edge for a while in silence, she recording angles and distances on her tablet, he with his hands in his jacket pockets doing the memorizing-thing. The field team arrived at seven-thirty — Priya, Luca, three of the field technicians — and the day's work formally began.

---

In the evening, when the work was done and the station had the specific quietness of people who have worked past the point of speech, Jun found her at the map table.

"Come for a walk," he said.

She looked up. He was in his jacket already, which meant outside. "It'll be dark soon."

"I know."

She closed the laptop and went.

The edge of the forest was fifteen minutes from the station on a path that the field team had worn over years of the same walk: up the ridge, through the cleared buffer zone, to the point where the trees began. Not the test plot — the intact forest, the northern sector, the part that was still in that condition called *old growth*, which was not a technical term but a name for something she'd had to re-learn the meaning of in this world where less of it survived.

The trees here were three hundred years old. She'd verified this when she first stood among them, because she hadn't trusted her initial reading — in her world, trees this size existed in protected areas and nowhere else. Here too, technically. The technical protection was more contested than in her world. She'd looked at the trees and felt something she didn't have good language for, and had been feeling it, in various registers, for ten weeks.

The quality of the forest was different from the test plot in every sensory register. The sound was different: the insect conversations were intact here, the birds were the birds of a full ecosystem rather than the birds of an edge, the wind through old needles had a different quality from wind through young canopy. The smell was the smell of an intact system — resin, moisture, the specific earthen note of fungal activity below the leaf litter. She'd started, three weeks ago, to think of this smell as the smell of a working network. The forest communicating with itself.

"My father used to bring me here," Jun said.

She looked at him. He hadn't said much about his father.

"He was a park ranger. In the nineties, when the designation still meant something." He was looking up into the canopy — old-growth canopy, three-hundred-year crowns, doing what they'd been doing since before anyone in the conversation's great-grandparents had been born. "He used to say that old-growth forests had earned their complexity. That you couldn't look at a tree this size and think a hundred-year-old replacement would be the same thing. They're not the same thing."

"No," she said.

"He died before the worst of it started. I used to think that was sad. Now I think —" He stopped.

"You think he didn't have to watch it."

"Yes."

The light was going. The air at this elevation, at this time of day, had the quality she'd noticed on her first visit to this forest: a depth to it, a layering of temperature from ground to crown that you could feel as you moved through it, the warmth settling, the cold descending. In her world, standing in a forest at dusk in May, this was a pleasure. Here it was also a pleasure. She was not so far from herself in this world that she could not feel it as a pleasure, which she thought about sometimes at night: what that meant about the nature of pleasure, or about the nature of her.

"I've stopped asking how you get here," Jun said.

She went still.

"I noticed," he said, "that you don't say. That what you say is technically true and doesn't explain anything. That I've been — I don't know. Not looking at it directly." He was not looking at her. He was still looking at the trees. "I've started asking a different question."

She waited.

"What keeps you from deciding?"

The birds in the canopy were doing the last-light thing — the specific intensification of sound that happened in the hour before dark, as though the forest were accounting for the day. She listened to it.

"I don't think it's a decision," she said.

Now he looked at her. "What else would it be?"

She thought about the garden in the photograph on her grandmother's bedside table. She thought about the word *crossing*, which her grandmother had used. She thought about the quality of the light in the Lantern Garden, and the quality of the light in the photograph, and the relationship between those two qualities and what it might mean that they were the same.

"I don't know yet," she said.

"But when you know —"

"When I know, I'll tell you."

He looked at her for a long moment. The old-growth trees were behind him, doing their ancient, slow work in the soil beneath them, the fungal network doing its messaging in the dark below the root systems. She thought about what it meant to stand in a dying forest that was, in its oldest section, still fully alive — to stand at the edge of two conditions, both of them real.

"I've stopped needing you to explain it," he said quietly. "I don't need that from you. But I think —" He stopped again. "I think I need to know if you're going to leave without telling me."

"I won't," she said.

"You can promise that?"

"That much I can promise."

He looked at her for another moment, then back at the trees. The last birds were settling. The dark was coming down through the canopy in layers, the way it did here — slower than in cities, more deliberate.

"What else would it be," he said again, and this time it wasn't quite a question. It was the thing he was turning over, holding up to examine.

"I don't know," she said. "But I think *decision* is the wrong frame."

"What's the right frame?"

She looked at the trees. "Something older than that."

He didn't say anything. He didn't push. After a while, they walked back along the path to the station, in the dark now, with headlamps, the sound of their footsteps precise and ordinary in the changed evening.

---

She crossed back the following morning, before dawn, while the station was still sleeping.

The crossing was always in the Garden — she'd learned this in the first month. The Lantern Garden existed in both worlds at the same coordinates, or at coordinates that were so close to the same as to be functionally identical. The garden in Jun's world was at the edge of the old-growth zone, near a specific clearing she'd found by accident in week three and had returned to methodically since.

She had not told Jun about the clearing. She had told him nothing about the crossing mechanism. She had told him she flew in from the coast and she had maintained this with the careful consistency of someone who understands that partial truths, once established, require maintenance.

The Garden in the half-dark was the Garden: the lantern-plants low to the ground, the fruiting structures catching whatever light there was and returning something more than they caught, the specific quality of the air that she had never successfully described because it occupied a register that didn't have a ready language — not temperature, not scent, not brightness, but some combination of all three that told her body it was in the between-place. She stood in it for a moment, as she always did, registering what was there. The plants. The light. The silence.

Then she crossed.

The interval was, as always, indescribable in terms that would translate. It felt like minutes. It felt like a specific weight, a specific gravity, operating differently than the world's gravity but not uncomfortably so. She was always aware, during the crossing, that she was moving through something that was not space and not time but was structured like both, and that the structure was not hostile — was, if she were honest, beautiful in the way that an elegantly solved problem is beautiful, the way a site that has been read correctly and responded to correctly has a beauty that goes beyond the aesthetic into something like rightness.

Then she was in her world, in the park, in the grey Vancouver morning, and the birds were the birds she knew, and the smell was the smell she knew, and her body was the specific weight of a body that had just crossed and would need, for the next two days, more sleep than usual.

She walked home. She made tea. She lay down on the floor beside her bed — not in it, not yet — and looked at the ceiling, which was ordinary and correct and belonging to her world.

She thought about Jun's question.

*What else would it be?*

She did not have the answer. She had the photograph on her phone, and the image of the photograph on her grandmother's bedside table, and the word *crossing*, and the word *garden,* and the word *Kenji* that her grandmother had not yet said to her — that she did not yet know was about to be said.

She thought about what decision meant. She thought about what the alternative to decision might be. She thought about a forest doing its slow work below the surface, the network doing its messaging, connecting what had been disconnected, restoring the path that had been interrupted.

She fell asleep on the floor with the tea untouched on the nightstand.
