### Chapter Three: The Care Facility

The name was Zhonghe Memory Care but everyone called it the Garden Facility because of the courtyard, which was small and carefully planted and was the best feature of the building by a significant margin — the kind of feature that had been designed by someone who understood that dignity in later life is partly a spatial question, that the availability of a view toward something growing was not a luxury but a medical intervention. I had come to appreciate this architecture with a specificity that had nothing to do with professional assessment and everything to do with what it did for my grandmother.

I arrived on Saturday afternoon. The flight had been smooth and the MRT had been crowded and Taipei in May had the particular humid green quality that Vancouver in May absolutely lacked, a density of growing air that settled on my skin like a second thought about summer. I took a taxi from the station because I was tired in the crossing-way and needed the sitting time.

The care facility was in Zhonghe District, a quiet block not far from a park. The reception desk had fresh gardenias on it — real ones, not artificial, which I noted because it was a deliberate choice, a small daily expense maintained against the logic of cost-cutting, and it told me something about who ran the facility and what they believed.

"Ms. Chen." The staff member at the desk recognized me from previous visits — a woman named Fang-ling, who had the patient warmth of someone who has been present for years of family grief and has decided to treat it as a form of sustained trust rather than a burden. "She's having a more confused day today. But she's been calm."

"Thank you. I'll just sit with her."

"She had lunch. She's been talking about the garden."

I paused. "The courtyard?"

"She looks out at it a lot. But she says things about it that don't quite match what she can see through the window. She says the light is different. Things like that." Fang-ling smiled with the professional gentleness of someone carefully not over-reading. "You know how it is."

I said that I did.

My grandmother's room was at the end of the corridor, second door from the window. The layout was good — I'd assessed it the first time I visited and had been relieved: not a room designed to manage a body, but a room that acknowledged a self. My grandmother's books were on the shelf, the actual books she'd had in her apartment, botanical texts and Chinese poetry and a few novels in Japanese, which I had always meant to ask about and had somehow never asked. Photographs on the dresser: my mother as a child, me at various ages, a professional portrait of Liling at perhaps fifty — severe and beautiful and holding a small potted plant with the confident ease of someone who has never had a complicated relationship with growing things.

On the bedside table: a photograph I had not seen before.

I noticed it but filed it, because my grandmother was awake.

Liling was sitting up in the bed, looking toward the window, and the window showed the courtyard with its careful plantings — a small maple, some ferns, a patch of what might have been flowering moss. My grandmother's face in profile was the face of someone doing something more complex than resting: there was an intentionality in the expression, a focused attention, as though the window were a page she was reading.

"Nǎi Nai," I said softly.

My grandmother turned. Her face rearranged itself through several expressions in quick succession — the first one unguarded, something I read as recognition and then almost something more than recognition, a specific *oh, it's you* that was different from the relief of a confused person seeing a familiar face. Then the dementia resumed its governance: the slight softening, the delayed focus, the warmth that was real but floating rather than anchored.

"Mei-mei," my grandmother said. Her pet name for me from childhood, which she still used, which I still loved with a tenderness that I had no professional language for. "You came."

"I came." I sat in the chair by the bed and took her hand. The hand was small and dry and still bore the particular calluses of someone who had, for fifty years of her life, worked with soil and tools. The hands of a botanist. "How are you feeling?"

"Tired," Liling said, with a kind of clarity. "Not sick-tired. Just... the week is long."

"The week is long," I agreed.

We sat for a while. This was one of the things I'd learned about visiting: the visiting didn't require constant speech. My grandmother sometimes needed the quiet, and I had learned to give the quiet without filling it with the anxious conversation people fill silences with when they're afraid the silence means something is wrong. The silence here meant something was real.

"The garden is different today," Liling said, still looking at the window.

I looked at the courtyard. The maple, the ferns, the careful planting. "What's different about it?"

"The light." My grandmother's voice was dreamy, only partly in the room. "At the crossing, the light is — different from here. I try to tell Fang-ling but she thinks I'm talking about the courtyard."

I kept my voice even. "What do you call it? The crossing?"

But the moment had passed — I could see it, the way a lucid window in my grandmother's cognition opened and closed like a lamp in wind. Liling blinked, refocused, and patted my hand.

"Have you eaten?" she said, in a voice that so precisely echoed my mother that I almost laughed.

"I ate on the plane, Nǎi Nai."

"Plane food." Same tone my mother used for *tea.* This was evidently a family inheritance.

We talked about the weather. We talked about the courtyard plants — my grandmother identified the maple variety precisely and correctly, which was one of the things about the dementia that I found most striking: the specialized knowledge survived when so much else was fragmentary. Liling could not always name the current year but she knew the mycorrhizal associates of a Pacific Northwest conifer from forty feet away.

"The acer in the courtyard," Liling said. "Japanese maple. *Acer palmatum*. The person who planted it in a north-facing position — that was a mistake. It will grow but it won't color properly in autumn. The anthocyanins need more light." She said this as a matter of established fact, without rancor. "I mentioned it to the woman who manages the garden. She said she'd consider it."

"Did she?"

"She nodded. Which is what people do when they have no intention of acting on information." My grandmother paused. "I used to do it too. When students brought me papers I wasn't going to read."

This was so precisely Liling — the Liling I'd known my whole life, the one who had run her mycorrhizal research group at the NTU Department of Botany with the cheerful ruthlessness of someone who considered vagueness to be a form of unkindness — that I felt the specific grief of these windows: the grief of seeing someone clearly and knowing the clarity was temporary.

"Nǎi Nai," I said, because I could. "Tell me something you remember. From when you were working. What was the most interesting thing you ever found?"

My grandmother turned to look at me. The window was still open; I could feel it.

"I found a forest that was dying and didn't know yet," Liling said. "It looked healthy from the surface. The trees were large, the canopy was intact. But the network below — the mycorrhizal connections, the fungal bridge — had been fragmenting for thirty years. A road had cut through the primary corridor. Nobody noticed because the trees above the cut were old enough to have stored reserves. They were living on what they'd saved." She stopped. "I wrote a paper about it. Nobody cited it for twenty years."

"But they were right?"

"I was right." A pause. "The forest eventually showed it. By then I was too old to feel vindicated. I just felt sorry for the trees."

"Where was the forest?"

My grandmother looked at the window. Something in her face was doing its wind-and-lamp thing — I watched it and prepared for the drift.

"Somewhere beautiful," Liling said. "I planted something there. Before I left." She blinked. "Did I tell you? I planted something."

"No," I said carefully. "Tell me."

But the window had tilted, the light inside my grandmother's attention reorganizing around something else. She patted my hand with the vague warmth of someone who has moved rooms.

"You should eat more," she said. "You look thin."

After an hour, Liling's attention drifted into a more complete confusion — she addressed me by a name that was not mine, then seemed to be talking to someone who was not in the room. I held her hand and didn't correct the confusion, which my mother had told me was the right approach and which I'd confirmed with the facility's dementia specialist: correction was not kind. Presence was what was needed.

Then, with no warning, Liling's grip on my hand tightened.

My grandmother's eyes focused, fully and precisely, in a way that was different from the drift. It was not a gentle focus. It was the focus of someone who has something to say and has located the window in which to say it.

"The garden blooms at the crossing," Liling said. Her voice was steady. "You mustn't leave your shoes there."

I went very still.

"Nǎi Nai —"

"I left mine. Once."

Then she was gone again — the grip relaxed, the eyes softened, she asked if the nurse had come yet with her afternoon medication.

I sat with my heart doing something specific and difficult. I had, for twenty years, been the kind of person who noticed pattern and resisted the urge to over-read it. In my professional work this was a discipline: the client's preferred reading of a site was not the site's actual argument. You had to be rigorous. You had to distinguish between what you wanted to see and what was there.

This was dementia. My grandmother was eighty-eight years old and in a middle phase of a dementia that produced vivid, internally coherent delusions, produced confabulation, produced the appearance of meaning in utterances that were neurological rather than semantic. I knew this. I had read the literature and spoken to the specialist and had navigated my grandmother's confused speech for three years.

I filed it. Not dismissed — filed. There was a difference.

I had been, in my professional life, meticulous about this distinction. In my first years, I'd been prone to what my mentor at the firm had called *over-attribution* — reading too much narrative into a site's features, constructing explanations that were elegant but that lacked sufficient evidence. My mentor, a Finnish landscape architect named Erika who was meticulous with site-reading and intolerant of architectural romanticism, had corrected me not unkindly: *The site doesn't know what we want it to say. We have to find what it's actually saying, which is harder and less satisfying and more useful.* I had internalized this to the point where I sometimes found myself applying it to situations that were not landscapes.

My grandmother was eighty-eight and had dementia. The crossing capacity — if she had it, if such a thing existed — was something I had not yet named to myself directly, six months in, because naming it directly would require a commitment to a framework I wasn't sure I could defend. I had been proceeding empirically: noting what happened, recording it, not drawing conclusions that exceeded the evidence. I had been doing this with the same discipline I applied to sites: looking for what was actually there, not what was satisfying.

What was actually there, today, was: my grandmother had used the word *crossing* in a context that was not the courtyard. My grandmother had referenced leaving shoes somewhere. My grandmother had mentioned planting something *there,* in the place with the different light.

These facts were consistent with multiple explanations. The most parsimonious was dementia confabulation, which produced internally coherent narratives that drew on emotional memories. The less parsimonious was something else. I noted both explanations and filed the facts between them and did not yet choose.

I was a scientist's granddaughter, even if I was not a scientist. I understood that not-choosing was sometimes the disciplined position and that the impulse to resolve uncertainty was not always an epistemically legitimate impulse.

I stayed another forty minutes. When I rose to go, I kissed my grandmother's forehead and said I'd be back soon, and my grandmother said, in a perfectly ordinary voice: "Bring me some of those cookies from the bakery near your apartment. Not the dry ones."

"You remember my apartment?" I said, surprised.

"I remember everything," Liling said, mildly. "Just not always in order."

At the door, I turned back. The bedside table. The photograph I hadn't looked at properly.

I crossed back to it quickly, while my grandmother's eyes were closed. The photograph was printed on regular photo paper — not a professional print, something that had come from a home printer or a pharmacy kiosk. It showed a garden. Not the courtyard; the framing was different, the light was different, the plants were different. I leaned closer.

Lantern-like plants, ground level, fruiting structures that — I looked at the quality of light around them and could not quite determine whether what I was seeing was a photographic artifact or something the photograph had caught despite having no technical reason to catch. The plants had the pendant droop of physalis. The background suggested forest edge, the specific thinning of light that happened at a forest's margin.

I photographed it with my phone. Quickly, without quite deciding to.

Then I went out into the New Taipei afternoon, into the density of May, and stood on the street outside the care facility and looked at the photograph I'd just taken, of the photograph, on my screen.

The garden in the image.

The quality of the light.

I put my phone in my pocket and walked toward the MRT, and did not let myself think about it for the rest of the evening, which required more discipline than I was accustomed to needing.
