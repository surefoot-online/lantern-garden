# The Tenth Week

*From the testimony record, Session One: "I'm going to describe what I was calling, to myself, the tenth week of crossings — or what I believed was the last phase. I believed that because I couldn't imagine continuing. I want to be clear: I was wrong about the last-phase part. I was not wrong that something had changed."*

---

What Mei noticed first, arriving, was always the quality of the silence.

Her world's forests — what was left of them, in the parks, in the green corridors she designed for, along the edges of city districts where the planners had been far-sighted or merely lucky — had a silence that was present. A noise made of smaller noises: insects articulating their specific arguments, birds in dialogue with the canopy, the systemic hum of things living toward their purposes. Even diminished, even pressed on all sides by the city's project of elimination, forests in her world were loud with being alive.

This forest was quiet in the wrong way.

She stood at the edge of the test plot with her bag still on her shoulder and understood, before she saw the extent of it, that something had gone badly. The trees at the perimeter were alive — she could read the turgor in the needles, the way the bark held its color — but fifty meters in, the crown closure changed. The canopy above the test plot was the color of old rust.

Jun was already there. He was standing at the plot's edge with his back to her, both hands in his jacket pockets, and he was not moving. This is the other thing she'd learned in ten weeks: when Jun stopped moving, the news was bad.

"How much?" she said, dropping her bag.

He turned. She had been watching his face long enough by then to read it the way she read land — by what was present, what was missing, what the surface could not quite conceal. He looked like someone who had been awake for thirty-six hours and was refusing to admit it, which she would learn later was accurate.

"The whole interior block," he said. "We checked at five this morning. The crown browning was thirty percent when we left at midnight. By five it was full die-off. Three months ahead of the worst-case model."

She looked past him into the plot. The light was wrong — not just the color of dying needles but the quality of it, the way it came through too easily where the canopy was supposed to be thick. Dead trees let in light. Living forests keep secrets.

"How wide is the interior block?"

"Roughly two hectares."

Two hectares of test-plot heart, three months ahead of schedule. She did the geometry without meaning to — it was the thing she'd been doing since childhood, reading space as argument. Two hectares at this rate of propagation, in a corridor under this kind of fragmentation pressure —

"Show me the vector," she said.

"Mei —"

"I know what it means. Show me the vector first, then we can discuss what it means."

He looked at her for a moment with the expression she'd been trying, for ten weeks, to stop noticing. Not gratitude, exactly. Not relief, exactly. Something more careful than either — the look of someone who has learned to be cautious about wanting things, and is being cautious now.

He took out his tablet and opened the mapped survey.

She studied it for four minutes without speaking. She was not being dramatic; it simply took four minutes to see what she was looking at. The die-off was not uniform and not random. It had moved from the southwest, which matched the prevailing beetle corridor, but the shape of it was not what beetle infestation alone would produce. Beetle infestation produces a thinning — it goes through a population the way any infestation goes, opportunistically, concentrating on the stressed and the isolated. What she was reading on Jun's map was something else: an interior collapse, as though the plot had lost its structural will from the inside out.

"You have a network problem," she said.

He exhaled slowly. "The mycorrhizal connectivity assessment was inconclusive —"

"The assessment was designed to detect a threshold collapse. This isn't threshold. This is what happens to a network when the nodes at the center drop out simultaneously. Like losing the middle columns of a building. The perimeter trees look fine because they're still connected to the surrounding matrix, but there's nothing for them to be connected to in the interior." She zoomed the map on his tablet with two fingers, rotated it. "What's the geomorphology here? Is there a sub-surface drainage feature running northeast?"

Jun leaned over the tablet. He was close enough that she could feel the warmth from his jacket. She noted this and redirected her attention to the map.

"There's a buried creek — historical drainage, rerouted when the access road was built in the nineties. Why?"

"The mycorrhizal network would have followed the original drainage pattern. It's the path of least resistance for fungal propagation. When the creek was rerouted, the network above it lost its connective tissue — not all at once, because the existing roots maintained continuity, but over thirty years it thinned. Your test plot sits directly over where the connection would have been weakest." She straightened. "The die-off looks like a beetle advance because the beetle pressure is real and it's tracking the same weakness. But the underlying failure is hydrological. The beetles are reading the terrain the same way I am."

Jun was quiet for a moment.

"The coalition has been modeling this as a beetle-infestation problem," he said.

"The coalition has been looking at the symptom."

Another silence. In the distance — two hundred, maybe three hundred kilometers, she couldn't always calibrate distance accurately in this world — smoke was doing something to the southern horizon, laying a bar of orange across the grey. She'd been watching the smoke seasons. In her world, the smoke seasons were bad and getting worse; here they were a different category of experience. Here they lasted. She had arrived in this world six weeks into a smoke season that had already been running for four months.

"If you're right," Jun said, "then the seeding strategy needs to account for the hydrological pattern."

"Yes."

"Which means we've been seeding in the wrong sequence."

"Not wrong. Suboptimal. You've been seeding for surface connectivity. You need to seed for the buried network — treat the missing creek as the primary propagation axis and work out from there."

He was quiet again. Then: "How long would it take you to build a drainage-corrected spatial model?"

"I need the historical hydrology data. Survey maps if you have them, or I can derive it from the elevation data and the current drainage pattern." She picked up her bag. "I can have a preliminary framework by this evening. Coalition meeting is —"

"Seven o'clock."

"Then I'll have something by six-thirty."

He was looking at her again with that careful, rationed expression. She had been, for ten weeks, fielding it and setting it aside, which was becoming increasingly difficult and which she refused to acknowledge.

"You flew in from the coast," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Yes," she said.

This was what she told people. She said she flew in from the coast — from Vancouver, usually, which was true in the sense that she was usually in Vancouver just before she crossed. She said she was freelancing on a consulting basis, which was true in the sense that no one was paying her and she was doing it anyway. She said she'd heard about the Cascade Coalition's work through professional networks, which was true in the sense that there were professional networks in both worlds that shared the same name and a similar institutional logic, if not the same individuals.

She had told these half-truths with increasing facility over ten weeks and they sat in her with increasing weight. This was one of the things she had not worked out a system for: what to do about the things that were true enough to say and false enough to carry.

"The morning flight?" Jun said.

"Yes."

He nodded, accepting this, which was the other thing she had not worked out a system for.

---

The coalition's field station was a converted ranger station at the road's end, twelve kilometers into what had been, in Jun's telling, a UNESCO heritage reserve until the designation was quietly reclassified six years ago. The building was a single-story structure of aging Douglas fir, expanded with a prefab annex that housed the lab equipment. The smell when she walked in was the smell she had come to associate with this place: instant coffee, pine resin, the particular staleness of a space where people work very long hours and sleep, when they sleep, in the back room on cots.

Three members of the core team were at desks when they arrived. Priya, the coalition's lead ecologist — small, fierce, prone to drawing on any available surface when she was thinking — looked up from her monitor and said, without preamble: "The mycorrhizal bridge is still holding in the northern sector."

"The northern sector isn't the problem," Jun said.

"The northern sector not being the problem is the only good news we have, so I'm reporting it."

Luca, who managed the data systems and was the youngest member of the core team by nearly a decade, was already pulling up overnight sensor logs. He wore headphones permanently, one ear off, one on — a state of permanent half-attention that Mei had decided was his superpower rather than his deficiency.

"Beetle pressure maps from overnight," he said, spinning his monitor toward the room. "The southwest corridor advance is consistent with last week's models, which you'd think would be reassuring."

"It isn't," Jun said.

"I know. I'm telling you where the consistent part ends." He navigated to a secondary display. "This is the sub-corridor. We had sensors in the interior test plot. At eleven forty-seven p.m., six sensors dropped signal simultaneously."

"Simultaneously," Mei said.

"Within forty-three seconds."

She leaned over his shoulder to look at the timestamp cluster. Forty-three seconds. Not a cascade failure, not a progressive thinning. Forty-three seconds was a simultaneous collapse, which meant the sensors had been reading a live system that had then, in a window narrow enough to call instantaneous, stopped being a system.

"The mycorrhizal network collapsed as a unit," she said.

"That's my read."

"Which means —" She stopped. She was doing the thing she sometimes did in this world, which was forgetting that the coalition scientists didn't always need her to explain things to them. They were not unintelligent; they had simply been looking at a different model. "That confirms the network failure I was describing outside," she said, more carefully. "The network didn't thin gradually. It went all at once."

"Which is consistent," Priya said, from her desk, "with a central-node failure model. Which we've been treating as a theoretical limit case."

"It's not theoretical anymore," Jun said.

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a thing that was theoretical becomes something to live inside of. Mei watched it happen. She had watched a version of this silence in her own professional world — in city council chambers when a preliminary flood model became a flood, in engineering offices when a projected failure became an actual one — and she recognized its texture. It was the silence of people reassessing what they were doing, and why.

What she couldn't say, in that room, was the thing she was also thinking: that she had been watching the Cascade Collapse from two vantage points for ten weeks, and the version she was watching in her own world — slower, milder, still in the zone of prevention if the political will could be summoned — was already showing the early markers of what this test plot had just become. Her world was ten, fifteen, maybe twenty years behind this one in trajectory. Not safe. Ahead of safe.

She went to the corner of the room where she'd claimed a small desk three weeks ago, opened her laptop, and began pulling the historical hydrology data from the county GIS archive.

She had six hours to build something useful.

---

She built something useful in four.

The model was preliminary and she said so three times in the presentation, but it showed what she needed it to show: a spatial framework for re-sequencing the fungal network seeding, oriented along the historical drainage axis rather than the surface topology. She'd derived the buried creek's path from the elevation data and the pattern of surviving moisture-indicator species at the plot's edge — the species that tracked subsurface water even when the surface was dry. The creek was there in the landscape, just invisible. It had been invisible since 1993 when the access road had been cut.

The coalition met at seven in the main room, folding chairs arranged in a rough circle, the smell of coffee now strong enough to be architectural. Eleven people. She had met all of them over ten weeks; she knew their professional roles and, in most cases, some fraction of their personal ones. Thomas, the Indigenous land steward who co-led the coalition, had a daughter beginning school in September. Anya, one of the project ecologists, was sleeping four hours a night and had been since February. Jun's relationship with them was not one of command but of sustained mutual trust, which she had been watching with the attention she usually reserved for sites: trying to understand how it had been built, and what kept it standing.

She presented the model. She walked them through the hydrological derivation, the sensor timestamp data, the projected re-sequencing. She was careful about her language — preliminary, working hypothesis, pending field verification — because in her professional experience, the fastest way to lose a room of technical experts was to overstate confidence, and she did not want to lose this room.

When she finished, Thomas asked the question she'd been waiting for.

"If the buried drainage is the primary propagation axis, what does the seeding timeline look like? Are we talking about re-doing work that's already been done?"

"Some of it. The northern sector seeding is fine as-is — that topology is different, the surface and subsurface drainage align there. The middle and southern sectors would need to be re-sequenced. Some of what's already been seeded in those areas will need supplemental inoculation along the drainage axis. It's not starting over. It's — correcting a navigational error. You've been going to the right destination on the wrong road."

"How long?"

She looked at the model. "Eight weeks, if the beetle pressure stays at current levels. Twelve if it advances as fast as this week's projection."

Twelve weeks. She saw the number land. Twelve weeks was a long time for people who had been running out of time for three years.

"It's still possible," she said, and she meant it, which was the thing about Mei in these coalition meetings that she had noticed people responding to and could not quite explain. She was not optimistic — she read the models, she knew the probabilities — but she could mean it when she said it, and apparently this was different from the formulaic *it's still possible* that most people in the room had been saying to themselves and each other for months.

After the meeting, the circle of chairs opened into small clusters of two and three. Jun found her at the side table pouring the last of the coffee, which was mostly hot water at this point.

"That was well-received," he said.

"The model is sound. Whether the re-sequencing works depends on field conditions we won't fully know until we're in the field."

"I know. I mean the way you presented it." He paused. "You said 'correcting a navigational error.' Three people used that phrase afterward. It gave them something to hold."

She looked at her coffee. "Language is a spatial problem. You have to put the idea somewhere they can walk to."

He smiled at this, briefly and with some effort. The exhaustion in his face was chronic now; she could see it had settled into the musculature. He had been running this coalition for three years with the resources of a mid-tier university research grant and the moral weight of a losing war, and it showed, and he carried it with a steadiness that she found — she would not say moving. She found it significant. The way a load-bearing element of a structure was significant.

"Thank you," he said. "For this."

"I want to be here," she said.

This was true. This was the truest thing she said in this world, and also the most complicated.

He nodded, carefully.

She went to the back room and lay down on one of the cots and did not sleep for a long time, listening to the wrong birds outside the window — birds that were almost the birds she knew but not quite, their calls at a pitch that registered in the part of her that tracked anomaly, the part that was always working.
