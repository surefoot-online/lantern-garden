### Chapter Four: The Cascade

*From the testimony record, Session One: "I want to be specific about what Jun was fighting. I think the Tribunal has not fully understood the scale. I understand that the Tribunal's brief concerns the crossings and their implications, but the crossings don't exist in a vacuum. They exist in a context. The context is a world that is losing its forest cover at a rate that would have been, fifteen years ago, a science-fiction scenario. It's not science fiction now. I want the record to reflect that the people I worked with on the other side of the crossing were operating under conditions that the term 'crisis' does not adequately name."*

---

The full coalition meeting was held on a Tuesday, in the largest room of the ranger station, with most members participating by video from field sites or from offices in Vancouver and Seattle — the cities in Jun's world that most closely corresponded to the ones in mine, which was something I'd spent three months calibrating and had not yet finished calibrating.

I sat at the back of the room with a legal pad and watched Jun present.

Jun presenting to a technical audience was a different version of Jun from the one I'd been working beside at field sites and in the station's cramped analysis room. This version was formal without being stiff, precise without being affectless. He spoke like someone who has had to fight for every listener's continued attention and has learned, from the fighting, exactly how to earn it. He did not apologize for the data. He did not soften the projections. He presented what was happening, in the specific language of what was happening, and trusted the audience to hold it.

I was grateful for this. I had been in rooms — in my own world, in client presentations, in city council meetings — where the presenting scientist had softened the data, had hedged the projections, had found the language that would make the room more comfortable. I understood why. I had done versions of it myself. But I was grateful that Jun did not.

He was walking through the current collapse models. The display behind him showed three maps: the 2019 baseline, the 2022 actuals, and the projected 2024 extent if current conditions continued. I had seen the maps. I looked at them again with the fresh eyes of someone who had, in the previous week, been in her own world looking at the 2019 baseline of a forest that had not yet arrived at what this map showed.

The temperate rainforest corridor — what in my world would be the coastal forest of British Columbia and northern Washington — was, in Jun's world, showing a die-off pattern that had been advancing since the late 1990s. The immediate driver was the mountain pine beetle, which had expanded catastrophically north and upward as the winters lost their cold-kill capacity. But the beetle advance was the headline under which a more complex ecological story was occurring: the beetle-killed trees were not being replaced by the species that would have regenerated in a pre-warming cycle, because the seed banks in the soil were themselves under heat stress, because the soil moisture was shifting, because the mycorrhizal networks that would have mediated the succession — that would have facilitated the transfer of carbon and nutrients from the dying old-growth to the emerging understory — were themselves fragmenting under pressure.

"The mycorrhizal network is not," Jun said, "simply a supporting element of the forest ecosystem. I want to be precise about this for the record, because I think the conventional framing undersells the mechanism." He advanced the slide. The new image showed a visualization of the network — the web of fungal hyphae connecting root systems across the soil, invisible except in the visualization, which rendered it in the blue-green of bioluminescent water. It looked, I thought, like a map of all the things a forest needed to say to itself.

"The network is the immune system. The messaging system. The credit system, if you want a financial metaphor. When a tree is stressed, it calls through the network. When a tree is dying, it transfers its remaining resources — carbon, phosphorus, water, everything it has — through the network to its neighbors and to the next generation of seedlings at its root. The network is how the forest manages succession, maintains resilience, and responds to disturbance. Without it, the trees are standing alone."

Priya, from a corner of the room, said: "And what we have now —"

"Is the network failing faster than the trees," Jun said. "The beetle infestation is killing trees. But the mycorrhizal collapse is killing the network that would allow the forest to reconstitute after the beetle pressure passes. We are looking at a scenario where the beetle kills the trees and the mycorrhizal collapse kills the forest."

A silence. A coalition-size silence, which was different from a field team silence: eleven people on video feeds, some of them mid-gesture, all of them holding the same arithmetic.

"Timeline," someone said from a video feed.

Jun looked at the model. I watched him look at it, watched him do what I had learned he did when he delivered hard numbers: he did not look at the audience. He looked at the data, as though he were checking it one more time, confirming it was what he thought it was, before reporting it.

"If current rates continue and the fungal network project fails to establish connectivity in the southern corridor within the next six months, we are looking at a tipping point between years three and five from now at which active management can no longer affect the trajectory. The forest self-recovery capacity will be below the threshold required. After that point, whatever grows in the corridor will not be what was there. It will be something different. Something adapted to different conditions." He paused. "This is sometimes presented as change rather than loss. I want to be clear that it is loss."

The room sat with this.

I wrote on my legal pad: *corridor. six months. threshold.* I was building my own model as he spoke, the spatial version that translated the ecological argument into terrain and geometry, the form it needed to take before I could see what intervention looked like from the ground.

"The fungal network project," Jun continued, "is currently our best active option. We are seeding an artificial mycorrhizal bridge — inoculating the soil in target areas with fungal cultures derived from the intact northern sector, attempting to establish a connected network across the fragmented patches in the southern and middle corridors." He advanced again. A new map — the proposed seeding sequence, the corridor topology, the projected connectivity timelines. "We have a revised spatial framework, as of this week, that realigns the seeding sequence along the historical hydrological axis. I'm going to ask Mei Chen to walk us through that."

I stood and went to the front of the room.

I kept it tight. Jun had laid the science. What the room needed now was the terrain — the spatial argument that translated the ecological model into intervention. I showed them the buried creek. I showed them the sensor timestamps from the night of the interior plot collapse. I showed them the revised seeding sequence.

"The old drainage axis gives us something the surface topology doesn't," I said. "It's a path the fungal network recognizes, at a biological level, as connective tissue. We're not inventing a new route. We're restoring the one that was interrupted."

Thomas said, from his seat: "What does restoring interrupted connectivity look like, spatially? I want to be able to explain this on the ground."

I looked at him. "You know how water finds its way back to a stream channel even when the channel's been rerouted? The path is in the topology. The landscape remembers it. This is the same. The network wants to go where the drainage went. We're giving it the starting inoculant and the spatial guide. It'll do the rest."

Thomas nodded slowly — the look of someone testing a statement against ground truth, against the terrain he'd walked for thirty years. He seemed to find it consistent.

Afterward, in the general dispersal — the video feeds dropping, the conversations reorganizing into pairs and small groups, the first signs of the working week resuming — Priya found me at the coffee station.

"The hydrological model," Priya said. "Where did you learn to derive buried drainage from surface indicators?"

"Fire-corridor modeling, originally," I said. "The moisture pattern in a burned landscape shows you the drainage history more clearly than a topographic map in some cases. I was doing fire-recovery work in the interior a few years ago."

Priya looked at me with the expression I had come to recognize as her version of respect: not warmth, exactly, but a downgrade in skepticism that served the same function. "I'd like to look at the full model. Would you share the working files?"

"Of course."

This was one of the things that had been, in ten weeks, gradually becoming something I had to manage: the coalition's increasing reliance on my spatial analysis. The work was good and I could see it was good and I was glad to do it. But every piece of good work made the story I was telling — the flying in from the coast, the consulting basis — a story that was larger and more load-bearing and more precisely wrong.

I found Jun in the doorway at the end of the day, looking at the smoke horizon. The orange bar to the south had shifted closer.

"Two hundred kilometers," he said, when I stood beside him. "Used to be the smoke seasons were a coastal event. Now we're smelling them up here."

The ranger station was at elevation, in a location that — in my world — was national park. Here it was contested ground, technically protected but with the protection under legal challenge from extraction interests. The air had a quality I'd been unable to fully describe in my mental notes: not quite smoke, not quite ordinary. Something between the two. A smell that had been named, I knew, by firefighters in the climate-change era: the smell of forests that were hot and dry in seasons when they should have been wet and cold.

"The vote on the southern corridor," I said. "Thomas didn't commit."

"Thomas is cautious. He'll come around when the field assessment confirms the hydrology."

"What if it doesn't confirm it?"

Jun looked at me sideways. "Are you not confident in the model?"

I thought about this. "I'm confident in the derivation. I'm cautious about the on-the-ground conditions. The model assumes the buried drainage path is intact — that the historical hyphal network followed it. If the soil composition was altered when the access road was built —"

"Then we'll find out in the field and adjust."

"Yes."

He turned back to the smoke horizon. "You always do that," he said.

"Do what?"

"State the failure mode. Before anyone else has thought of it. You build it into the presentation, the model, the conversation. You've already accounted for being wrong."

I considered this. "It's faster than being surprised by it."

"Is that what it's for."

"What else would it be for."

He didn't answer directly. He said, instead: "Priya wants you on the southern corridor field assessment team. She asked me to ask you."

"When?"

"End of next week, if the seeding prep is on schedule."

End of next week. I filed this. I did not know, at end of next week, where I would be — my crossings were not fully on schedule, or rather they were on a schedule I did not control. I had some influence over it. I had no certainty.

"Tell Priya yes," I said.

"You're sure?"

"I want to be there," I said.

Same answer as before. Same truth. Same complication.

The smoke horizon held its orange at the south and the sun went down behind the ridge to the west, and for a moment the light was extraordinary — not the quality of a beautiful sunset but the quality of a beautiful sunset made by fire, which was its own category of beauty: the kind that carries its meaning in what made it.

I stood in it with Jun and said nothing, and he said nothing, and the silence was the kind of shared silence that meant something and that both of us were, for different reasons, not yet ready to name.
