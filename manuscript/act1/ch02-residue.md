### Chapter Two: Residue

I woke in my own apartment on a Thursday morning with the specific confusion of someone who cannot account for the previous twenty-four hours, which was, by now, a familiar confusion. This did not make it comfortable.

The apartment was in East Vancouver, on a block I'd chosen because it was within walking distance of the park — the specific park, the specific corner of it, where the lantern-plants grew and where I'd first, six months ago, walked into the Garden without understanding what I was walking into. The apartment had good bones and bad windows and a view of the alley that I had never successfully made anything of, design-wise, which I considered an ongoing professional embarrassment. I had plants on every sill, most of them from my world and one, I'd noticed three months ago, that I couldn't identify in any Pacific Northwest guide and suspected I'd brought through on my shoes.

I lay still for a moment, doing the inventory. My body felt the way it always felt after a crossing: the particular heaviness of a fatigue that was not quite physical, as though something slightly more essential than muscle had been worked. My hands were fine. My eyes were slow to focus. These were reliable indicators, not alarming ones; I'd been tracking them for six months.

What I could not account for: Wednesday morning.

I knew Wednesday had happened. I'd had a site review meeting for the Burrard corridor project — I could see, on my phone, a series of emails I'd sent on Wednesday afternoon that indicated the meeting had occurred. I had notes. I had a marked-up PDF. What I could not access was any experiential memory of Wednesday morning: the walk to the meeting, the coffee I'd presumably had, the particular quality of that morning's light, the specific faces of the junior staff who'd attended the review. Not amnesia — I had the afternoon, clear and sequential. Just a gap, a curtained interval, of maybe four hours.

This had been happening since the second month of crossings. I'd mapped it as carefully as I could: the gaps always covered peripheral time, not core professional function. I did not lose work. I did not lose conversations I'd had with people who needed me present. I lost ambient experience — the weather, the commute, the texture of an ordinary morning. I'd been reading it as the mind's editing function, prioritizing what had to be retained and releasing what could be spared.

I had not yet decided whether this interpretation was correct or whether it was something I'd constructed to make the gaps livable.

I made tea. I stood at the bad windows looking at the alley with the tea in my hands and tried to be somewhere specific, which was what I did now in my own world when I felt the displacement starting. I named what I could see: the recycling bins, the neighbor's cat sleeping on the fence in a position that could not possibly be comfortable, the way the rain from the previous night had pooled in the alley's low point. Ordinary. Existing. I was in my world, in my kitchen, in my body, on a Thursday morning in May.

I went to my office.

---

The firm was three stops on the SkyTrain, a walk across a plaza I'd helped design seven years ago — the drainage was still off at the northeast corner, I noted this every time and had stopped saying anything about it because the client had not wanted to hear it and the firm had agreed to sign off and this was one of the things I kept in a file in my mind labeled *decisions that were made over my objection and that I remain correct about but that no longer constitute an emergency.* The plaza looked fine. The northeast corner was subtly wrong in a way that only I and the rain knew.

My desk was the kind of desk that tells you things about the person who occupies it: I had cleared the paperwork into organized stacks but the organization was the kind that required extensive personal decoding — files by project in sequence of complexity rather than alphabetically, reference materials annotated in three colors I'd long since forgotten the original coding of, a small collection of soil samples in labeled vials that I kept meaning to return to their respective sites and never did. My colleagues treated my desk as a controlled substance: approaching it only when necessary, handling its contents with care.

I opened the Burrard corridor project. I had notes from Wednesday's meeting — good notes, professionally legible, indicating a version of myself that had been fully present and professionally competent. I read them with the slight dissociation of reading a document authored by a close colleague: recognizing the hand, recognizing the thinking, not quite accessing the experience of having written it.

The green roofs section. The client wanted green roofs on three of the five tower blocks — I'd been advocating for all five, which was the ecologically coherent choice, and the client had been pushing back on cost, which was the economically coherent choice, and this was the conversation I'd been having with real estate developers for eight years.

I opened the file for tower block three — the one the developer was most reluctant on, a forty-two-floor residential tower on the south end of the Burrard corridor site. The brief called for a rooftop garden as a "green amenity" for residents; the developer had approved the line item because the marketing team wanted the renderings. I had the renderings. They were good — I was good, the junior designer who'd done the visualization work was particularly good — three hundred square meters of sedum, ornamental grasses, two specimen trees in engineered soil pits, a serpentine path that would photograph beautifully from the adjacent towers.

I looked at them for a long time.

The ecological math was sound. The drainage modeling was precise. The species selection was climate-appropriate and the maintenance schedule was realistic. I had pushed for deeper soil pits than the structural engineer initially budgeted for, and won, which meant the trees would actually establish rather than performing establishment for three years and then quietly failing. Everything was right.

The space would be beautiful. It would be photographed extensively for the development's marketing materials. It would be featured in the building's sales pitch as evidence that this was a development that cared about the city's ecological future. And it would be used, genuinely used, by perhaps forty residents on warm days, in a building of four hundred and thirty units, in a city of two and a half million people, in a decade when the question of what cities were for was becoming urgent in ways the developer's brief did not address.

The work felt paper-thin.

I had been trying to name this for two months. The closest I'd gotten was: my work in Jun's world has mass. When I built the hydrological model that morning — last night, whenever it had been — I had been working with something that was genuinely at risk, in a situation where the spatial analysis was the difference between a failing intervention and a viable one. The stakes were real in the way that the word *real* only partially covers: ecological collapse is happening now, on a specific hillside, to specific trees that are dying in a way that is preventable if the analysis is correct. My work there lands on something solid.

This rooftop garden was good. It was not nothing. In my world, the right small decisions, made at scale across a decade, might prevent this world's trajectory from becoming Jun's. I believed this professionally. I had believed it in both worlds, the same belief. But in Jun's world, I was working on what had already become the emergency. Here, I was working on the argument that the emergency might be preventable.

The gap between those two things was not a professional gap. It was a different kind of gap entirely, and I didn't have good language for it yet.

My phone rang at eleven-fifteen.

My mother's face on the screen.

"Mā," I said.

"You didn't call Sunday."

"I know. I was in — I had a project thing. I'm sorry."

My mother, Huimei, was sixty-two years old and lived in Taipei and had the specific telephone personality of someone who has decided to express love through mild accusation, which I had been navigating since my early teens and could now read with great accuracy. Behind the mild accusation was the actual message: *I thought about you this week, and I thought about you because I was worried, and I was worried because you've sounded tired, and I don't like that you're tired and far away.*

"Have you eaten?" my mother said.

"It's eleven in the morning."

"That's not an answer."

"I had tea."

"Tea." My mother produced the specific tone she reserved for inadequate nutrition.

I smiled at my desk, at the rendered green roofs. "Tell me about Liling," I said. "How is she?"

Liling was my grandmother. Eighty-eight, Zhonghe Memory Care, New Taipei. My grandmother had been at the care facility for three years, since the dementia had progressed to the point where living alone was no longer safe — and my mother visited twice a week and reported back in the careful, precise language of someone trying to convey a situation that did not fit standard language.

"She's having a hard week," Huimei said. "She keeps asking for you."

I felt something shift in my chest. Not quite guilt — I had been visiting when I could, which was not as often as I wanted, which was complicated by the facts of the situation in ways I couldn't explain. "Asking for me specifically?"

"By name. She keeps saying — she's confused, you know how she gets — but she says your name. And she says something about a garden. She says it a lot, actually. Some garden somewhere." My mother paused. "It's probably nothing. They get fixated on things."

I looked at my hands on the desk.

"I'll come," I said.

"You don't have to —"

"I want to. I have some flexibility this month. I'll book the flight."

My mother went quiet for a moment. Then, with the particular warmth that sat beneath all the mild accusation: "She'd like that. I think she'd be very happy to see you."

After I hung up, I sat for a long time at the desk with the rendered green roofs on the screen, thinking about the word *garden.* About what my grandmother was asking for. About the specific probability that it was coincidence and the specific probability that it was something else entirely, and the fact that I did not know, yet, which probability to believe.

I called the airline at noon. The flight left Friday morning.

I tried, for the rest of the day, to make the green roofs feel real. They stayed paper-thin.

---

In the afternoon I went to a site meeting at a development in Yaletown — a plaza redesign, earlier work of mine that the developer now wanted adjusted because the retail configuration had shifted. The changes were logical and I accommodated them professionally, walking the site with the developer's representative, a man named Curtis who wore the specific expression of someone who has been told the site's ecological features were non-negotiable and has spent two months looking for the negotiation anyway.

I walked the site. I read its argument: the drainage basin I'd engineered along the south edge, the tree-pits I'd designed with the soil volume the trees actually needed rather than the soil volume the parking layout preferred, the relationship between the building's shadow pattern and the seating areas I'd placed to catch the morning light. The site was doing what it was supposed to do. I felt a professional satisfaction in this, clean and familiar, the kind that didn't require any external validation — the drainage was working, the trees were establishing, the seating areas were, on the days I walked past them at lunch, actually occupied by people eating lunch. This was the measure I used. A plaza that people inhabited.

"We'd like to close off the south edge seating and add a retail kiosk," Curtis said.

I looked at the south edge. The south edge caught the morning light from seven-thirty to eleven. It was where the benches were most consistently occupied. I knew this because I had, in the months since installation, walked past this plaza twelve times at various hours and documented the occupancy patterns. This was not a formal methodology. It was a habit I had and could not stop having: going back to the sites. Checking.

"The south edge seating is in the highest-use quadrant," I said. "You'd be removing the most occupied element to add a retail kiosk that will compete with the coffee shop at the north entry."

Curtis looked at the south edge. He was counting the benches. Three benches, all of them occupied right now, in the mid-afternoon on a Tuesday — two people with laptops, one person reading a physical newspaper, which I noticed.

"The kiosk would generate revenue," he said.

"The occupied seating generates dwell time, which generates the revenue that makes a plaza commercially valuable. You remove the seating and you reduce dwell time and the kiosk you build performs worse than it would have." I said this without heat. I had been saying variations of this sentence for eight years. "I can provide you with occupancy data to support this analysis, if it helps."

Curtis looked at the benches. He looked at the person reading the newspaper with the expression of someone being forced to update a model.

"Send me the data," he said.

On the way back to the office, walking because I needed the air, I passed the park.

I always passed the park. I had chosen this office, six months ago, for the same reason I'd chosen the apartment: proximity. I had not articulated this to myself at the time, had told myself it was the lease terms and the natural light and the fact that the coffee shop on the ground floor was genuinely good. But I had been choosing proximity to the Garden for six months, had been orienting my life around it the way a plant orients itself around a light source, and this was something I had been aware of in a peripheral way and had not examined directly.

I slowed at the park's entrance. It was a Wednesday afternoon in May. People were walking dogs, pushing strollers, doing the ordinary calibrating things people do in parks. The park smelled of new-growth grass and Douglas fir from the stand in the northeast quadrant that predated the park's current design by a hundred years and would, if the park's management plan was followed correctly, predate its next redesign by another hundred.

I walked to the northeast quadrant. Past the firs. Through the clearing where the lantern-plants grew, low and dense, unremarkable to anyone who didn't know what they were.

I stood at the Garden's edge and looked in.

I didn't cross. I wasn't planning to cross today — I had work tomorrow morning, a meeting I couldn't miss. I just stood at the edge and looked at the threshold light, which was doing what it always did in the late afternoon: gathering in the space between the plants and the treeline in a way that had no good explanation in terms of the actual afternoon sun, which was coming from the wrong angle to produce this effect.

The Garden was there. It was what it was. It had been what it was for at least fifty years, long before I had found it.

I stood at its edge for three minutes — I counted — and then walked back through the park and caught the SkyTrain and went back to the office and spent the rest of the afternoon on the Burrard corridor files.

The green roofs were good work. They were my work, and my work was good, and I would not let the comparison with Jun's world make me dismissive of it. I would not do that.

But when I closed the laptop at six-thirty, the renderings were still paper-thin.
