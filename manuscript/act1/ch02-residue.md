# Residue

She woke in her own apartment on a Thursday morning with the specific confusion of someone who cannot account for the previous twenty-four hours, which was, by now, a familiar confusion. This did not make it comfortable.

The apartment was in East Vancouver, on a block she'd chosen because it was within walking distance of the park — the specific park, the specific corner of it, where the lantern-plants grew and where she'd first, six months ago, walked into the Garden without understanding what she was walking into. The apartment had good bones and bad windows and a view of the alley that she had never successfully made anything of, design-wise, which she considered an ongoing professional embarrassment. She had plants on every sill, most of them from her world and one, she'd noticed three months ago, that she couldn't identify in any Pacific Northwest guide and suspected she'd brought through on her shoes.

She lay still for a moment, doing the inventory. Her body felt the way it always felt after a crossing: the particular heaviness of a fatigue that was not quite physical, as though something slightly more essential than muscle had been worked. Her hands were fine. Her eyes were slow to focus. These were reliable indicators, not alarming ones; she'd been tracking them for six months.

What she could not account for: Wednesday morning.

She knew Wednesday had happened. She'd had a site review meeting for the Burrard corridor project — she could see, on her phone, a series of emails she'd sent on Wednesday afternoon that indicated the meeting had occurred. She had notes. She had a marked-up PDF. What she could not access was any experiential memory of Wednesday morning: the walk to the meeting, the coffee she'd presumably had, the particular quality of that morning's light, the specific faces of the junior staff who'd attended the review. Not amnesia — she had the afternoon, clear and sequential. Just a gap, a curtained interval, of maybe four hours.

This had been happening since the second month of crossings. She'd mapped it as carefully as she could: the gaps always covered peripheral time, not core professional function. She did not lose work. She did not lose conversations she'd had with people who needed her present. She lost ambient experience — the weather, the commute, the texture of an ordinary morning. She'd been reading it as the mind's editing function, prioritizing what had to be retained and releasing what could be spared.

She had not yet decided whether this interpretation was correct or whether it was something she'd constructed to make the gaps livable.

She made tea. She stood at the bad windows looking at the alley with the tea in her hands and tried to be somewhere specific, which was what she did now in her own world when she felt the displacement starting. She named what she could see: the recycling bins, the neighbor's cat sleeping on the fence in a position that could not possibly be comfortable, the way the rain from the previous night had pooled in the alley's low point. Ordinary. Existing. She was in her world, in her kitchen, in her body, on a Thursday morning in May.

She went to her office.

---

The firm was three stops on the SkyTrain, a walk across a plaza she'd helped design seven years ago — the drainage was still off at the northeast corner, she noted this every time and had stopped saying anything about it because the client had not wanted to hear it and the firm had agreed to sign off and this was one of the things she kept in a file in her mind labeled *decisions that were made over my objection and that I remain correct about but that no longer constitute an emergency.* The plaza looked fine. The northeast corner was subtly wrong in a way that only she and the rain knew.

Her desk was the kind of desk that tells you things about the person who occupies it: she had cleared the paperwork into organized stacks but the organization was the kind that required extensive personal decoding — files by project in sequence of complexity rather than alphabetically, reference materials annotated in three colors she'd long since forgotten the original coding of, a small collection of soil samples in labeled vials that she kept meaning to return to their respective sites and never did. Her colleagues treated her desk as a controlled substance: approaching it only when necessary, handling its contents with care.

She opened the Burrard corridor project. She had notes from Wednesday's meeting — good notes, professionally legible, indicating a version of herself that had been fully present and professionally competent. She read them with the slight dissociation of reading a document authored by a close colleague: recognizing the hand, recognizing the thinking, not quite accessing the experience of having written it.

The green roofs section. The client wanted green roofs on three of the five tower blocks — she'd been advocating for all five, which was the ecologically coherent choice, and the client had been pushing back on cost, which was the economically coherent choice, and this was the conversation she'd been having with real estate developers for eight years. She opened the renderings. They were good — she was good, the junior designer who'd done the visualization work was particularly good — and they showed what three green roofs would look like against what Vancouver's skyline had become.

She stared at the renderings for six minutes. They were technically correct. The ecological math was sound. The drainage modeling was precise, the species selection was climate-appropriate, the maintenance schedule was realistic. Everything was right.

The work felt paper-thin.

She had been trying to name this for two months. The closest she'd gotten was: her work in Jun's world has mass. When she built the hydrological model that morning — last night, whenever it had been — she had been working with something that was genuinely at risk, in a situation where the spatial analysis was the difference between a failing intervention and a viable one. The stakes were real in the way that the word *real* only partially covers: ecological collapse is happening now, on a specific hillside, to specific trees that are dying in a way that is preventable if the analysis is correct. Her work there lands on something solid.

The green roofs were good. The green roofs were not nothing. In her world, the right small decisions, made at scale across a decade, might prevent this world's trajectory from becoming her own. She believed this professionally. She had believed it in both worlds, the same belief. But in Jun's world, she was working on what had already become the emergency. Here, she was working on the argument that the emergency might be preventable.

The gap between those two things was not a professional gap. It was a different kind of gap entirely, and she didn't have good language for it yet.

Her phone rang at eleven-fifteen.

Her mother's face on the screen.

"Mā," Mei said.

"You didn't call Sunday."

"I know. I was in — I had a project thing. I'm sorry."

Her mother, Huimei, was sixty-two years old and lived in Taipei and had the specific telephone personality of someone who has decided to express love through mild accusation, which Mei had been navigating since her early teens and could now read with great accuracy. Behind the mild accusation was the actual message: *I thought about you this week, and I thought about you because I was worried, and I was worried because you've sounded tired, and I don't like that you're tired and far away.*

"Have you eaten?" her mother said.

"It's eleven in the morning."

"That's not an answer."

"I had tea."

"Tea." Her mother produced the specific tone she reserved for inadequate nutrition.

Mei smiled at her desk, at the rendered green roofs. "Tell me about Liling," she said. "How is she?"

Liling was her grandmother. Eighty-eight, Zhonghe Memory Care, New Taipei City. Her grandmother had been at the care facility for three years, since the dementia had progressed to the point where living alone was no longer safe — and Mei's mother visited twice a week and reported back in the careful, precise language of someone trying to convey a situation that did not fit standard language.

"She's having a hard week," Huimei said. "She keeps asking for you."

Mei felt something shift in her chest. Not quite guilt — she had been visiting when she could, which was not as often as she wanted, which was complicated by the facts of the situation in ways she couldn't explain. "Asking for me specifically?"

"By name. She keeps saying — she's confused, you know how she gets — but she says your name. And she says something about a garden. She says it a lot, actually. Some garden somewhere." Her mother paused. "It's probably nothing. They get fixated on things."

Mei looked at her hands on the desk.

"I'll come," she said.

"You don't have to —"

"I want to. I have some flexibility this month. I'll book the flight."

Her mother went quiet for a moment. Then, with the particular warmth that sat beneath all the mild accusation: "She'd like that. I think she'd be very happy to see you."

After she hung up, Mei sat for a long time at the desk with the rendered green roofs on the screen, thinking about the word *garden.* About what her grandmother was asking for. About the specific probability that it was coincidence and the specific probability that it was something else entirely, and the fact that she did not know, yet, which probability to believe.

She called the airline at noon. The flight left Friday morning.

She tried, for the rest of the day, to make the green roofs feel real. They stayed paper-thin.

---

In the afternoon she went to a site meeting at a development in Yaletown — a plaza redesign, earlier work of hers that the developer now wanted adjusted because the retail configuration had shifted. The changes were logical and she accommodated them professionally, walking the site with the developer's representative, a man named Curtis who wore the specific expression of someone who has been told the site's ecological features were non-negotiable and has spent two months looking for the negotiation anyway.

She walked the site. She read its argument: the drainage basin she'd engineered along the south edge, the tree-pits she'd designed with the soil volume the trees actually needed rather than the soil volume the parking layout preferred, the relationship between the building's shadow pattern and the seating areas she'd placed to catch the morning light. The site was doing what it was supposed to do. She felt a professional satisfaction in this, clean and familiar, the kind that didn't require any external validation — the drainage was working, the trees were establishing, the seating areas were, on the days she walked past them at lunch, actually occupied by people eating lunch. This was the measure she used. A plaza that people inhabited.

"We'd like to close off the south edge seating and add a retail kiosk," Curtis said.

She looked at the south edge. The south edge caught the morning light from seven-thirty to eleven. It was where the benches were most consistently occupied. She knew this because she had, in the months since installation, walked past this plaza twelve times at various hours and documented the occupancy patterns. This was not a formal methodology. It was a habit she had and could not stop having: going back to the sites. Checking.

"The south edge seating is in the highest-use quadrant," she said. "You'd be removing the most occupied element to add a retail kiosk that will compete with the coffee shop at the north entry."

Curtis looked at the south edge. He was counting the benches. Three benches, all of them occupied right now, in the mid-afternoon on a Tuesday — two people with laptops, one person reading a physical newspaper, which she noticed.

"The kiosk would generate revenue," he said.

"The occupied seating generates dwell time, which generates the revenue that makes a plaza commercially valuable. You remove the seating and you reduce dwell time and the kiosk you build performs worse than it would have." She said this without heat. She had been saying variations of this sentence for eight years. "I can provide you with occupancy data to support this analysis, if it helps."

Curtis looked at the benches. He looked at the person reading the newspaper with the expression of someone being forced to update a model.

"Send me the data," he said.

On the way back to the office, walking because she needed the air, she passed the park.

She always passed the park. She had chosen this office, six months ago, for the same reason she'd chosen the apartment: proximity. She had not articulated this to herself at the time, had told herself it was the lease terms and the natural light and the fact that the coffee shop on the ground floor was genuinely good. But she had been choosing proximity to the Garden for six months, had been orienting her life around it the way a plant orients itself around a light source, and this was something she had been aware of in a peripheral way and had not examined directly.

She slowed at the park's entrance. It was a Wednesday afternoon in May. People were walking dogs, pushing strollers, doing the ordinary calibrating things people do in parks. The park smelled of new-growth grass and Douglas fir from the stand in the northeast quadrant that predated the park's current design by a hundred years and would, if the park's management plan was followed correctly, predate its next redesign by another hundred.

She walked to the northeast quadrant. Past the firs. Through the clearing where the lantern-plants grew, low and dense, unremarkable to anyone who didn't know what they were.

She stood at the Garden's edge and looked in.

She didn't cross. She wasn't planning to cross today — she had work tomorrow morning, a meeting she couldn't miss. She just stood at the edge and looked at the threshold light, which was doing what it always did in the late afternoon: gathering in the space between the plants and the treeline in a way that had no good explanation in terms of the actual afternoon sun, which was coming from the wrong angle to produce this effect.

The Garden was there. It was what it was. It had been what it was for at least fifty years, long before she had found it.

She stood at its edge for three minutes — she counted — and then walked back through the park and caught the SkyTrain and went back to the office and spent the rest of the afternoon on the Burrard corridor files.

The green roofs were good work. They were her work, and her work was good, and she would not let the comparison with Jun's world make her dismissive of it. She would not do that.
