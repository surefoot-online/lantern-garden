### Chapter Thirteen: Found

She received a message from Jing-wei on a Monday morning, while she was on a work call with the green roof client — a call about the fifth tower, about whether the ecology argument would hold against the final cost analysis. It was a call she'd been having in various forms for three months and she was conducting it with the professional patience it required and reading Jing-wei's message with the portion of her mind the call didn't need.

*Dr. Park has shared the field journals that Kenji gave her before he chose to stay. He asked her to carry them back when she returned, said they were documentation for whoever came looking. I'm sending you scans tonight. He wrote in Japanese and in English — he switched languages at different points in a way that I think is meaningful but I don't yet understand. The English sections are remarkable. I think you should read them first.*

She finished the call — the fifth tower was approved, which was the right outcome and a conversation she'd been having for three months that was finally over — and waited for the scans.

They arrived at seven in the evening. She poured tea and sat at the kitchen table and opened them on her laptop.

Kenji's field journals were not what she'd expected. She'd expected something like Liling's TFRI reports — precise, methodological, the voice of someone documenting a scientific process. The journals were that, but they were also more than that. He was a scientist and he was also, she understood immediately, a person who had been taught to look at a place the way Thomas had been taught to look at it: not as a resource or a problem but as a relationship.

The scientific entries documented the network monitoring, the soil assessments, the species surveys, the specific sites where Liling was seeding and what she was seeding. The English sections — scattered through the Japanese, appearing when he was writing about something that he apparently thought in English rather than in Japanese, which meant they appeared when he was writing about the most important things — were the entries she read twice.

*July 1973: Walked the boulder field with L this morning before the survey team arrived. She showed me the hydrological path again — not on a map, in the terrain itself, the way the moisture-indicator species follow the buried drainage even through the substrate change. She can read the path just by looking at the surface. I told her this is the most extraordinary technical skill I have ever witnessed. She said: you read the network density from the soil chemistry, I read it from the surface. Different windows into the same room.*

*September 1973: She sealed the vessel today. I watched. She worked without notes — she had memorized the protocol, the species sequence, the layer order, the specific thickness of the resin seal. When she finished she held it in both hands for a moment and looked at it. I asked: do you think someone will find it? She said: if the crossing brings people who are paying attention to this forest, then eventually someone will find it who knows what to do with it. She was not making a prediction. She was stating a structural fact about how connected things eventually find each other.*

*November 1973: L is going back for the winter. She said she needs to be in her world — that there are things there that require her. I said: when will you return? She said: spring. The network is dormant in winter anyway. She is practical about the crossings in a way that I am still learning. She does not sentimentalize the going back. She goes back because her world needs her and the forest here needs the spring.*

*February 1975: I have decided. I told L today and she was not surprised. She said she could see it in how I walked the old-growth section, the way I've stopped calculating whether it's practical to stay. She said: when you stop calculating, you've decided. She's right. I have stopped calculating.*

*March 1975: L is leaving for the last time. She has the matrix prepared. We spent this morning at the cedar. She chose the depth, the orientation, the placement among the roots. She was exact about everything. I asked: how long do you think it will hold? She said: long enough. I asked: long enough for what? She said: for someone who is paying attention to come find it.*

*She crossed back at midday. I stood in the Garden for a while after she had gone. The light was the threshold light. I have been in this Garden three dozen times and it is still strange every time — the specific quality of an in-between space that is genuinely between.*

*I am going back to the north ridge tomorrow to begin the spring monitoring.*

*She is gone. The forest needs attention.*

She read the last entry three times. She read it sitting in her kitchen with the cold tea and the Vancouver evening making its ordinary progress outside the bad windows and she did not try to name what she felt because what she felt was larger than naming and she had learned, in the last several months, to let things be larger than naming when that was what they were.

He had stayed. He had stayed and he had spent the next thirty years monitoring the cedar and the network and the north ridge old-growth, and he had died in that forest in 2009 without knowing whether anyone would find what Liling had left.

She read the final English entry again.

*She is gone. The forest needs attention.*

She thought about the bridging sequence, about the eleven nodes above threshold, about Priya's characterization of the seven viable strains, about Thomas's plan for the acknowledgment ceremony at the cedar. She thought about Jun at the monitoring flags in the dark, his hand warm in hers, the headlamps making their overlapping circles.

The forest needs attention.

That was the sentence that had been true for fifty years and would be true for the fifty years after that and was not a tragedy, was not a complaint, was simply the operational reality of a thing that was alive and that needed what living things needed: to be seen, to be attended to, to have someone standing in it who understood what it was saying.

She thought: and we are paying attention. We are, right now, paying the most specific and expert attention we have.

She called Jun.

He picked up in two rings. Background sounds: the monitoring station, someone's voice at a distance.

"I have the field journals," she said. "Kenji's journals. Jing-wei had them from Dr. Park."

A brief silence. "What do they say?"

"He was — he documented everything. The seeding, the soil monitoring, the hydrological surveys. He was precise." She looked at the scans on her laptop screen. "He chose to stay. He wrote about the decision. He said he stopped calculating whether it was practical and that's when he knew." She paused. "He monitored the cedar for thirty years. He died in 2009 not knowing whether anyone would find it."

Jun was very quiet.

"He wrote about Liling in the English sections," she said. "He switched to English when he was writing about the most important things. He described her — the way she read the terrain, the way she prepared the matrix, the day she left." She looked at the final entry. "He wrote: 'She is gone. The forest needs attention.' And then he went back to the north ridge the next morning."

The silence from Jun's end had the specific quality she'd learned to read as his version of being moved — not the performance of emotion, not the avoidance of it, but the quiet of someone holding something very large and being careful with it.

"He sounds like you," she said. "Dr. Park said that. That you sound like him — patient. Working toward something that might not happen in your lifetime."

"Is that a compliment?"

"She meant it as one."

Another quiet. Then: "When are you coming?"

"Thursday," she said.

"The north ridge planting is Saturday. Thomas is preparing the acknowledgment."

"I'll be there."

She sat for a while after the call, looking at the scans on the screen. The journals. The field notes. The specific ordinary beauty of someone doing the work that needed doing and leaving a record of it, not for publication, not for institutional credit, just so that whoever came after would have the map.

The network is still alive. That's what matters.

She had heard her grandmother say that. She was going to make it true.

She read the journals again, more slowly, not for the information this time but for the voice. Kenji's English sections had a quality she kept returning to: a specificity of observation that she recognized as the same quality she applied to sites. He was not writing about Liling as a person in the conventional biographical sense — not her habits, not her preferences, not the things you described when you wanted to make someone present to a reader who hadn't met them. He was writing about her as a professional. The way she read the terrain. The way she organized field data. The way she made a decision and executed it without second-guessing the execution. The way she sealed the vessel.

She thought about what it meant to have been observed that way. Precisely, professionally, with the kind of attention that recorded what you were rather than what you seemed. She thought about the care in it — that Kenji's English sections were love letters of a sort, not written in romance's language but in the language of professional respect, which was, she had come to understand, a deeper language for people who cared most profoundly about work.

She read: *She does not sentimentalize the going back. She goes back because her world needs her and the forest here needs the spring.*

She read: *When you stop calculating, you've decided.*

She thought about the calculation she was running — the one about her own world, her own work, the timeline of both. She thought about what it would mean to stop calculating. She thought about whether she had already stopped without noticing.

She had not. She was still calculating. She could feel the calculation running in the background the way she'd once felt the productivity tracking she'd disabled at twenty-nine — the persistent accounting, the register of what was owed and to whom. She still calculated.

Which meant she had not decided.

Which meant there was still something to decide.

She set the laptop on the coffee table and sat back on the couch and looked at the bad windows and the alley beyond them — the neighbor's cat, who had persisted through the entire six months of this period in its characteristic positions on the fence, an anchor of ordinary continuity. She had been meaning to take the cat's photograph for weeks. She had not done it. This was, she decided, a small thing she would do this week.

She thought about what she was waiting to decide.

She thought about Jun saying: *I can want that without wanting you to be something you're not.* She thought about the weight in that sentence, the specific form of generosity in it — the acceptance of a limitation without making the limitation into a failure. She had been receiving that generosity for months and had not, she realized, said anything to acknowledge it directly. Not because she didn't want to. Because she'd been waiting to know if she could say more.

She still didn't know if she could say more.

But she could say something.

She texted Jun: *I read the journals. Kenji saw her the way you see things. Precisely, and from care.*

He responded in four minutes: *I know. I've been thinking about that.*

*He chose to stay.*

*Yes.*

*I understand why.*

A long pause. Then: *I'm glad you didn't.*

She looked at the screen. She looked at the cat on the fence. She thought about what it meant that she was glad too — that the gladness was specific, not vague. That she was glad she hadn't stayed in a way that told her something about why she hadn't.

She was glad because her world needed what she was bringing back to it.

She was glad because her grandmother needed what she was able to give.

She was glad because the work she was doing in her world — the green roofs, the Burrard corridor, the long slow argument for the right small decisions at scale — was work that mattered in its own right, not as a lesser alternative to the emergency work but as the work that prevented the emergency. She was glad because she was not in a dying forest. She was in a forest that was still ahead of dying and where her work was preventative rather than reconstructive, and there was a specific kind of gladness that lived in preventative work that she had been, for months, devaluing against the drama of the emergency.

She was not Kenji. She had not chosen to stay. She was Liling, or she was trying to be: the person who crossed, did the work, came home, and spent the rest of her career building the framework that would make what she'd done comprehensible to the world that eventually caught up.

She texted back: *I'm glad too.*

She set down the phone and sat for a while in the apartment, in the ordinary evening, in the specific version of her life that was hers — and she let herself be in it, fully, without the deflection of comparison or the weight of the obligation she was carrying. Just the apartment. Just the evening. Just the life that was here when she was here.

Tomorrow she would cross. Tonight she was in her world.

She went to the kitchen and made dinner from actual ingredients for the first time in three weeks, which required a brief reconnaissance of the refrigerator and a tactical decision about the onions, and she ate it at the kitchen table with Kenji's journals and thought about the passage where he described Liling identifying the moisture-indicator species along the drainage axis, the way she could read the path in the surface vegetation. She thought about standing in the southern corridor with Jun and reading the buried creek from the species above it. Different windows into the same room.
