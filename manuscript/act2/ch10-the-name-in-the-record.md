### Chapter Ten: The Name in the Record

Jing-wei's second report arrived on a Wednesday morning — twenty-three pages, meticulously sourced, organized in the precise structure of someone who understood that some of her findings required very clear separation between what the evidence established and what it implied.

She read it at her office desk, with the green roofs rendering open on the second screen, which she had stopped seeing.

*Item one: The Pacific Research Collective.*

*I have confirmed the existence of visa renewals for three researchers sponsored by the Pacific Research Collective, British Columbia: Dr. Wǎng Liling, Taiwan; Dr. Tanaka Kenji, Japan; Dr. Park Jinhee, South Korea. All three were in the Cascade zone, British Columbia, between 1972 and approximately 1975. The Collective does not appear in any Canadian corporate or charitable registry. It does not appear in any Canadian university or government records. It appears only in the visa renewal documentation of these three researchers.*

*I believe it was a constructed institution — an administrative fiction created to provide the documentation needed for extended research visa renewals. I cannot determine by whom it was constructed or for what purpose.*

*Item two: The researchers.*

*Dr. Tanaka Kenji was a forest ecologist affiliated with Kyushu University. His institutional record shows an extended sabbatical from 1971 to 1975, described as "international collaborative research." After returning to Japan in 1975, he published extensively on mycorrhizal network recovery in degraded temperate forest systems. His 1977 paper in the Japanese Forestry Society journal is notable for containing methodological approaches that closely parallel Dr. Wǎng's 1968-1972 work — work not available in Japanese translation at the time of publication.*

*Dr. Park Jinhee was a botanist affiliated with Seoul National University until 1971. Her SNU record shows a resignation in November 1971. She was in British Columbia from January 1972 to March 1974. After returning to Korea, she took a position at a regional university and worked there until her retirement in 2001. She is now seventy-eight years old. I have a contact address for her.*

*Item three: The missing-persons concern.*

*In November 1972, colleagues at the TFRI filed a welfare inquiry about Dr. Wǎng. She had indicated she would attend the Vancouver symposium and return within two weeks. She did not return. The TFRI administrative record shows a note describing the inquiry and a follow-up confirming she had attended the symposium's first day but had not been seen afterward. The note then closes: "Dr. Wǎng has indicated she will be conducting extended field research in the region and will be in contact when the research permits." There is no indication of when or how this information was communicated to the TFRI.*

*When she returned in 1975, the administrative note reads: "Dr. Wǎng has returned from an extended Canadian field research period. Specimens submitted. She reports in good health and intends to resume her position." The note is matter-of-fact. Someone decided to characterize three years of undocumented absence as an extended field research period and apparently no one chose to contest this characterization.*

She read this and thought about the specific kindness of that administrative decision. Someone — a colleague, a department head, someone who understood that Liling was worth having back and that demanding an explanation would be both futile and unkind — had written the language that permitted the return. *Extended field research period.* A door held open.

She had been doing a version of the same thing. She had been using the language that permitted her own continued professional life — the morning flights, the consulting basis — and what she'd been granted was not unkindness but the specific patience of people who trusted her and had decided to trust the work she was doing rather than the coherence of the story she was telling.

*Dr. Park may be willing to speak about the Collective and about her experience in the Cascade zone. I can facilitate contact if you wish.*

She wrote back: *Yes. Contact Dr. Park. Tell her I'm trying to understand the Pacific Research Collective and what the three researchers were doing. Tell her it matters.*

Then she called her mother.

"I need to come to Taipei," she said. "I need to see Nǎi Nai."

"Is something wrong?"

"No. I have questions I need to ask while she still can answer." She paused. "Māmā. I've found some things about the gap. The 1972-1975 period. Things I want to tell you in person."

Her mother was quiet for a long time. The quality of the silence was different from her usual silences — not the thoughtful pause, not the processing beat, but something larger. The silence of someone who has been waiting for a door and is now hearing it open.

"I'll have the guest room ready," Huimei said.

She crossed on a Thursday before the Taipei flight, spending two days in Jun's world with the morphological analysis Priya had run in the previous week. The preliminary results were what she'd hoped and more than she'd hoped: the culture contained eleven distinct fungal strains, of which seven were identifiable as historic northern Cascade corridor species — species present in the region's pre-fragmentation records, now absent from or severely reduced in the test zone. The remaining four strains were, Priya said, "novel to the regional literature." Which meant either that Liling had found them somewhere in the corridor that the literature hadn't documented, or that they were from somewhere the literature hadn't touched.

She did not say this to Priya. She added it to the list she was building.

"The viable strains are the seven corridor species," Priya said. "The four novel ones are interesting but not actionable at this stage — we don't know their ecological function in this system." She had the precise clarity of someone describing what she'd found rather than what she wanted to have found. "The seven corridor strains, cultured from this sample, could serve as a source for bridging inoculation. We'd need to establish a live culture first — weeks of work — but if we can do that, the species composition of the bridging seeding improves significantly."

"How significant?"

"It goes from 'establishing a synthetic network that approximates the original' to 'restoring what was actually here.'" Priya said it simply. "It's the difference between a functional facsimile and an actual restoration."

She looked at the analysis printout. Eleven strains. Seven of them the original network. Her grandmother had preserved, in a clay vessel with pine-resin seal, the composition of a forest that had been fragmenting for thirty years before the coalition had started its work. She had preserved it specifically enough, with sufficient precision of methodology, that fifty years later the living strains were identifiable, viable, recoverable.

She had known what was needed. She had done the thing that was needed. She had left it for someone to find.

"The bridging seeding sequence," Mei said. "If we incorporate the culture —"

"We sequence it from the north ridge out. The culture becomes the interior node and we seed toward it from the established inoculation sites in the southern corridor. Connect the outer ring to the inner source." Priya was already drawing on the nearest available surface — a paper towel, her default when she was thinking too fast for anything else. "If the seven corridor strains establish in the bridging zone, the network's composition shifts. It starts to have — it starts to be the network it was. Not a replacement network. The original one, reconstituting."

She thought about her grandmother's handwriting in the TFRI summary. *Details available on request.*

"Let's build the sequence," she said.

Jing-wei's contact with Dr. Park Jinhee produced a video call that she participated in from her Taipei hotel room, the morning before the care facility visit. Jing-wei had arranged it and sat in as translator, though Dr. Park's English was good — careful, precisely formal, the English of someone who had learned it for professional purposes and had kept it accurate rather than fluent.

Dr. Park was seventy-eight. She sat at a desk in a tidy room that had the specific organization of someone who had spent a career managing large quantities of information and had found a system and kept it. Behind her: shelves of journals. A window with a garden visible.

"You're Dr. Wǎng's granddaughter," she said.

"Yes."

"I thought someone would come eventually." She said it with the matter-of-fact quality of someone who has been holding an expectation for a long time. "I'm glad it's family."

"I've been researching the Pacific Research Collective," Mei said. "And the work the three of you did in the Cascade zone. I've found the TFRI records, the visa renewals, the oral history accounts from the people you worked with."

"Yes." Dr. Park's hands were folded on the desk. "What do you want to know?"

"Everything you can tell me."

Dr. Park looked at her hands. The camera was slightly below eye level — the angle of a laptop positioned on a desk — and through it Mei could see the shelf behind her, the window with its garden, the orderly compression of a working life kept organized. The call had the specific texture of transoceanic video: a half-second of acoustic delay that turned every pause ambiguous, a slight softness in the image that made the room feel more distant than Seoul actually was.

"I was twenty-nine when I went to the Cascade zone," Dr. Park said. "I had resigned from Seoul National University because —" She stopped. Looked at something off-screen. The pause was longer than the lag accounted for. "This is the part that is difficult to explain simply."

"I know," Mei said. "You don't have to simplify it."

Something shifted in the image — not quite a smile. Dr. Park's hands, still folded on the desk, turned slightly. The tell of someone recalibrating who they're talking to.

"I had found the Garden three years earlier, in 1969. I had been crossing for two years before I met Liling. I found her in the same forest by the same process that the Garden uses."

Mei went still. "The Garden finds people."

"The Garden connects people who are working on the same problem." Across the lag, Mei heard Dr. Park exhale — the sound arriving a beat after the image of her chest moving. "I don't claim to understand the mechanism. But in my experience, and from what Liling told me, and from what Kenji described: the crossing tends to bring together people who can be useful to each other and to the forest they've arrived in." She looked directly into the camera — the directness of someone who had learned to substitute eye-line for eye contact. "We were three scientists from three countries with three different institutional perspectives on the same ecological crisis, none of us officially affiliated with anything in that country, all of us doing the most important work of our careers."

"The Pacific Research Collective —"

"Was Kenji's idea." She reached off-screen. When her hand returned it held nothing — a gesture abandoned, or perhaps just the habit of someone used to having paper nearby when they talked. "He had a connection, someone in the Canadian federal bureaucracy who was sympathetic, who understood what we were doing and wanted to help us stay without wanting to know more than he needed to know. The Collective provided the visa documentation." A pause. "The Collective was not a deception. It was the administrative surface of a real collaboration. We were doing real research. We were in the real forest."

"What did you leave behind?"

"I left a soil analysis methodology. Monitoring protocols." Dr. Park looked at the garden through her window — Mei could track her gaze from the slight turn of her head. "Kenji — I think you know what Kenji left." The delay swallowed the next beat of silence before Dr. Park continued. "Liling left the matrix. She spent eighteen months preparing it. She consulted with Kenji on the preservation method. She wanted a long-term viable culture — not three years, not ten." Her gaze came back to the camera. "Fifty years minimum. She told me once that she was planting something for whoever came after us. She said whoever came after would be equipped to use what we couldn't finish."

"I found it," Mei said.

Dr. Park's face changed. The formal precision softened, and underneath it was the expression of someone who has been carrying something for a long time and has just put it down. "You found it."

"The cedar on the north ridge. Fifty-two years. It was still viable."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "Tell me about the forest. What state is it in?"

Mei told her. The coalition and the beetle advance and the mycorrhizal fragmentation and the clearcut litigation and the south site's inoculation and the seven viable strains from the preserved culture. She told it the way she told the technical things: in order, without embellishment, the facts organized spatially. She told it the way her grandmother had written her papers.

Dr. Park listened.

When Mei finished, Dr. Park said: "She would have wanted to know that someone found it in time."

"The culture can still contribute to the restoration," Mei said. "If the characterization is correct, the seven corridor strains can be cultured into a live inoculant and incorporated into the bridging seeding sequence. The network the coalition is trying to build can be built from the network's own original composition."

Dr. Park looked at her. "She was extraordinary," she said. "Liling. I've worked with many scientists. I've never worked with anyone else who could see fifty years ahead with the same clarity she used to read what was in front of her."

"She's still alive," Mei said. "She's in a care facility in Taipei. She has dementia. But she has her windows."

Another silence. "Does she know you found it?"

"I'm going to see her this afternoon," Mei said. "I'm going to tell her."
