### Chapter Ten · The Botanist's Record

Jing-wei found me through the TFRI archivist, which was not how I had planned it.

I had planned to post a research query to one of the academic history networks — a careful, impersonal request for a Taipei-based research assistant familiar with mid-century botanical institutional records. This is how I would have handled it in any professional context: appropriate channels, clear scope, compensated engagement. I had drafted the query on the plane back from Jun's world and revised it twice.

The TFRI archivist emailed me the following day with a short note: *I mentioned your query to a researcher I know who is doing her doctoral work on Taiwanese women scientists of the postwar period. She may be interested. With your permission, I'll share your contact.*

The email from Jing-wei arrived two hours later.

*Dear Ms. Chen, Dr. Feng mentioned your inquiry about Wǎng Liling. I have been researching Dr. Wǎng's career as part of my dissertation chapter on women in Pacific Rim forest ecology, 1960–1980. I know her published record in detail. The gap in her institutional affiliation from 1972–1975 is something I have also noticed, and have not been able to explain. If you are looking for a research assistant on this question, I would be very interested to help. My rate is [she listed a number]. I can begin this week.*

I called her that afternoon.

She was younger than I expected from the formality of her email — early twenties, a doctoral student at National Taiwan University, serious in the way of someone who has chosen a difficult research subject and is not going to be casual about it. She had been working on the 1960–1980 generation of Taiwanese women scientists for two years. Wǎng Liling was, she said, one of the most interesting cases in the cohort: genuinely significant work, early career recognition, and then a gap followed by a return that was curiously underdocumented.

"I've tried to fill in the gap," Jing-wei said. "I contacted the TFRI, I looked at the conference records, I looked at the Canadian university archives for any record of a visiting researcher with her name or affiliation. I found the 1973 conference registration and then — nothing. She's simply not in the record for those three years."

"Do you think the record is incomplete?"

"I think the record is accurate," Jing-wei said. "There are researchers from that period who were away for extended fieldwork and whose institutional record shows a gap — that's not unusual. What's unusual is that she's not at any institution. She's not listed as a visiting researcher anywhere in North America that I can find. She was not at the University of British Columbia, not at Simon Fraser, not at Oregon State. If she was doing fieldwork for the TFRI under their affiliation, she should appear in Canadian records as a foreign researcher." A pause. "She doesn't."

"What about visa records?" I asked. "I've heard she renewed her Canadian visa three times during that period."

"I haven't been able to access Canadian immigration records from that period — they're not digitized at the level I would need. But the visa renewals you've described would be unusual for someone not attached to an institution. You typically need institutional sponsorship for a research visa renewal." Another pause. "Unless she had a different kind of documentation."

"What kind?"

"I don't know. That's what I can't figure out."

I hired her on the spot. I gave her my budget for the next four weeks and told her what I was looking for: anything about Wǎng Liling's movements between 1972 and 1975, any institutional records or correspondence that might illuminate the gap, and anything about the seed specimens she brought back.

"The specimens are in the TFRI collection," Jing-wei said. "I've seen them. But you said she brought back a mycorrhizal matrix? That's not in the collection database."

"I know. She mentioned it in her 1975 summary as being preserved off-site, in situ. I don't know what that means."

"In situ would mean at the field site." I could hear her thinking. "She left it where she collected it?"

"I think so."

Silence. Then: "That would be — that's an extraordinary choice for a botanist. You bring specimens home. That's the whole methodology. You don't leave a significant collection in the field."

"Unless you can't bring it home," I said.

The pause was longer this time. "What do you mean?"

"I'm not sure yet. Can you look into the specimens first? The registration codes from the 1975 submission — I'll send them to you. And anything else in the TFRI archive from her 1975 return. Anything she filed, any correspondence."

"Yes. I'll start today."

---

The papers were dense, but once I was inside the methodology I could move through them. Liling's 1968–1972 body of work was more substantial than I had initially appreciated — not just seventeen papers but the underlying data sets that several of them drew from, the field notes that were partially archived, a conference presentation from 1970 that was transcribed in one of the forestry journals. I read all of it over the course of four days, alongside the coalition's substrate work, alongside my firm's project queue that I was managing at increasing remove.

She was, to be precise, doing something that the field would not name for another twenty years.

The mycorrhizal network — the underground fungal web that connects individual trees and allows them to exchange nutrients, water, and chemical signals — had been observed in laboratory conditions for decades. What Liling was doing was documenting it in field conditions, across scale, across species, across what she called *connectivity gradients*: the varying densities of network connection that correspond to varying ecological health. A forest at high connectivity gradient is a forest in conversation with itself. A forest at low connectivity gradient is a forest of isolated individuals, each fighting its own struggle, the collective intelligence of the system lost.

She was measuring the difference between a forest that knows itself and a forest that doesn't.

The methodology was rigorous. The data collection was meticulous. And the conclusions she drew were quietly radical: she was arguing, in 1971, that the connectivity gradient was more predictive of forest resilience than any individual species health indicator, and that any intervention aimed at restoring forest health had to begin with the network, not the individual tree.

She was right. I know she was right because I have been watching Jun's coalition try to operate from the same principle, with the benefit of fifty years of subsequent science to ground them, and the work is still the hardest thing I have seen anyone attempt.

Liling figured it out in 1971, in Taiwanese, in a journal that international literature did not read.

On the fourth day, I called my mother again.

"Did Nǎi Nai ever talk about her papers?" I asked. "Her research?"

"Not to me, not really. When I was young she was always working, and what she was working on was — it was not the kind of thing you explain to a child. Soil science. Underground networks." My mother paused. "She would sometimes talk about the forest the way other people talk about music. Like it was doing something, all the time, that you could only hear if you listened correctly."

"She was ahead of the field by twenty years," I said. "What she was documenting in the early seventies — the scientific establishment didn't catch up until the nineties."

Silence. "Is that so."

"Significantly so." I paused. "She must have known she was working faster than the field could follow her."

"Maybe." My mother's voice had something careful in it. "She never — she never seemed frustrated by that. She seemed patient. Like she was planting something and was willing to wait for it to grow." A pause. "That's a strange thing to say about a scientist."

"No," I said. "I don't think it is."

---

Jing-wei's first report came three days later: eight pages, meticulously footnoted, organized in the clear prose of a doctoral student who has been trained to distinguish between what is established and what is inferred.

The established facts: Liling had registered for the 1973 Vancouver symposium and did not present. Her visa was renewed in January 1974, July 1974, and February 1975. The renewing institution was listed as "Pacific Research Collective, British Columbia" — an organization that, as far as Jing-wei could determine, had no registered presence in Canadian records before 1972 or after 1977. It appeared in two other visa renewal documents from the same period, both for Taiwanese researchers, both in the forest ecology field.

The Pacific Research Collective.

An institution that existed only in visa paperwork. An institution that apparently sponsored at least three Taiwanese botanists for extended research stays in British Columbia in the early 1970s. An institution that had no registered address, no published output, no presence in any archive Jing-wei could access.

She had written, at the bottom of the report: *This looks like a front organization. I cannot determine for what.*

I wrote back: *Can you find the other two researchers? The ones with the same sponsor?*

She responded within the hour: *I'm already looking.*
