### Chapter Seventeen · Garden and Woman

I flew to Taipei on a Friday morning.

The flight from Vancouver takes twelve hours, and I spent most of them in the particular suspended state of long-haul travel — not quite sleeping, not quite working, processing in the mode that I've learned to use when I have something large to hold. I read Liling's papers again on my laptop. I read the coalition's field log. I read Jing-wei's full research report, which now ran to thirty pages and which she had structured as a proper historical document, with sources and inference clearly distinguished.

Jing-wei had been in contact with Dr. Park Jinhee, who was seventy-eight years old and living outside Busan and who had, after a period of careful consideration, agreed to a video call. The call had happened while I was in Jun's world and Jing-wei had sent me a summary:

*Dr. Park was in the Cascade zone from January 1972 to March 1974. She confirms the Pacific Research Collective's existence and says it was not a front for anything sinister — her words. It was an informal network of researchers who had all, in her description, "found the same path." She said this several times: "the same path." She would not explain what she meant and I did not want to press her.*

*She says Dr. Wǎng Liling was the most technically gifted mycorrhizal scientist she encountered in her career. She says Dr. Tanaka Kenji was a careful ecologist and a thoughtful person and that she could see why Dr. Wǎng trusted him. She says she returned to Korea because she had a family she had left, and that this is the decision she has always felt the weight of.*

*"Some of us came back," she said, at the end of the call. "Some of us stayed. I came back. I think Liling came back for the same reason I did. Not because we stopped loving the work. Because we had something in our own world that the other world couldn't give us."*

*She would not specify what. I thanked her and ended the call.*

---

The care facility in the morning had the same smell. I was inside it now — I knew it, the layered components of it, the starch and floral and antiseptic underneath — and the knowing changed the smell's quality from environment to information. A space that you have walked through enough times begins to tell you things. The corridor that led to Liling's wing: the particular quality of light from the south-facing windows, warm even on a gray morning, the snake plant in its pot by the common room door, the sounds of the facility's morning routine.

She was awake.

She was sitting by the window in the sea-glass chair, looking at the light on the wall the way she does — attending to something. I said her name softly and she turned, and there was the moment: the moment of contact, the person who is known registering the presence of someone known. The "ah" of acknowledged arrival.

I sat in the family chair. I took her hand.

We sat for a while in the way we sit — the silence that is not uncomfortable because it has been inhabited enough times to become its own kind of room. The light moved on the wall. From the corridor, the distant sounds of the morning.

I had planned what I was going to say. I had practiced the version of it that was most accessible, that used the simplest language, that did not require her to follow complex chains of inference. I had thought about the windows — the lucid ones, the ones where she was present — and I had thought about how to ask a question in a way that might survive the gap between her world and mine.

I said: "Nǎi Nai. Do you remember the cedar?"

She was looking at the light on the wall. Her hand was warm in mine.

"The one with the split trunk," I said. "On the ridge."

A long silence. The light moved.

Then her hand tightened on mine. Not urgently — with the deliberate pressure of someone emphasizing a point.

"The cedar," she said.

"Yes."

Her chin lifted slightly. The angle I had seen in the June visit — the field-station angle, the woman looking at something worth looking at. "The one with the two trunks," she said. "Above the survey zone."

"Yes," I said. My voice was very even. "Do you remember where it is?"

She was quiet for a moment. Her eyes, which had been soft, sharpened — not the alert recognition-sharpness of the lucid window, but a different quality: the focus of someone reaching for something they stored very carefully. "North of the boulder field," she said. "You walk to the boulder field and then you go north. The ridge runs east-west. The cedar is at the highest point of the ridge. You'll see the split trunk before you're close enough to touch it."

I was writing it in my notebook. I had the notebook open on my knee, pen in hand, because I had known — I had been almost certain — that this was possible, that the core memory was still there, that the coordinates were in the part of Liling that the dementia couldn't reach.

"What's below the cedar?" I asked.

"The roots," she said. "The package is with the roots. On the north side — the side that faces the slope. The bark is wrapped in more bark." She was speaking with the matter-of-fact precision of a field researcher describing cache coordinates. "You'll need a trowel. It's not deep. I didn't want to risk the network."

"We'll be careful."

"Yes." She settled back. The focused quality was still there, but softer now. "The network is still alive. I checked — I checked the readings before I came back." A pause. "It may need help by now."

"It does," I said. "Jun's coalition is working on it."

She looked at me with an expression I had not seen before — an expression that was not dementia and was not confusion and was not even quite the lucid-window clarity. It was something older and steadier than any of those things. Recognition, not of me as her granddaughter, but of me as something continuous with herself.

"You found the garden," she said. It was not a question.

I couldn't speak for a moment. "Yes," I said. "I found it."

"Good." She patted my hand — a single pat, the gesture of someone confirming a satisfactory outcome. "I've been waiting for that. I knew you would. You always know what a space is doing."

"Nǎi Nai." My voice was careful. "How long have you known I was crossing?"

"Since you started showing the gaps," she said. "The tiredness. The smell." She looked at her own hand, resting in mine. "I have the same smell. I can always smell it on myself, and now I can smell it on you." She was quiet for a moment. "It runs in the maternal line. I didn't know that when I crossed. I learned it after — I went looking, through the records that the Garden keeps. There are records. Not like ours. But if you look the right way, you can see who has crossed before you."

"I didn't know there were records."

"There are many things you don't know yet." She said it without condescension — with the gentleness of someone who is not surprised by the extent of what remains to be learned and is not impatient with it. "You have time."

"Nǎi Nai," I said. "Tell me about Kenji."

She was very still.

Then, slowly, her face changed. Not the navigational-confusion look. Not the lucid-window sharpness. Something third — the expression of someone remembering something they have kept very carefully, the care itself visible in the quality of the attention.

"He was patient," she said. "More patient than I was. He understood the work at a level that—" She paused. "We were both ahead of our fields. We understood each other." Another pause. "He was the one who said to plant the matrix. He said: if you leave it, someone will find it. Someone who has crossed will find it, because they will know to look." She looked at the light on the wall. "He was right. You're here."

"He crossed too," I said.

"Yes. All three of us — Park Jinhee, Kenji, me." She said it with the simplicity of a fact so long held that it had gone past the need for emphasis. "We found each other the way people find each other when they're looking for the same thing. The Garden doesn't —" She frowned slightly, reaching for language. "It doesn't direct you. But it puts you where the work is. If you're paying attention, you find the others who are paying attention."

The room was very quiet. The corridor sounds were distant. The morning light lay across the wall in its yellow-warm rectangle, steady, not moving.

"Kenji," she said again. Not a question this time. A placement — the name in the space of her interior geography, in the position it had always occupied. "He stayed. I came back." She looked at me, and for a moment the expression in her eyes was something I had no name for: loss and completion and something past both of them. "I came back because of your mother. She was —" She made a small gesture with her free hand, her fingers spreading — the gesture I had seen in the April visit, the gesture like something rooting. "She was already becoming. I couldn't — I couldn't leave her to become without me."

"You made the right choice," I said.

"I made the choice I made," she said. "Right is what you learn about it later." She patted my hand again. "You're going to find the cedar."

"Yes."

"Good." She settled back into the chair, and some quality of the focused attention began to soften, the way a focused light softens when you move the source. She was still present but beginning to be present in a different way — the way of someone who has said what they needed to say and is now releasing the effort. "The network is still alive. That's what matters."

I sat with her for another hour. She drifted — not into agitation, just into the quieter mode, the attending-to-something-I-cannot-see. She mentioned Mrs. Tan's tomatoes. She asked if I had closed the lab window. She looked at the light on the wall with the expression of someone watching a slow and interesting piece of evidence.

When I rose to leave, she said, without turning from the window: "Tell Jun hello."

I stood still.

"He sounds like Kenji," she said. "Patient. Working toward something that might not happen in his lifetime." She was still watching the light. "Tell him it matters that he keeps going. Tell him from me."

"I will," I said.

"Good," she said. And then she was somewhere else, the internal somewhere, and I was standing at the door with my notebook and the coordinates she had given me, and the light was on the wall, and the city outside was warm and bright and nothing like Vancouver, and I walked down the corridor and past the snake plant and through the door and out.

I called Jun from the street outside.

He picked up on the second ring. I could hear the monitoring station's background sounds — the hum of equipment, someone's voice at a distance.

"I have the coordinates," I said.

A long silence.

"Jun."

"I'm here." His voice was very even — the voice he uses when something has happened that he's processing in real time and doesn't want to respond to until he's certain of the response. "You have the coordinates."

"North of the boulder field. The ridge runs east-west. The cedar is at the highest point, you can see the split trunk before you're close enough to touch it. The package is on the north side, in the roots, wrapped in birch bark. Not deep."

Another silence. "She remembered."

"She remembered."

"Mei." His voice, now, had something I didn't have a word for — something past the professional steadiness, past the controlled emotion, something that was simply a person receiving news that was very large. "That's not nothing."

"No," I said. "It's not nothing."

The street around me was Zhonghe mid-morning — the traffic, the sound of a market somewhere nearby, the warm smell of food from an open door. I stood on the sidewalk and held the phone and felt the coordinates in my notebook, Liling's handwriting transcribed into my own, the precise north-side-of-the-roots specificity of a scientist who had known, when she buried it, that someone would one day need to find it.

"She said to tell you hello," I said. "She said it matters that you keep going."

Jun was quiet for a long time. Then: "Thank you."

"She says you sound like him. Like Kenji."

"Patient?"

"Working toward something that might not happen in your lifetime."

He was quiet again. "She's right," he said. "About both things."

I looked up at the sky above Zhonghe, which was blue and clear and nothing like Jun's smoke-hazed orange. "I'll come Thursday," I said. "We'll go to the ridge."

"Yes," he said. "Thursday."

I stood on the sidewalk a moment longer, holding the phone after the call had ended, and I thought about Liling in the sea-glass chair with her hand in mine and the field-station angle of her chin and the way she had said *he stayed, I came back* — not with grief, not with regret, but with the particular completeness of someone who has carried something for fifty years and has found, at last, the person to whom it can be given.

She had given me the coordinates.

She had given me Kenji's name in its full weight.

She had given me the thing I had not known I was missing: the knowledge that someone had been here before me, had crossed the same crossing, had loved in a place that was not her world, had come back, and had not come back diminished.

She had come back with what she found and planted it in the ground for someone to find fifty years later.

I put my phone in my pocket and walked toward the MRT, and I thought: *I want the Tribunal to understand that I knew, at this moment, that the next question I had to ask would not be "which world do I live in." It would be "who am I, that this is my inheritance."*

The street curved. The city held its warm morning. Behind me, somewhere up the hill, Liling was watching the light on her wall, attending to something.

The network was still alive. She had checked.
