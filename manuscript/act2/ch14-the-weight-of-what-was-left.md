### Chapter Fourteen: The Weight of What Was Left

*From the testimony record, Session Two: "You've asked me to characterize the state of my understanding at the conclusion of the second phase of crossings. I want to be specific: I understood the origin of the work. I understood what my grandmother had done and why and what she had left for me to find. I understood that I was not the first and that the Garden had a logic that was older than I was and more patient than I had previously given it credit for. What I did not yet understand was what the Tribunal was, exactly, or what testimony to them would require. I knew they were watching. I knew eventually they would ask. I was not afraid of the asking."*

---

The planting at the cedar happened on a Saturday morning when the sky was overcast but not threatening — the specific grey of a temperate morning that held its moisture in suspension without releasing it, the light diffuse and even, the kind of light that made the forest look exactly like what it was.

Thomas had prepared a text that he spoke in two languages: first in his family's language, which she did not understand but which she received in the register that such things were meant to be received — not as content but as a form of address, a naming of the relationship between the people present and the land they were standing on. Then in English, which was briefer and which said, essentially: we are doing this because it is right to do it, and because the people who came before us understood that it was right to do it, and because we are inheriting an obligation that is older than our names.

She stood with Jun and Priya and Thomas and two of the field technicians at the base of the cedar and listened, and the forest was the forest — the birds doing their morning accounting, the understory moving in whatever air was moving through it, the soil beneath them doing what soil did: everything.

The planting was precise. Priya had cultured the seven viable strains into a live inoculant that could be delivered through the same methodology as the coalition's standard mycorrhizal seeding. The cedar's root zone was inoculated with the culture derived from Liling's preserved matrix, and the inoculation points were marked with the same orange flags the coalition used for all the monitoring sites.

When it was done, they stood for a moment.

She had been thinking, all morning, about what her grandmother had said about Kenji: *he stayed, I came back.* She had been thinking about Dr. Park's words: *some of us came back because we had something in our own world that the other world couldn't give us.* She had been thinking about what the something was, for her — what it was in her world that this world, with its specific beauty and its specific work and the person beside her who had stopped calculating, could not give.

She thought: the green roofs. The plaza drainage that was still wrong. The Burrard corridor. The work that was preventing the trajectory in her world from becoming what she was standing inside of in this one.

She thought: my mother, who has been waiting to know something she didn't know could be found.

She thought: my grandmother, in a sea-glass chair, watching light on a wall, having given away the coordinates and the name and the explanation, having waited until she was found.

She thought: both. The inheritance that ran in two directions. The work in this world that was her grandmother's work continued, and the work in her world that was preventing her world from needing this work. She was the crossing — not just the person who crossed but the connection itself, the bridge between the two trajectories.

She was going to be the bridge for as long as the Tribunal permitted.

She was going to tell the Tribunal what she'd found and why it mattered, and she was going to do it with the precision her grandmother had applied to the sealing of the matrix: every layer in the right place, every seal correctly made, the thing inside preserved because the container was correct.

She looked at the orange flags. Five of them in the cedar's root zone, marking the inoculation points, the coalition's standard orange bright against the cedar's dark-rust bark. The flags were vivid in the diffuse grey morning. She thought about what Kenji would have thought, seeing the flags. He had spent thirty years monitoring this site with no flags, with no coalition infrastructure, with only the knowledge that something was here that mattered and that mattering required continued attention. The flags were new. The care behind them was not.

She thought about what she was going to say to the Tribunal about care. She thought about how to say it precisely — not as sentiment, but as data. The fact of sustained attention was itself a form of evidence. You monitored something for thirty years because you had grounds to believe the monitoring mattered. The grounds were not bureaucratic or institutional. They were the grounds of someone who had seen the mechanism at work and understood that the mechanism was real and that real mechanisms needed to be maintained.

This was the argument. Not the argument in the form of an argument — she was not going to stand in the grey room and argue. She was going to describe. She was going to give the Tribunal the description of what had happened, precisely and in order, and let the description be what it was: the record of people paying attention to something that needed attention, over fifty years, across two worlds, because it needed it and because they could.

Priya was packing the soil kit. Luca was photographing the flagged sites. Jun was talking with Thomas in the low tones of two people confirming details that are significant enough to warrant a final review. The field technicians were doing what field technicians did: making themselves useful without being asked, moving efficiently in the space around the more senior team members.

She stood at the edge of the cedar's root zone and thought about her grandmother's hands, fifty-two years ago, placing the birch-bark vessel in this exact position with the specific precision of someone who has memorized a protocol and is executing it for the first and only time, knowing it needs to be right.

She thought about the morning her grandmother had stood at the edge of the Garden, in the photograph she'd found on the bedside table, with the slight lean forward and the weight on the front foot and the beginning of motion caught at the threshold.

Her grandmother had done this before. Had stood at the edge of something and then stepped into it, not because the outcome was certain but because the work was necessary and the work required presence.

She thought about presence — the quality of it that appeared only when you were doing the work that was yours to do.

She was doing that work.

---

She had one more conversation with Jun before she crossed back — at the end of the day, in the analysis room, the maps on the wall showing the corridor in its current state: eleven established nodes, the bridging sequence planned, the north ridge inoculation complete and waiting for its establishment indicators to declare.

"I want to tell you something," she said.

He looked at her.

"I've been reading the journals. Kenji's. He described making his decision to stay. He said: when you stop calculating, you've decided." She paused. "I'm still calculating."

He was quiet for a moment, with the patience she'd come to understand as the quality of his attention rather than just his disposition — the space he created around difficult things, the refusal to fill it with the first available word.

"What are you calculating?" he said.

"Whether I've understood what the crossing is for. Whether I'm using it for the right things. Whether the work I'm doing in my world is — not equivalent to what you're doing here, but genuinely necessary in its own right." She looked at the map. "I've been, for months, reading my world's work against yours and finding it less urgent. Paper-thin, I used to think. The green roofs and the corridor design and the plaza drainage. Work that prevents the emergency rather than responding to it."

"And now?"

"And now I understand that preventing the emergency is the only version of this story where it doesn't happen." She looked at him. "Kenji stayed because the emergency was already here and the forest needed someone to stay. Liling crossed and came back because her world hadn't reached the emergency yet and there was still time to apply what she'd learned before it did. They were doing the same work. Different phases."

"And you," he said.

"I'm doing both. I'm in the emergency with you. And I'm going back to the prevention work. Both." She paused. "That's the crossing. That's what it's for. Not escape and not replacement. Connection. Between the phase that hasn't arrived yet and the phase that's already here."

He looked at the map for a long moment. "And when the Tribunal asks you what you were doing," he said.

"I'll tell them I was being the connection," she said. "I'll tell them it's what my grandmother was. It's what Kenji was, from the other direction. It's what the Garden has been facilitating, if the crossing has logic rather than randomness." She paused. "I'll tell them the forest in both worlds is better for it. I'll let that be the argument."

"Start with the fourteen nodes," he said.

"Start with the fourteen nodes," she agreed.

She looked at the map one more time. The corridor in its current state: damaged, fought for, beginning — just beginning — to show the signs of something that might hold. The network finding its language again. The forest starting to be what it was.

She crossed back the following morning.

---

She was going to give them the geometry and let it speak.

Jun found her hand again at the cedar, the way he had at the monitoring flags, the specific ease of it. She had learned in the last three weeks that this was not a resolution of anything — not the resolution of which world she lived in or what the crossing meant or what was required on both sides of it. It was the recognition of something that was possible within the situation she was actually in, rather than the situation she might have preferred. She had been, her whole career, a person who worked with the situation that was actually there rather than the situation the rendering showed. She was doing that now.

"It's planted," he said.

"It's planted."

"Now we wait."

"Three months, minimum, before the network bridge shows establishment indicators," she said. "Then another growing season before we know whether the corridor strains are integrating with the current inoculation."

"So we keep working."

"So we keep working."

The forest was the forest. The cedar was the cedar, doing what it had been doing for three hundred years, and at its root zone the culture that Liling had prepared fifty-two years ago was beginning its slow, patient work of reconnection. Below the forest floor the network was sending its signals, the chemical language of connected things, the forest accounting for itself.

She stood in it with Jun, in the grey morning light, and she thought about what she was going to say to the Tribunal. She thought about the grey room and the manufactured neutrality and the woman who had listened to too much testimony. She was going to walk into that room and she was going to tell them about a botanist from Taipei who had found the Garden in 1972 and spent three years preparing a gift for a forest that didn't yet know it needed it, and she was going to tell them about the seven viable strains and the cedar on the north ridge and the man who had stayed to monitor the cedar for thirty years and the coalition that had been fighting for three years before the gift was found, and she was going to tell them that the crossing had been used, from the beginning, to carry what was needed from where it existed to where it was needed.

That was the argument. That was the geometry.

She looked at the orange flags around the cedar's root zone. The marked sites. The evidence, in the spatial language she knew, of something being established.

"Thank you," Jun said. "For finding it."

She thought about the photograph on the bedside table. The young woman at the edge of the Garden, facing the light. The fifty years between that photograph and the present morning.

"She found it first," she said. "I just found what she left."
