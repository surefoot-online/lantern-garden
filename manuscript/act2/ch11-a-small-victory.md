### Chapter Eleven · A Small Victory

The inoculation took.

Not everywhere — Jun had not expected everywhere — but in the nodes closest to the test site's center, where the substrate was marginally better and the microclimate disruption from the clearcut had not yet fully propagated, the early indicators were clear. Sera came to find us in the monitoring station on a Thursday afternoon with the expression she uses when something has happened that she had considered possible but not probable: a kind of controlled disbelief, as though she doesn't want to believe it too strongly before the data firms up.

"Look at this," she said, and put her tablet on the table between us.

The mycorrhizal density readings at seven of the twelve primary nodes were above the establishment threshold. Above it, not at it — above it by a margin that Sera, in her cautious language, described as "statistically significant but requiring confirmation over the next monitoring cycle." She had been watching these nodes for six weeks. Six weeks ago they had been showing faint chemical signals, the barest evidence of network formation. Now they were — not healthy. Nothing in this zone was healthy. But they were connected.

They were talking to each other.

Jun looked at the readings for a long time. He did not say anything. His processing face: that particular stillness.

"The supplementary inoculation worked," Sera said. "The northern buffer nodes held. The microclimate impact is real — we're seeing it at the three exposed nodes on the east side, and we're going to lose those, I think. But the central nodes are past the threshold."

"How long to the next monitoring cycle?" Jun asked.

"Three weeks. We'll know more then. But—" She looked at the readings again. "If this is what I think it is, Jun. If this holds." She didn't finish the sentence. She was being careful, the way she is always careful, the way of someone who has lost enough to know that naming a good thing too early is its own kind of vulnerability.

Jun looked at me. "What's your read on the propagation geometry from these seven nodes?"

"If the central cluster holds and the eastern nodes fail, you have an irregular but continuous network across most of the test site's central band." I had been looking at the topology while Sera was presenting. "It's not optimal. But a continuous network, even irregular, has propagation properties that disconnected nodes don't. The central cluster can carry the load."

"You're saying the system is still coherent," Jun said.

"I'm saying it might be. In three weeks, you'll know."

Sera gathered her tablet with the careful movement of someone who is going to carry good news back to the rest of the team and does not want to drop it. "I'm going to tell them," she said. "Not prematurely. But they need to know we have something."

After she left, the monitoring station was quiet. Outside, the late afternoon had the specific quality of Jun's world in early summer: the sun lower in the sky than it should be for the hour, the smoke haze soft rather than acute, a quality of light that in another context would be beautiful and in this context was the beauty of a damaged thing that had not yet finished being itself.

"She's right," Jun said. "We have something."

"You do."

He looked at me. "We."

I looked at the tablet, which still had the density readings on the screen — the seven nodes above threshold, small lights in a large dark. "We," I said.

---

He asked if I wanted to walk the test site. We went out in the last of the light, with headlamps because it would be dark before we were done, and we walked the monitoring path in the reverse of the direction he usually used — starting at the north and moving south, which was not how the protocol specified but which is how Jun walks when he's thinking, working backward from the conclusion to its premises.

He carried the tablet with the density readings. I carried my notebook, which I hadn't opened, which I was carrying as I always carry it in the field: available, not yet needed, the presence of the object a kind of readiness.

The seven nodes were marked with orange flags — the same orange as the monitoring boundary flags, a color that Jun's team used for anything that needed to be visible regardless of conditions. In the headlamp beams, the flags caught the light and held it. Seven points of orange in the dark, spread across the test site's central band in the irregular pattern that corresponded to the better substrate, the less-disturbed soil, the places where the network had found enough to hold.

Jun stopped at the third flag. He shone the headlamp down at the soil, which looked like soil — dark, slightly sandy, the surface disturbed by the sensor posts but otherwise unremarkable. There was nothing to see. The network, if it was there, was below the surface.

"It's strange," he said. "You look at this and you see nothing. The thing that matters is invisible."

"Most things that matter are invisible," I said.

"That's either profound or a cliché." He was almost smiling. Jun almost-smiles rather than smiles; the full expression is saved for situations of unusual significance, and he uses its partial form as a placeholder.

"Probably both," I said.

He crouched at the flag's base and put his hand flat on the soil. This was not protocol — you don't touch a monitoring site without gloves, without documentation. He was doing it the way a person puts their hand on a wall to feel if it's warm, a gesture that is outside the methodology but is also the methodology at its most basic: attending to the thing.

"Three years of coalition work," he said. "Before you arrived, we had models. Projections. Good data, wrong direction. The network was degrading faster than we could stabilize it." He looked up at me from the crouch, the headlamp giving him the slightly upward-from-below quality of someone illuminated from underneath. "I don't know how to say this without it sounding — it sounds like I'm making you responsible for something that isn't your responsibility."

"Say it," I said.

"When you started coming. The spatial analysis, the substrate work, the propagation modeling. We had something we could actually aim at." He stood, his hand leaving the soil with a slight reluctance I might have imagined. "I was going to run out of things to aim at. And then you were here, and I wasn't."

I thought about the gradient in the south plot's die-off, the northeast quadrant with its three surviving trees, the first thing I had seen in his world that I could actually do something about. I thought about the satisfaction of that — the specific, irreplaceable feeling of a skill that has found its correct use.

"I know what it is," I said, "to be in a fight you might be losing and have something arrive that gives you a direction."

He was watching me with the full quality of attention he reserves for things that matter. "Is that what crossing is for you? Arriving in a fight?"

"No." I looked at the seven flags, the small orange lights in the test site's dark. "I think crossing is — I think I'm working out what crossing is. But arriving somewhere that needed what I have — yes. That part is real."

He reached out and found my hand in the dark between us. His hand was warm from being in his jacket pocket and slightly rough from the field work and it closed around mine with the particular ease of something that has been practiced, even though we had not done this before.

We stood like that at the third monitoring flag. The headlamps made circles in the dark. The test site held its invisible network. Somewhere beneath the soil, seven connected nodes were doing something alive.

"I want to ask you something," Jun said. "And I want to ask it once, and I don't want to have to ask it again."

"Ask."

"Are you staying? Not permanently — I know that's not — I'm not asking for permanently. I'm asking if you're in this. If the work we're doing together is something you're committed to, the way I'm committed to it. Not because you feel obligated. Because you've chosen it."

The orange flags. The seven nodes. The coalition's last best bet.

"I'm in this," I said. "I don't know what 'this' looks like in six months. But right now, I'm in it."

He squeezed my hand once, and then we kept walking, the headlamps sweeping the dark, the monitoring flags marking the shape of something that was not yet saved but was, for the first time in a long time, fighting back.
