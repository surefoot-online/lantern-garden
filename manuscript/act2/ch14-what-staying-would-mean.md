### Chapter Fourteen · What Staying Would Mean

The week I spent embedded with the coalition was not planned.

It happened the way things happen when circumstances align in a particular direction and the logic of continuing outweighs the logic of returning: the supplementary inoculation's critical monitoring window, the follow-up with Elio, the arrival of two new coalition members from a university ecology department who needed introduction to the spatial modeling framework. Each day had a reason to stay. The reasons added up.

By Thursday I had been in Jun's world for six days, and on Thursday evening I was sitting in the coalition's communal kitchen eating rice and pickled vegetables and listening to Sera describe the monitoring readings and it occurred to me, with a quiet definiteness that did not feel like revelation but like recognition, that I had stopped calculating.

I had been calculating, since the first crossing, how long I could stay before the lie became untenable. How many days before Hana Park's professional patience reached its limit. How long before my apartment's automated features noticed I hadn't been home. How many crossings before the memory cost became too high. I had been running these calculations in the background of everything I did in Jun's world, a constant low-level accounting that I had become so accustomed to that I no longer heard it until the moment it stopped.

It stopped on Thursday evening in the coalition's kitchen.

I sat with the cessation of it for a while. What remained in its absence was the specific quality of presence that I associated with the work at its best — the feeling of being in the right place for the right reasons, not suspended between obligations but here, doing this.

I thought: I understand now why she stayed.

The understanding was not abstract. It was physical: the particular tiredness of someone whose body has calibrated to a place, whose sleep has adjusted to the ambient sound, whose sense of where things are in a room has stopped requiring active attention. Jun's world had become, in six days, navigable in the way that a space becomes navigable after enough time — not just intellectually known but bodily known.

I understood why Liling had stayed three years.

I also understood, though I could not yet say how, that I couldn't.

This was not a decision. It was more like understanding the shape of something. I was holding both things at once: the completeness of being here, and the knowledge that the completeness was not the whole of what I was. There was something in my world — not a person, not an obligation, something more like a structural fact — that the completeness here did not contain.

I couldn't name it yet. I sat with not being able to name it.

On Friday I worked with the coalition's mapping team, running the updated substrate model through the new data Sera's team had collected from the northern quadrant. On Saturday I helped Jun review the legal documentation from the clearcut injunction, which had been partially successful — the contractor had been fined, the injunction formally served, but the cleared area was not going to be restored. The buffer zone was gone. We worked around this, adjusting the inoculation plan to compensate.

On Saturday evening Jun and I walked the test site again.

The monitoring flags were the same orange in the dark. The nodes were holding — the last reading had confirmed all seven of the original establishments and added a possible eighth, a site at the northwestern edge of the central band that Sera was cautiously optimistic about. We walked the path between the flags and I counted them and thought about what a network looks like when it's growing: not uniform, not planned, finding its way through the substrate toward the next available connection.

"Tell me something about your world," Jun said.

I was not expecting this. "What do you want to know?"

"Anything. What's the light like?"

I thought about this. "Wetter," I said. "More gray. Vancouver has a hundred different qualities of gray light — you learn to distinguish between the gray that comes before rain and the gray that comes after, the gray of an overcast morning versus the gray of an overcast afternoon. Different grays do different things to the color of plants." I paused. "The forest — our forest — is louder. More birds. The difference in bird density is one of the things I notice most when I cross."

"That's — that's the thing I don't think about," Jun said. "What we've lost that you still have."

"You haven't lost the birds," I said. "Just the density."

"Not yet." He looked up at the canopy, which was sparse enough to see through to the stars. "What does the Cascade zone look like in your world?"

"Healthy," I said. "Mostly. There's stress — there's always stress now, the advance front exists in my world too, just slower, further away. But the old-growth patches in my world still register above zero-point-eight on the connectivity scale. The mother trees are still there."

He was quiet for a moment. "What would it take," he said, "to prevent what happened here from happening there?"

"Faster action than what happened here. Starting with the network, not the individual trees, the way your coalition has been trying to do. And—" I paused. "And people who can see the topology. Who can read the gradient."

He looked at me.

"What?" I said.

"You're describing yourself."

"I'm describing a methodology."

"You're describing a methodology that you embody in a specific way that most landscape architects don't." He was using the tone he uses when he's working through something in real time and wants to be precise. "Mei. What if the crossing isn't just — what if it's not just your situation. What if there are reasons why someone with your specific skill set crosses to the specific place where the work is happening?"

I felt a familiar resistance. "You're suggesting the Garden has preferences."

"I'm suggesting the Garden might have logic. Ecological logic." He looked at the flags, the test site. "In my world's ecology, nothing is random. The collapse has vectors. The network finds paths through the substrate that are determined by conditions we can measure. What if the crossing works the same way — finds the most effective path between connected points?"

"That would mean I didn't have a choice," I said.

"It would mean your choices were informed by conditions you didn't know about." He looked at me. "That's different from not having a choice."

I thought about this for a long time. The canopy overhead, the stars through the sparse branches. The seven flags and their possible eighth.

"Your grandmother didn't choose to come here randomly," Jun said. "She came from a world with a healthier forest and she worked in a world with a dying forest for three years and left behind exactly what the forest needed. That's not coincidence, Mei. That's ecology."

"Or it's a story we tell about coincidence after the fact," I said.

"Or both." He was almost smiling. "I'm not saying the Garden is sentient. I'm saying that liminal spaces, in ecology, always have function. Nothing is just a place. Everything is a system."

The system. The network. The path through the substrate that finds the next available connection.

"I need to visit Liling," I said. "When I go back."

"I know." He reached out and took my hand, the same ease as before, the same particular warmth. "Before you go. I want to tell you something I haven't said."

"Say it."

"I know you can't stay." He said it plainly, without drama or reproach — the way he presents the worst data without performing the tragedy of it. "I don't think staying is what you are. And I think I've been pretending not to know that because it's easier to want something impossible than to want the thing that's actually available." He looked at our hands. "But the thing that's actually available is real. You're real. What we're doing is real. I can want that without wanting you to be something you're not."

The seven orange flags in the dark. The possible eighth.

"Thank you for saying that," I said.

"Thank you for not leaving before I said it."

We stood in the test site a while longer, until the chill of the night came up through the ground and made standing still impractical. Then we walked back along the monitoring path, Jun's headlamp making its circle, mine making mine, the two circles overlapping in the space between us.

On Sunday, I crossed back.
