### Chapter Eight · Archive

I did not look at the photograph again for three days.

This was not avoidance. I want to be precise about that. I had the photograph in my phone, accessible, and I chose not to open it — which is a different thing from not being able to. I was doing what I do when I have found something that changes the shape of what I'm looking at: I was letting myself see the new shape without pressing toward it too quickly. Some analytical processes work better in the peripheral field. Some recognitions need time to land.

I worked on the coalition's substrate priority model for three days. I wrote two emails to Hana Park about the Burrard Street development that I subsequently did not send. I ran the coastal mycorrhizal data Sera had forwarded and identified seven high-priority nodes in the test zone's northwest quadrant that I was nearly certain would be the next collapse vectors if they weren't seeded before the beetle season's peak. I submitted this analysis to Jun in a brief message at eleven PM on the second day, received a response at four in the morning — *"This is right. I think this is right. I'm going to send it to the board."* — and felt, for the first time in two weeks, the specific satisfaction of work that had found its correct use.

On the third day, I opened the photograph.

I had been avoiding the video call with my mother for two days as well. This was avoidance — Huimei would see it in my face, which I could not prevent, and I was not ready for what she would see there. On the fourth day after the Taipei visit, I called her.

She picked up on the second ring. The screen showed her at her kitchen table in Taipei, mid-morning, a cup of something steaming at her elbow, the window behind her bright with the particular quality of Taipei's late-spring light — a diffuse warmth that makes everything look slightly overexposed. She looked at me. I had forgotten how precisely my mother reads my face.

"Ah-Mei," she said. Not a greeting. More like a confirmation.

"I have something to show you." I held the phone up so the screen faced mine, then navigated to the photograph and held it to the camera. "Look at this."

She looked. I watched her face.

Huimei Chen is not someone who shows reactions quickly. She has the particular expressiveness of a person who has learned to read others precisely and, as a consequence, learned to keep her own face at a slight remove from her feelings — not concealment, exactly, but caution. A lag between the feeling and its appearance on the surface. I know her face well enough to see underneath the lag.

What I saw: recognition. Not of the garden — I was watching for that and did not see it. Something else. The quality of attention she gave the photograph was not the attention of someone seeing something new. It was the attention of someone encountering, from an unexpected angle, something they had been avoiding.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Where did you find this?" she said.

"On Nǎi Nai's bedside table. The last visit. I photographed it."

Silence. She was still looking at the screen, still looking at the photograph I was holding toward the camera. "She had it in her room?"

"Propped against the lamp. I don't know when it appeared. It wasn't there in November."

"No," my mother said. "It wasn't."

The way she said it — very measured, very careful — made something settle in the room between us.

"Do you know this garden?" I asked.

"No." She said it without hesitation, and I believed her on the narrow technical question. She did not know the garden. She knew something adjacent to it. "I don't know where it is. I've never seen the photograph before. But—" She stopped.

"But what?"

"Your grandmother had photographs she kept in boxes, not in frames. A box of work photographs, field photographs. I've seen some of them. But she kept some separate — a smaller tin, the kind that biscuits come in, red lacquer. She took it with her when she moved to the care facility. I thought it was just — keepsakes. You know how she is. Was." My mother corrected herself with the particular intonation of someone practicing for a loss that hasn't happened yet. "She was always precise about what she kept and what she let go."

"Was the tin in her room?"

"I haven't seen it since the move. I assumed it went with her other things into storage. But if she had the photograph on her table—" My mother looked at me through the screen. "She wanted you to see it. She could have kept it in the tin. She put it where you would see it."

I set down the phone and looked at the photograph properly. The lantern-plants in their rows. The figure at the edge of the frame. The tree line that I now knew, from the coordinates, corresponded to the ridge above the Cascade zone's test area.

"She traveled for fieldwork in the early seventies," I said. "You told me that. Canada."

"Yes."

"Do you know which institution she was affiliated with? When she was in Canada?"

"The Taiwan Forestry Research Institute. TFRI — that's what it was called. That's where she was for most of her career. The Canadian visit was through them, I think — some kind of international research collaboration. Botanical exchange, something like that." My mother's voice had the tone of someone reciting what they'd been told, not what they knew. "She never talked about the work specifically. Just that she went. That it was important work."

"Did she say what kind?"

"Soil science. Forest ecology. The kind of thing she always did." Huimei was quiet for a moment. "She brought back seeds, I think. Specimens. She was always bringing things back from fieldwork — it was what botanists did, you collected. I remember a smell in the house after she came back, something resinous, like cedar but not quite. I asked her what it was and she said it was from the forest."

"The TFRI archives would have records," I said. "Her field reports, her research affiliations. Conference registrations."

"Probably." My mother was watching me with the particular attention she brings to situations where she has decided to let something unfold rather than shape it. "Why are you looking into this?"

"She said a name, Māmā. Kenji. When I visited." I had told her this already, by phone from the taxi. "I can't stop thinking about it."

"I told you — she mentions him sometimes. Old friend, old colleague—"

"I think he was more than that."

The lag between feeling and expression: I watched my mother's face and saw something move through it that she didn't let reach the surface. "That's possible," she said, finally. "She was a person before she was my mother. She had a life I don't have access to." A pause. "Most of us have lives our children don't have access to."

After we hung up, I opened the TFRI's online archive.

The Taiwan Forestry Research Institute had been digitizing its historical records for the better part of a decade — the project was ongoing, unfinished in the way that institutional archives are always ongoing and unfinished, but enough of the 1960s and 1970s material had been scanned and indexed that I could search by author. I searched for Wǎng Liling.

Seventeen papers. The earliest from 1966, a methodological piece on soil inoculation techniques for conifer transplanting. The latest from 1972 — a study of mycorrhizal network density differentials in high-altitude Douglas fir populations, published in a Taiwanese forestry journal and also cited in three subsequent international papers by researchers in Oregon and British Columbia.

I read the 1972 paper first.

Liling's prose style was precise and unsentimental, which surprised me. I had expected the writing of someone from that era to carry the elaborate formal constructions of mid-century scientific Mandarin — the rhetorical scaffolding that substitutes for the kind of directness that later decades would find acceptable. Her writing wasn't like that. She stated her findings plainly and did not embellish her conclusions beyond what the data supported. She was, I understood from this, someone who trusted the data to speak if you presented it correctly. She did not trust interpretation to do work that evidence could do.

The 1972 paper described something that in 1972 was not yet named — the collaborative, resource-sharing function of mycorrhizal networks in old-growth forest ecosystems. She was describing, with careful data, the thing that Suzanne Simard would name in the 1990s and that the scientific establishment would spend two decades slowly crediting. Liling had it. In 1972. In a Taiwanese forestry journal that the international literature had largely ignored.

She was twenty years ahead of the field.

After 1972: nothing.

I searched for the 1973 symposium my mother had mentioned. There was a record — a Vancouver conference on Pacific Rim forest ecology, a gathering of researchers from Taiwan, Japan, Canada, and the United States. The conference program was not digitized but the registration list was, scanned from a physical document, slightly crooked on the page. Wǎng Liling, Taiwan Forestry Research Institute, Taipei. Registered. No presentation listed.

She went to Vancouver for a conference she did not present at. And then she did not come back.

Not for three years.

I closed the archive window and opened my modeling software. The substrate priority nodes were still there, unchanged by what I had just learned. The coalition's beetle-season deadline was six weeks away. I should have been running the final analysis, sending Jun the prioritization grid, doing the work I had said I would do.

I sat for a long time looking at the modeling software without using it.

In 1972, Wǎng Liling was twenty-five years old. She was one of the most precise forest ecologists working in Pacific Rim mycorrhizal systems — a field that would not understand what it had had in her for another two decades. She registered for a conference in Vancouver and didn't present. And then she was gone for three years, and when she came back she was different in a way my mother could feel but not name.

I knew the feeling my mother described. I had been living it.

I opened a new document and began to write down what I knew:

*TFRI, 1966–1972: active research, 17 papers. No work in demography — this was a scientist at full capacity.*

*Vancouver, 1973: conference registration, no presentation. Last confirmed activity in World A.*

*1972–1975: gap. No papers. No institutional affiliation. Three visa renewals.*

*1975: return to TFRI. Returned with seeds. Changed.*

I stared at the list for a while. Then I added:

*Note: the crossing takes peripheral memory. Not core memory. Not your publications or your methodology or your understanding of the work. But the record of your time.*

*If you crossed for three years, you would not remember arriving in Vancouver. You would not remember the specific movements between the conference and the Garden. The border crossings, the institutional records — these would be in the peripheral band. These would be the kind of thing that comes back with smooth edges.*

*Liling's three-year gap is not an absence. It's a residue.*

I saved the document and opened the substrate model again. This time I used it. I worked for two hours without stopping. The priority nodes required my full analytical attention and I gave them all of it, which is the way I have learned to do two things at once: by doing one of them completely.

The next morning I called the TFRI's archive department.

The archivist who answered had the voice of someone who has been helping researchers navigate an underfunded archive for a very long time and has arrived at a stable equilibrium of patience and mild resignation. I asked about Wǎng Liling's records — specifically whether her field notes and correspondence from the 1968–1975 period would be in the archive.

"Researcher records from that period are partially digitized," she said. "Published papers, yes. Submitted reports, some of them. Personal correspondence would not typically be part of the institutional archive — those are considered private documents unless donated by the researcher or their family."

"Are there any gaps in her institutional record? From the mid-seventies?"

A pause. The sound of searching. "There's a notation here. After 1972, her institutional affiliation was inactive. She returned to active status in 1975. There's a note in the file from that year — she returned with a collection of samples and submitted a brief summary of what she described as 'field research, Canada, 1972–1975.' But the full field report was apparently never submitted."

"She submitted a summary but not the full report?"

"According to the file, yes. The summary is in the archive. Would you like me to send you a scan?"

I said yes. I gave her my email address and thanked her.

The summary arrived forty minutes later. It was a single page, handwritten in Liling's precise, unembellished script — the same voice I had read in the 1972 paper, now compressed into eight paragraphs. It described three years of field research on mycorrhizal system recovery in degraded temperate rainforest ecosystems. It named specific tree species, specific soil composition conditions, specific inoculation methodologies. It was the summary of someone who had done serious work and was filing the minimum required documentation.

The final paragraph read:

*Specimens and seed material collected during the research period have been deposited with the TFRI specimen collection under registration codes attached. A mycorrhizal matrix derived from the Pacific Rim conifer populations studied has been preserved and stored separately, off-site, in situ, under conditions appropriate for long-term viability. Details available on request.*

*Details available on request.* Fifty years of silence as a detail available on request.

I searched the TFRI specimen collection database for Liling's registration codes. The specimens were there — pressed botanical samples, correctly filed, unremarkable. The mycorrhizal matrix was not in the database. It was not in the TFRI collection. It was off-site, in situ, and wherever it was, no one had asked for the details in fifty years.

I wrote Jun a message: *The substrate model is complete. Sending now. Also: I have a question that isn't about the substrate model. Do you keep any kind of historical record of people who have worked in the Cascade zone? Going back to the seventies?*

He responded in seventeen minutes: *Yes. The coalition has an oral history project and some written records from local botanists and foresters. What are you looking for?*

I looked at Liling's field summary on my screen. The precise handwriting. The disciplined understatement. The detail available on request.

*I'm not sure yet,* I wrote back. *Can we talk when I arrive?*

*Yes,* he wrote. *Come soon. The beetle season is moving faster than the model.*
