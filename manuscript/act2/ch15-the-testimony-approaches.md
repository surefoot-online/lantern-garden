### Chapter Fifteen: The Testimony Approaches

She knew the Tribunal was coming closer by the way the dreams changed.

She had been dreaming of forests — both world's forests, sometimes simultaneously in the logic of dreams, the Vancouver rain on old-growth needles and the specific amber of the smoke horizon blurring into the same image. She had been tracking the dreams the way she tracked everything: not with interpretation, not yet, but with the discipline of noticing. Something in her was processing what she was living inside, in the sleep hours when the professional maintenance of two lives relaxed its grip.

The dreams that began in the week after the cedar planting were different. She was in a room. The room had high ceilings and manufactured grey walls and a laminate table. She was not afraid of the room. She was looking at it the way she looked at sites: reading the proportions, reading the materials, reading the light. The room was designed to be neutral and had therefore been designed, and she read the design with the fluency of someone for whom design was a primary language.

She woke each morning with the specific knowledge of having been in that room, and she lay still for a moment and ran through what she would say. She ran through the argument, the spatial version of it, the geometry that translated the ecological evidence into the form the Tribunal would need to hold. She was, she understood, preparing for the testimony the way she prepared for difficult client presentations: not scripting it, but building the spatial model of it, the room where the argument lived, the paths through it.

She booked the flight to Taipei one more time.

Her grandmother was in one of the hard periods — Fang-ling had warned her by message, the days where the confusion was deep and the windows were small. She came anyway. She sat in the family chair and held Liling's hand and talked about the cedar, about the orange flags, about the first connectivity indicators that Priya had reported the previous week — preliminary, requiring confirmation, but present. The network showing what it was beginning to be.

She talked without expecting response, because the response she needed was not one her grandmother could give in this period. The response she needed was one she had already received: the coordinates, the science, the name Kenji, the confirmation that someone had been here before her. She had what she needed. She was here now just to be here.

Liling was looking at the light on the wall. The specific attending quality, the field-station angle.

At the end of the visit, when she rose to leave and bent to kiss her grandmother's forehead, Liling's hand came up and closed briefly around hers.

"The network is talking," Liling said.

Her grandmother's eyes were soft, not fully focused. She didn't know if the sentence was directed at her or at the light on the wall or at something that had no address in this room. It didn't matter. The sentence was true either way.

"Yes," she said. "It's talking."

She left the facility and stood on the street in the Taipei afternoon and she did not call anyone. She stood with the sun on her face and the traffic going by and the city being entirely itself, enormous and ordinary and nothing to do with any of it, and she stood in it and felt the weight of what she had been given and the weight of what she was going to do with it, and both weights were the same weight, and she could carry it.

She went to the airport.

She came home.

---

The last crossing before the testimony was on a Tuesday morning. She walked through the park to the northeast quadrant, to the clearing, to the edge of the Lantern Garden. The lantern-plants in the morning light, the gathered threshold-light between them and the treeline.

She stood at the edge for a moment.

She looked at the Garden the way she looked at sites before she entered them: reading the perimeter, understanding the approach. She had been crossing this threshold for six months and she had never once looked at it as a site to be read. She had been in it, using it, treating it the way you treat a road: as the path between two places rather than a place itself.

She looked at it now.

The plants were doing what they always did: the biological patience of the fruiting structures, the specific gathering of light that had no good explanation in terms of the actual morning sun. She had spent six months not looking at them directly and she looked now, crouching at the edge of the planting, examining the structure she'd been walking past.

She reached out and touched a fruiting structure gently. The papery calyx was dry at the tip and green toward the base — new growth, she thought. Something establishing. Something that was in the process of becoming what it would be.

She thought about what her grandmother had told her: that the crossing runs in the maternal line. That whoever crosses pays attention in the right way. That the Garden connects people who are working on the same problem and brings them to where the work is.

She thought about the seedling she'd found in the unmapped section of Jun's forest — the second-generation specimen, the legacy planting reproducing itself. She thought about what it meant for something to plant its own continuation.

She stood up.

She crossed.

The interval was the interval: the not-quite-space, the structured weight of it, the movement through something that was neither space nor time but had the properties of both. She was aware, as she always was, that she was moving through something that was not hostile and was not random and was, in some way that she did not yet fully understand, organized. The Garden had a logic. Kenji had said it, Jun had suggested it, her grandmother had confirmed it: not sentience, not will, but ecological logic, the logic of connected things finding the paths that needed to be found.

She arrived in Jun's world.

The morning. The birds of this world. The forest smelling of an intact system — that specific earthen note of fungal activity, the smell she had come to know as the smell of the network working.

She stood at the Garden's edge in Jun's world and looked into the forest and she knew, with the particular spatial certainty that was her most reliable instrument, that the testimony was close. That what she was going to be asked to account for was already what it was — the months of crossing, the discovery of the preservation, the bridging seeding sequence, the seven viable strains, the orange flags at the cedar. The argument was already made. The geometry was in the landscape.

She had the evidence. She had built it the way her grandmother had built the preservation: layer by layer, each layer exactly what it needed to be, sealed against the time when someone would need to open it.

She walked to the station.

Jun was at the map table, which was where he was in the mornings, doing the work that needed doing, working toward something that might not happen in his lifetime with the patient certainty of a person who understood that this was what working toward something meant.

He looked up when she came in.

"The Monday monitoring came in overnight," he said. "Fourteen nodes above threshold."

She stopped.

"Fourteen," she said.

"Fourteen." He turned the tablet toward her. The connectivity map. The blue-green rendering. Fourteen points of light, connected by lines that had not been there three weeks ago, connected by lines that were the evidence of a network that was, in the mapped section of the corridor, doing what networks did: reaching.

She looked at the map for a long moment.

The work was working.

"I may need to be somewhere for testimony soon," she said. "The Tribunal. I don't know exactly when."

"I know," he said.

"I don't know how long it will take."

"Come back when it's done," he said. "The monitoring doesn't stop."

She looked at the fourteen points of light on the screen.

"Fourteen," she said.

"Fourteen," he said. "And the north ridge seeding hasn't established indicators yet. When it does —"

"It will."

"I know." He set the tablet down and looked at her. "You've been in the Garden this morning."

"Yes."

"The testimony is soon."

"I think so."

He looked at her for a moment with the full quality of attention he used for things that mattered. Not the almost-smile, not the working face. The complete, unguarded looking.

"Tell them about the fourteen nodes," he said. "Start there."

"Start with the geometry."

"Start with the fact of a forest beginning to be what it was. Let that be the first thing."

She held his gaze. She thought about the grey room with its manufactured neutrality and the chair she would sit in and the recording light that would go red and the woman with the face of someone who had listened to too much testimony.

She thought about what she would say to the chair.

She thought: *I'm going to describe what I was calling, to myself, the weeks of crossings that constituted the second phase — the phase of discovery. I want to be clear about what I found and what I didn't find. I want to be clear about the difference between the crossing as I understood it at the beginning and the crossing as I understand it now.*

She thought: *And the thing I understand now is that the crossing is not a door. It's a network node. It's the point in the topology where the connection between two systems is maintained. The Garden didn't find me — the Garden connected me to the next point in a network that had been building for fifty years before I arrived. My grandmother was the first node. Kenji and Jinhee were nodes. And I am a node, and the work I've done is the signal that moves through the node, and the signal is the history of the forest and the history of the work and the history of the people who paid attention.*

That was what she was going to tell them.

Not as metaphor. As the precise structural description of what had happened.

"Okay," she said. She turned toward the map table. "Can I have an hour with the connectivity model before we talk about anything else?"

"Yes." He sat down across from her and opened the model on his laptop. Fourteen nodes. The blue-green rendering. The network saying what it had to say. "What are you looking for?"

"I want to build the visual argument. For the Tribunal. The spatial argument, in visual form — the before and after, the trajectory of the corridor from the network's point of view. Not the damage. The recovery."

He looked at her.

"The Tribunal is going to see a record of crossings and they're going to assess them," she said. "I want to give them a record of what the crossings produced. The spatial evidence. The thing that exists in the terrain because of the work." She looked at the model. "I want to show them the fourteen nodes and the north ridge culture and the bridging sequence as a spatial argument — not a narrative, a geometry. The geometry of something being restored."

He turned the laptop toward her.

"Show me what you need," he said.

---

She spent two hours with the model. Jun brought her coffee and then sat at his own desk and did his work, which was the specific form of presence she'd come to rely on and no longer registered as distinct from the professional landscape — it had become part of the ambient conditions, like the sound of the forest and the smell of the station and the quality of the light in the analysis room at various times of day. She worked in it the way she worked in sites she'd learned well.

What she built was not a presentation in the conventional sense. It was a spatial narrative — the corridor in sequence, from the baseline 2019 state through the documented fragmentation through the coalition's three years of work through the south site's fourteen established nodes through the north ridge legacy culture and its projected integration. She mapped each stage as a state of the network, not a state of the crisis: the crisis was visible in the negative space, in what was missing at each stage and what had been recovered by the next.

The last frame was a projection: the corridor at full bridging connectivity, with the historical network composition restored in its interior through the integration of Liling's culture. It was a model. The model was sound. The geometry was in the data.

She exported it and labeled it and sent it to herself on both the professional email account and the personal one, because she did not yet know from which account she would be sending it when it was needed.

She looked at the last frame for a long time.

This was what fifty years of patience looked like, rendered in the blue-green of things that were alive and connected. This was what a woman had planted in a clay vessel with pine resin and birch bark and left under a cedar on a north-facing slope with the knowledge that someone would come who knew what to do with it.

This was what Kenji had kept alive for thirty years, walking to the north ridge twice a week, not knowing whether anyone would find it.

This was what the coalition had been building toward without knowing what it was building toward.

And this was what she was going to carry into the grey room and place before the Tribunal with the same matter-of-fact precision her grandmother had used to state the findings she'd spent her career working toward.

*The network is talking.* That's what mattered. She had heard her grandmother say it. She was going to make it true.

---

The night before the testimony began — she knew it was the night before not because anyone told her but because she knew, the same way she knew when a crossing was coming, the same way she'd come to know this world's timing: by living in it long enough to read its indicators — she walked to the park.

It was late. The park was nearly empty at this hour, just a few dog-walkers moving through the amber light of the paths, the Douglas firs in the northeast quadrant doing their patient work in the dark. She walked to the northeast quadrant and stood at the edge of the Lantern Garden and looked at it in the dark.

The lantern-plants were doing what they did at night: the low bioluminescence, the thing that had no explanation in terms of physics she knew, the gathering of light from some source that was not the ambient light of a city-lit park. She had been walking past this for six months and she had learned not to explain it, to receive it the way you received evidence before you had a framework: as a fact. As something that was there.

She crouched at the edge of the planting and looked at the fruiting structure nearest her. The pendant droop. The papery calyx. The light inside it.

Her grandmother had crouched here fifty years ago. Had looked at the same structure and had understood what it was and had stepped through. Had crossed because the work needed presence and because she was the person who could provide the presence.

She, Mei, had found the same Garden and had crossed for the same reason and had been, for six months, in the process of understanding what the reason meant.

She understood it now.

She stood up.

The light at the threshold was doing what it always did at the edge of the Garden — gathering in the space between the plants and the treeline, the specific quality of between that she had been calling threshold light since the first month and that she had never successfully explained but that her body recognized as reliably as it recognized sleep. The Garden was there. It was waiting.

She was not going to cross tonight.

Tonight she was going to go home and sleep in her own apartment and let her body have the rest it needed before the testimony. She was going to wake in the morning and make tea and look at the bad windows and be present in her world for the hours before she was needed in the grey room.

She looked at the Garden for one more moment.

She thought: when this is over, I will come back here. I will cross. The work is not finished. The fourteen nodes need the north ridge integration and the beetle season is coming and the bridging sequence needs confirmation at every stage. The work is not finished, and the work needs presence, and I am the presence.

She thought: and my grandmother is in a care facility in Taipei having windows in her dementia where she can name *Rhizopogon vesiculosus* by its propagation preference and tell me to keep the inoculation at twelve centimeters. She is still there. She has given me what she held for fifty years. She is still there.

She thought: the network is talking. In both worlds. Slowly, in the dark below the surface, through the mechanisms that were there before any of us and will be there after. The network is doing what the network does. The people above it are doing what they can. This is enough. This is what there is. It is, in the specific and non-sentimental sense of the word, enough.

She straightened.

She walked back through the park, through the amber path-light, past the Douglas firs, through the gate. She walked home. She made tea. She sat at the kitchen table and looked at the connectivity model on her laptop screen — the last frame, the projected restoration, the blue-green of the forest finding its language — and she let the specific quality of that image be what it was for as long as she needed it to be.

Then she closed the laptop and went to bed.
