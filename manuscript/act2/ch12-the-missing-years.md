### Chapter Twelve · The Missing Years

Jing-wei's second report was longer and more careful.

*Dear Ms. Chen,*

*I have found three relevant items. I want to be precise about what is established and what is inferred, because some of this requires you to make judgments I am not positioned to make.*

*Item one: The missing-persons report.*

*In November 1972, colleagues of Dr. Wǎng Liling at the Taiwan Forestry Research Institute filed a concern with the TFRI's administrative office. She had told them she would attend the Vancouver symposium in October 1972 and return within two weeks. She did not return. The TFRI administrative note describes it as a welfare check, not a missing-persons report in the police sense — they were checking whether she had been in contact with the Canadian host institution, whether there was a health matter. The Canadian contact (the symposium's organizer) confirmed she had attended the conference's first day and had not been seen afterward.*

*The TFRI made a note in her file. They did not escalate further. This was, I think, not negligence — it was the assumption that a professional adult researcher had made a professional adult decision about her fieldwork, and that she would communicate when she had something to communicate. She was known as self-directed and uninclined to keep regular contact during fieldwork.*

*She did not communicate for three years.*

*Item two: The return.*

*In March 1975, Dr. Wǎng appeared at the TFRI in Taipei. She submitted the field summary you have already seen, deposited her specimens, and resumed her institutional affiliation. The administrative note from her return is brief: "Dr. Wǎng has returned from an extended Canadian field research period. Specimens submitted and accepted. She reports in good health and intends to resume her position in the Institute's forest ecology program."*

*The note's tone is interesting. It reads as though nothing unusual has happened. Someone decided to characterize three years of undocumented absence as an "extended field research period," and no one, apparently, chose to contest this characterization. She was a valued researcher. She had come back. The specimens were good. The note closed the administrative concern.*

*Item three: What she brought with her.*

*In the days following her return, Dr. Wǎng met with a colleague named Professor Huang Binglong, who was at that time the head of the TFRI's specimen collection. Professor Huang is now ninety-one years old and in a care home in Nantou. I contacted him by phone, with his family's permission.*

*He remembers Dr. Wǎng's return clearly. He describes her as looking well but different — "steadied," he said, which was his word. He helped her catalog the specimens she brought back. He noted, in our conversation, that the specimens were unusual — the species were from the Pacific Rim conifer zone, as he would have expected, but some of the mycorrhizal samples were from substrate and climate conditions that did not correspond to any Canadian research site he was familiar with.*

*He asked her about this. She told him the samples were from "a high-elevation site with unusual microclimate conditions." He accepted this. He was not suspicious — she was a careful researcher, and unusual microclimate conditions exist. He filed the specimens under her registration codes.*

*He also told me something I had not asked about: she asked him to help her separately with something he described as a "preservation protocol." Not a specimen for the collection — she was explicit that this was not going to be deposited with the Institute. She called it a "site preservation." She brought a prepared matrix — mycorrhizal, he said, highly concentrated, in a birch-bark container sealed with what he thought was pine resin — and she discussed with him the methodology for long-term in-situ preservation. He helped her work through the technical parameters.*

*He did not know where she was going to deposit it. She thanked him, took the matrix, and he did not see her again for several months.*

*I believe Dr. Wǎng Liling planted a mycorrhizal matrix somewhere in the Canadian forest zone, in 1975, before she returned to Taiwan. I cannot determine where.*

*Jing-wei*

---

I read the report twice. Then I sat for a while and thought about the administrative note's phrase: *extended field research period.* Someone had written that. Someone had looked at three years of undocumented absence and the return of a woman who was clearly alive and well and had clearly done *something*, and had decided the most useful characterization was: extended field research period.

The note had been written by a person who understood, on some level, that the full truth of where Wǎng Liling had been for three years was not a truth that could be put in an administrative record. And who had also understood that Wǎng Liling was worth having back. So they had found the language that permitted her return without requiring her explanation.

*Personal matter,* the case closure said.

I thought about my own absence from things: the client meetings I had rescheduled, the office I had half-left, the lie about driving up from the coast. The version of the truth that has been broken in for use. I had been living an extended research period of my own and calling it something else.

I wrote to Jun that evening: *I need to ask you something about your coalition's historical archives. Can I access them when I arrive?*

He responded within minutes: *Yes. When are you coming?*

*Thursday,* I wrote.

*Good,* he wrote. *Bring what you've found. I want to understand what you're looking for.*

---

I told my mother about the missing-persons report on a Wednesday evening. It was mid-morning in Taipei, and she was in her apartment, and I could tell from the background sounds that she had been in the middle of something — the radio, the sounds of a kitchen — before she picked up.

I told her in order: the administrative concern at the TFRI, the Canadian conference, the disappearance, the three-year gap, the return in 1975 with specimens that didn't match any known research site.

She was very quiet.

"Māmā," I said. "Are you still there?"

"I'm here." Her voice had the quality of someone standing very still in a room they've just recognized. "I knew some of this. Not all of it. I knew she had been away — my bà bà told me she'd gone to work in Canada. I was very young. I don't remember her being gone. I just — I remember her coming back. I remember the smell, I told you, the cedar smell. And I remember—" She stopped.

"What?"

"I remember my father's face. When she came back. He was happy. But it was a particular kind of happiness. The kind where someone is relieved but also — accepting something. Like he'd decided, while she was gone, to receive whatever came back." A pause. "I always thought he was just being patient with her temperament. She was always a little far away. I thought that was just who she was."

"It may have been who she had become," I said.

My mother was quiet. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying that three years is a long time. And that she came back different, and that she stayed. She chose to come back and she stayed." I paused. "She loved you."

"I know she loved me." This was matter-of-fact, not defensive. "She was not always present but she was — she was there. In the ways that counted." She exhaled slowly. "What happened in those three years?"

"I'm trying to find out," I said. "There may be more to find in Canada. In Jun's world—" I stopped. I had said it before I thought it. I waited.

"In Jun's world?" My mother's voice was careful. "Who is Jun?"

"A colleague. A researcher I've been working with. He has access to some historical records about the Cascade forest zone. The period your mother was there."

"I see." She said it in a way that meant she did not quite see but was choosing, as she chooses, to let the thing develop its own shape. "Alright. Tell me when you find something."

"I will."

"Ah-Mei." Her voice had shifted to the register she uses when she's about to say something she has been holding. "Whatever she did. Whatever she was doing those three years. She came back. That's — I need you to understand that that's the part I'm holding on to. She came back."

"I know," I said. "I know that's the part."
