### Chapter Sixteen · The First Crossers

Jun had been expecting the question.

I could tell from the way he paused before answering — not the pause of someone thinking, but the pause of someone who has already thought and is deciding how much to say. I had crossed on a Thursday, arrived at the test zone, and over the morning's monitoring work had found the moment to ask: *Do you have records of anyone crossing before me? Historically? The Cascade zone, early seventies?*

"We have stories," he said. "I've always called them stories."

"Tell me the stories."

He told me what Elio had told us, in more detail now that I was asking differently. He told me what the coalition's oral history project had collected — three accounts, from three different witnesses, of a woman working in the Cascade zone between approximately 1972 and 1975. She had worked primarily with independent botanists. She had been described, in all three accounts, with slight variations that were consistent with actual memory rather than mythologized story: *small, serious, kept a notebook, asked very specific questions about substrate conditions, came and went unpredictably.*

Came and went unpredictably.

"There's a fourth account," Jun said. "I didn't include it in the oral history report because it seemed too — the witness was unreliable in other ways. An older man who had worked as a backcountry guide in the Cascade zone in the seventies. He died eight years ago. But before he died he gave an interview to a local historical society in which he described a woman he'd encountered twice in the backcountry, in 1973 and again in 1974. He said she was doing something at a specific cedar — the one with the split trunk, on the ridge north of the main survey corridor. He said she was burying something. He said when he asked her what it was, she told him it was a gift for the forest."

A gift for the forest.

"And the coalition archivist has two photographs," Jun said. "They came with the oral history materials — donated by one of the witnesses, a botanist who died in 2015 and left her papers to the coalition. The photographs are — they're not good quality. Old prints, partially damaged. But there are two figures in the clearer of the two, at what looks like a forest research site."

"Can I see them?"

He went to the filing cabinet in the monitoring station's back room — an actual physical filing cabinet, which I had always registered as slightly incongruous in an otherwise digital-first operation but which Jun maintained as the archive's primary storage for anything that existed only in physical form. He came back with a folder.

The photographs were bad. The first was barely legible — a forest scene, some monitoring equipment visible, figures too small and blurred to make out. The second was better, though still damaged: two people standing at the edge of a clearing, the forest behind them. One was a man I didn't recognize. The other was a woman. Small, her face turned slightly away from the camera, holding a notebook.

I could not identify her from the photograph. The resolution was too low, the angle too oblique. But the notebook — a specific kind, a narrow hardcover field notebook in dark green — was the same as the notebook I had seen in Liling's work photographs, the TFRI's archive of her field research images. Same format, same color, same way of holding it: tucked against the forearm, not open, ready.

"Jun," I said.

"I know," he said. "I think I've known for a while."

"The man," I said. "In the photograph."

He looked at the photograph for a long moment. "I don't know who he is," he said. "But I can find out. The coalition archivist knows who donated these. The botanist who left her papers — she worked in this zone in the seventies. She might have labeled her photographs."

"They weren't labeled?"

"Not on the prints. But sometimes people label the negatives, or there's a corresponding entry in a field log." He looked at me. "We have her field logs."

We spent the rest of the afternoon in the monitoring station with the coalition archivist — a retired schoolteacher named Bettina who had taken on the oral history project as a retirement project and had become, over five years, the repository of more institutional memory than anyone had intended. Bettina knew where everything was.

The field logs of the botanist who had donated the photographs were in three binders, handwritten, covering 1970 to 1985. We found the year 1973 and began to read.

The entry for July 12, 1973:

*Working session today at the Pemberton diner. Two new people — Elio's contacts from the coast. One of them, Liling (she gave only her first name, Taiwanese botanist, won't say her affiliation), asked the questions about substrate boundary measurements that I've been trying to frame for months. She is doing something at the cedar on the ridge. Won't say what, exactly. Kenji (Japanese, also won't specify affiliation) took the photographs today. He says he's documenting the survey zone. He is also, I think, documenting her.*

Kenji.

*He is also, I think, documenting her.*

The handwriting of a person who had been watching two people in the way that people are watched when the watcher has understood something that the people themselves might not have fully articulated yet.

I looked at Jun across the field log.

"He was here," I said. "He crossed too."

Jun was very still. "The Pacific Research Collective," he said. "Three researchers from different countries, all in the same zone, all at the same time, none of them affiliated with any real institution."

"An institution that existed only in visa paperwork." I thought about Jing-wei's report. "Someone helped them. Someone with enough institutional reach to create a credible-looking sponsoring organization across multiple countries, enough to satisfy immigration services, not enough to leave a real trace."

"Or something," Jun said.

We looked at each other.

"The Garden," I said.

"I've been thinking about this for a long time," Jun said. "The crossing doesn't deposit people at random. It deposited you here, where the work needed exactly what you have. It deposited Liling here, fifty years ago, with exactly what the forest needed. If the crossing has been — if the Garden has been doing this, facilitating, for decades—"

"Then there's an infrastructure," I said. "Not just individual crossers. A pattern."

The field log was open to July 1973. The handwriting of someone who had been watching without understanding what she was watching.

*He is also, I think, documenting her.*

I thought about Kenji's 1977 paper, the one that drew on methodology from Liling's work without citing it. He had known her work the way you know something when you've watched the person doing it, when you've stood beside them in the field while they collected the data that would later be transformed, through the alchemy of publication, into something the wider world could access. He had carried what she knew across the crossing, back to his world, and planted it in his own literature the way she had planted the matrix in the forest: a gift for something that couldn't receive it yet.

They had both left gifts.

Both of them, in the same zone, at the same time, in a forest that was dying in ways no one in their world fully understood yet — and both of them had left something for the future to find.

"Jun," I said. "The field log. Does it mention the cedar? The split trunk?"

He was already looking.

July 28, 1973: *Liling up at the ridge again. Kenji went with her this morning. They came back in the afternoon and she was very quiet and he was — not quiet, exactly, but careful. The way someone is careful when they've just done something they want to hold without disturbing.*

August 15, 1973: *Asked Liling what she's working on at the cedar. She said: a preservation. She wouldn't say more. Kenji said: she's planting the future. She gave him a very particular look when he said that. The kind of look that goes into the record as data.*

I read both entries twice. Then I said: "We have to go to that ridge."

"I know," Jun said. "But we don't have the coordinates."

"She does," I said. "She has them in whatever part of her that the dementia hasn't taken." I closed the field log. "I need to go to Taipei."
