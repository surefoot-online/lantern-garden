### Chapter Eight: The Archive Question

*From the testimony record, Session Two: "I want to be clear about my state of knowledge when Act II began, as I've come to think of it — when the second phase of crossings started. I knew my grandmother had been in the Garden. I had the photograph, I had the word 'crossing,' I had the word 'Kenji,' and I had an arithmetic that I could not stop doing. What I did not have: any of the institutional records. Any of the corroboration. I was operating on the edge between hypothesis and certainty, which is the most uncomfortable place a person who works with evidence can inhabit."*

---

She did not look at the photograph again for three days.

This was not avoidance. She distinguished carefully between avoidance and the particular discipline of letting a finding settle before pressing it — the way she would walk around a site twice before she walked through it, establishing the perimeter before committing to the interior. The photograph needed to be a fact before it became a framework. She had spent enough of her professional life unwinding premature frameworks to know the cost of building them before the foundation was solid.

She worked on the coalition's substrate model for three days. She sent Jun an analysis of the northwest quadrant's priority nodes — seven high-probability collapse vectors ahead of the beetle season's peak — and received a response at four in the morning that said, with Jun's characteristic economy: *This is right. I think this is right. I'm going to send it to the board.* She held the specific satisfaction of work that had found its correct use, and then she went back to the green roofs and the Burrard corridor files and the plaza in Yaletown where the northeast corner drainage was still wrong, and she did each of these things with the complete professional attention they required, because that was the other discipline: that the work in this world was real, that its realness was not diminished by what she was carrying.

On the third day she opened the photograph.

---

She had, since the Sunday morning kitchen conversation, been building a list. A landscape architect's list: not an emotional register, not an interpretation, just the facts in order with the gaps marked explicitly.

*Liling: active TFRI research, 1966–1972. Seventeen papers. Mycorrhizal connectivity, hydrological propagation, network communication.*

*1973: Vancouver conference registration. No presentation on record.*

*1972–1975: Institutional affiliation inactive. Three Canadian visa renewals. No published work.*

*1975: Return to TFRI. Submitted field summary. Description: "extended Canadian field research period." Specimens deposited. Mycorrhizal matrix preserved separately — off-site, in situ.*

*April 1976: Huimei born.*

She had written the last item and then sat for a while with the pen in her hand, not adding to it.

The photograph was the only visual evidence. A garden she recognized, a figure she recognized, a quality of light she had been moving through for six months. The photograph had been kept close — not archived, not in an album, not packed into storage when Liling moved to the care facility. Kept on the bedside table where she could see it from the bed.

This was the behavior of someone who wanted to remain in contact with what the photograph showed. Not to display it. To maintain the proximity.

Mei looked at the figure at the edge of the garden. The slight forward lean. The weight on the front foot.

The posture of someone about to enter.

She called the Taiwan Forestry Research Institute's archive department at eight-thirty in the morning, which was eight-thirty in the evening in Taipei and which she apologized for, though the archivist seemed untroubled by the hour.

"Researcher records from the 1960s and 1970s," the archivist said. "The published papers are fully digitized. Institutional correspondence and field reports — partially, depending on the researcher. Personal notes and private correspondence wouldn't typically be in the institutional archive."

"Are there gaps in her institutional record? Dr. Wǎng Liling — from the mid-seventies?"

The archivist's search took forty seconds. "After 1972, her institutional affiliation was inactive until 1975. When she returned, she submitted a summary of her research period and deposited specimens. The full field report was apparently never submitted." A pause. "The summary is in the archive. Shall I scan it?"

She gave her email address and thanked him and worked for the rest of the morning on the Burrard project and at one-thirty the scan arrived.

The summary was a single page. Liling's handwriting — precise, unembellished, the same voice she'd found in the published papers — described three years of field research on mycorrhizal system recovery in degraded temperate rainforest ecosystems. It named species and soil conditions and inoculation methodologies. Eight paragraphs, the minimum required documentation, the report of someone who had done serious work and was filing what had to be filed and no more.

The final paragraph:

*Specimens and seed material collected during the research period have been deposited with the TFRI specimen collection under registration codes attached. A mycorrhizal matrix derived from the Pacific Rim conifer populations studied has been preserved and stored separately, off-site, in situ, under conditions appropriate for long-term viability. Details available on request.*

*Details available on request.*

She put the scan down on the kitchen table and looked at it for a while, at the careful handwriting and the fifty years of silence compacted into that final sentence.

She thought: she was twenty years ahead of the field professionally. She was also twenty years ahead of anyone who might have asked the right question. She filed the detail available on request and went home and planted her granddaughter and waited.

She opened a new email to Jun. *When I arrive next week, I want to look at your coalition's historical records. Not the technical archive — the oral history materials, the land stewardship documentation, anything from the Cascade zone going back to the sixties and seventies. There's something I'm trying to locate.*

He responded in twenty minutes: *Yes. When are you coming?*

*Thursday,* she wrote.

*Good,* he wrote. *Come soon. The beetle season is moving faster than the model.*

She booked the flight, added the itinerary to the paper trail she maintained, and spent the rest of the week doing what she had been doing for months: being a landscape architect in this world, with the full attention her professional life required, while underneath the professional life a different kind of model was running.

---

She had been trying to locate a doctoral student in the TFRI's network who was working on the history of mid-century Taiwanese women scientists. The archivist had mentioned her. She sent the message, explained what she was looking for, and received a response within two hours: precise, formal, interested.

The doctoral student's name was Jing-wei. She was twenty-six, working on her dissertation at NTU, and she had been trying to explain the gap in Liling's institutional record for two years. The gap had appeared in a footnote of her dissertation research and she had been unable to fill it by conventional means. She said this in a message that was eight sentences long and occupied exactly the space the facts required.

Mei hired her on the spot, gave her the budget she'd set aside for the project, told her what she was looking for: anything that documented Liling's movements between 1972 and 1975. The visa renewals, the sponsoring institution, the field work. Anything about the specimens she'd brought back.

Jing-wei said: *I've already found the visa renewals. The sponsoring institution is listed as the Pacific Research Collective, British Columbia. I've been trying to identify this organization for months. It doesn't appear in any Canadian institutional records before 1972 or after 1977.*

She wrote back: *Keep looking. Can you also find the other researchers they may have sponsored? Same period, same zone.*

Jing-wei's response took six minutes: *I'll start today.*

---

She arrived at the ranger station on a Thursday afternoon, after a crossing that had been — she had been tracking this for six months, had a spreadsheet — marginally harder than the previous week's. Something about the interval was changing. Not the crossing itself, which was the same structured not-quite-space it had always been, but the effort required to initiate it. The way certain doors required slightly more force to open in winter, the frame swelled by moisture. She noted this and added it to the spreadsheet and did not yet know what to do with the data.

The station was fuller than usual when she arrived — two additional team members from a university ecology department, a legal consultant the coalition had brought in about the clearcut litigation, Priya running three soil analysis rigs simultaneously in the annex. The coalition in crisis mode looked like the coalition in every mode but louder: more coffee, more conversations happening simultaneously, the particular intensity of people who have been running a response for long enough that the response has become its natural state.

Jun found her at the map table before she'd set her bag down.

"The clearcut litigation," he said. "The court issued a temporary injunction last Tuesday. The contractor moved in on Thursday, before the injunction was formally served. Twelve hectares of the southern corridor's buffer zone."

She looked at the map. The buffer zone, marked in green hatch, now had a red overlay that she understood immediately: clearcut. The test site itself was intact — the legal protection around a permitted research site had apparently held — but the buffer that surrounded it on the north and east was gone.

"Microclimate impact?" she said.

"Sera's modeled six weeks before the temperature and humidity differential reaches the network nodes at the test site's perimeter. The inoculation needs twelve weeks minimum to establish." He looked at the map with the expression she'd learned to read as something he was holding rather than something he was ready to state. "There's a coalition meeting this afternoon. Two senior members want to redirect resources to the northern corridor. They think the south is compromised."

She studied the topology. The northern corridor was stable but its substrate profile was different — better-draining, which sounded advantageous but was not, for mycorrhizal network propagation, because the drier substrate slowed establishment. She'd shown this in the priority model. "The northern corridor's spread rate is a third of the south site's," she said. "Even with a compromised buffer, the south is the higher-leverage intervention."

"I know. I need you to say it in the meeting."

She said it in the meeting, with the whiteboard and the propagation arrows and the numbers that made the argument for the numbers to make. The room held two reasonable positions and she presented both of them clearly and then explained why the south site remained the correct bet given the full spatial model. The vote was not unanimous. It was sufficient.

Afterward, in the quiet that followed a coalition decision — the particular exhaustion of having made a choice under inadequate conditions — Jun sat down in one of the folding chairs with his elbows on his knees and put his face in his hands. Not dramatically. Just occupying the shape of a man who had been running for forty hours and needed a moment to be somewhere that wasn't responsible.

She sat in the chair beside him. Close enough that he would know she was there.

After a while he lowered his hands. "The supplementary inoculation," he said. "We have the budget for one. If this doesn't take, we go north and we start over from a worse position."

"The south site will take." She said it with the specific quality she'd identified in herself during the first coalition meetings, the thing that was different from conventional optimism: she meant it. The spatial model was sound. The substrate had the profile the network needed. The bet was correct.

He looked at her. "You always mean it when you say that."

"Yes."

"Other people don't always mean it."

"I know." She looked at the whiteboard, the propagation arrows, the two weights of line she'd used to show the two interventions' different spread potentials. "In my experience, most people are optimistic as a social act. I'm optimistic as a spatial conclusion. They're different operations."

He almost smiled. "Spatial conclusion."

"The model supports the bet. That's not hope. That's geometry."

He looked at her for a moment with the careful expression — the full attention, the look she'd been receiving for ten weeks and had never found a satisfactory framework for, because the frameworks she'd tried all captured part of it and missed the rest. Then he said: "I stopped asking how you get here."

She went still.

"You mentioned it in the forest," he said. "That you'd stopped — or I'd stopped. I stopped asking because the question was getting in the way of working with you. But I want to say it explicitly now so it's not the thing underneath everything else." He looked at the map. "Whatever the answer is, I don't need it to keep working with you. I stopped needing it a while ago. I just wanted to say it out loud so it wasn't only in the room between us."

She was quiet for a moment, holding this. The care in it. The deliberate making-explicit, the thing he'd decided she should have said rather than unsaid. "Thank you," she said.

"You said something in the old-growth section. That you'd tell me when you knew."

"I said that."

"I'm not asking. I'm saying I remember, and I'm not asking."

She looked at the whiteboard. "I'm getting closer to knowing," she said. "To understanding what there is to tell."

"Okay," he said.

The legal consultant found them twenty minutes later with questions about the documentation requirements for the injunction appeal. Jun straightened and became the version of himself that ran meetings, and she went back to the map table and the substrate model, and the afternoon resumed.

That evening, after the station had gone quiet and she'd spent three hours in the archive room with Jun's coalition's oral history materials, she sat with a cup of tea that had gone cold and looked at what she'd found.

The oral history project had interviewed fourteen people over three years — former field workers, botanists, forest guides, a provincial natural resources employee who'd worked the Cascade zone in the 1960s through the 1980s. Three of the interviews contained references she'd been looking for.

The first: a botanist named Helena Forsythe, who had conducted informal research surveys in the Cascade zone in the early 1970s. She described, in a 2019 interview, a colleague she'd encountered at what she called "the working sessions": *a woman, Taiwanese, she didn't give an affiliation, she called herself Lin or Liling, I think Liling, she asked the best questions about substrate connectivity I've ever been asked and I've been asked a lot of questions about substrate connectivity.*

The second: a former provincial natural resources employee named Douglas Ogg, interviewed 2018. He'd encountered a small group of independent researchers at a site in the northern Cascade zone in the summer of 1973. He remembered *a Japanese man and a Taiwanese woman, both of them clearly scientists, both clearly not affiliated with any Canadian institution as far as I could tell, and they were doing something at a cedar on the ridge above the main survey zone, I could see them from the access road, and when I came closer the woman said they were doing a preservation and would I please not report the site coordinates, and I said I wouldn't, and I didn't.*

The third: the coalition's own Thomas, who had been twenty-two in 1973 and had been working with his father in the land stewardship capacity his family had maintained for three generations. He had not seen her directly but his father had mentioned her: *a small woman with a notebook, precise, came to the forest regularly for about two years, knew the mycorrhizal systems at a level his father didn't recognize from any literature he'd read. His father said she was doing something important.*

She read all three accounts twice. Then she wrote down: *Japanese man. Same zone. 1973.*

She thought about Kenji.
