### Chapter Nine · The Fracture Line

*Jun had been awake for forty hours when I arrived. I know this because he told me later, when we were talking in the way that people talk after a crisis has moved from acute to chronic — with the dry affect of someone cataloguing damage from a distance. He had been awake for forty hours and he had kept the coalition together across two emergency calls and a board meeting in which two members had voted to abandon the southern corridor entirely, and when I came out of the Garden at the test zone's edge on a Tuesday that felt like a Thursday, he was standing at the monitoring station's door looking at a map that had too many red notations on it.*

*I want to be clear about something before I describe this chapter. The Tribunal has asked me, in various forms, whether my continued crossing during this period was voluntary. I want to answer that question precisely: the crossing was voluntary in the sense that I chose to return. I could have stayed in my world. I could have kept sending Jun data remotely and never stood in his forest again. What I chose was to cross because the work required presence, and because I understood, by now, that presence in Jun's world was something I was able to give. Whether this was altruism, or something more complicated than altruism, I'll leave for the Tribunal to characterize.*

---

He didn't hear me arrive.

This was unusual. Jun is attentive to arrival, in the particular way of someone who has learned to track who is coming and going from a space because tracking it is part of keeping the space safe. The coalition's test zone has exactly the security profile of a place that has been vandalized before and expects to be vandalized again — cameras at the zone's perimeter, a check-in protocol for team members, the kind of careful maintenance of a small territorial claim that you develop when the larger institutional systems are not reliably protecting it.

He didn't hear me because he was on a call, standing at the monitoring station's door with his phone held slightly away from his ear in the gesture of someone who has been listening to bad news for long enough that they've physically withdrawn from it. I stood at the tree line and waited.

The call ended. He looked at the phone for a moment before he put it in his pocket. Then he looked up and saw me.

"The south site," he said.

"Tell me."

He told me.

The previous Friday — four days ago by my reckoning, five by his — a logging contractor had entered the southern corridor on a timber license that the coalition's lawyers had been fighting in court for eighteen months. The court had issued a temporary injunction the previous Tuesday. The contractor had moved in on Thursday, before the injunction was formally served. By Friday morning, twelve hectares of the southern corridor's buffer zone had been cleared. Not the test site itself — the legal risks around a permitted research site had apparently been sufficient to keep the contractor out — but the buffer zone that separated the test site from the adjacent managed forest.

Without the buffer, the test site's north edge was now adjacent to bare clearcut. The microclimate differential between a test site embedded in forest and a test site at the edge of a clearcut is not small. Wind exposure, temperature swing, humidity loss — all of it working against the fragile fungal inoculation that was, based on my analysis, showing early signs of taking hold.

"The inoculation?" I asked.

"Still showing the early indicators. Sera thinks we have six weeks before the microclimate change reaches the network nodes." He looked at the map with the too-many red notations. "Six weeks is not enough to establish. We need twelve minimum."

"What does your legal team say about injunctive relief for the cleared area?"

"They're working on it. The contractor claims the injunction was served after the work was completed. There's a time-stamp dispute." He folded the map. The folding was controlled — precise creases, a map that had been folded and unfolded many times, something Jun does with his hands when his mind is working on something that hands can't solve. "There's a coalition meeting in two hours. Two of the senior members want to redirect the southern corridor resources north. They think the southern site is compromised and we should concentrate where we can show results."

I knew the test zone's topology. I had the spatial model open on my tablet, and I had been running it in my mind during the crossing — a habit I had developed, the three-minute liminal interval of the Garden used as a processing window for the models. I knew the southern site's propagation geometry.

"The northern route doesn't have the substrate profile for the propagation vector we need," I said. "I showed this in the priority model. The north quadrant's substrate is better — less glacial till, better water retention — which sounds like an advantage but it means the network will establish more slowly. The south site's marginal substrate is what makes it the high-spread vector. If the network takes there, it spreads faster than anything we can establish in the north."

"I know," Jun said. "I know all of this. I need you to say it in the meeting."

"How many people are you expecting?"

"Twenty-eight. Most of the core coalition. The two who want to abandon the south are Henriksen and Yiu — you know Henriksen. Yiu is newer, came on six months ago through the municipal partnership. She's not wrong to be cautious. If the southern site fails we've spent half our remaining seed budget on a site we can't recover."

"She's not wrong," I said. "But the spatial model is also not wrong."

Jun looked at me with the expression I had learned to recognize as his form of gratitude: a specific quality of steadiness, the look of someone who has confirmed a weight-bearing calculation. "No," he said. "Both things can be true."

---

The barn was warm from twenty-eight bodies and a propane heater that had seen better decades. The coalition had the particular energy of a group that has been through something difficult and is sitting in the immediate aftermath, not quite recovered, not quite ready to move on. People held coffee cups without drinking from them. The whiteboards had the previous session's notes still visible underneath new writing, palimpsest.

Jun presented the situation without editorializing. He was good at this — presenting the worst conditions without performing tragedy, which is harder than it sounds. The tragedy performs itself when you let the facts stand alone. He presented the clearcut, the microclimate projections, the timeline risk. He presented the northern route as a genuine alternative, not a fallback. He was fair to both options.

Then he said: "I want Mei to walk us through the topology of each route."

I had done this before, though never quite under these circumstances. I stood up and took the marker and turned to the whiteboard and drew the test zone from memory — the monitoring station, the old south site, the cleared buffer, the northern corridor's substrate profile in approximate form. I drew the propagation vectors as arrows with different weights: the southern route's fast-spread, high-risk profile, the northern route's slower, more stable establishment pattern.

"The south site's propagation potential is higher by a factor of three," I said. "If it establishes — and that's still a conditional — the network can reach the adjacent forest patches in the northern corridor within eighteen months. If we start from the north, we're looking at four to six years for equivalent coverage, assuming no further setbacks."

"Assuming," Yiu said. She was sitting in the second row, arms crossed, the posture of someone who has been listening carefully and has a specific disagreement. "The south site's propagation potential depends on the inoculation taking. Which depends on the microclimate not being disrupted. Which we can no longer guarantee, given the clearcut."

"The microclimate impact is real," I said. "Sera's estimate of six weeks is conservative — I'd put it closer to eight before we see significant disruption. Eight weeks is not twelve, but it's more than the nothing we had this morning." I looked at the diagram. "The question isn't which site is safer. The question is which site gives the system the best chance of becoming self-sustaining before the next beetle season."

"And your answer?" Henriksen asked. He was in the front row, grey-haired, the patience of a man who has been in this fight long enough to have earned the right to be wrong occasionally and has not been wrong often enough to make it a habit.

"My answer is the south site. With an emergency supplementary inoculation at the points closest to the cleared buffer, to reinforce the network nodes that are most exposed to the microclimate change." I looked at Jun. "If we have the budget."

"We have enough for one supplementary inoculation," Jun said. "If this doesn't take, we go north and we start over."

The room did the math. Twenty-eight people, the math slightly different for each of them, the answer the same for all of them: this is the last best option. The coalition voted. It was not unanimous — Yiu voted against, and two others, and their dissent was correct in its way, the reasonable caution of people who had learned to protect what remained. But the vote went for the south.

Jun thanked the group and the meeting ended and the room began its complicated choreography of people who need to talk to each other but also need coffee and a moment to stand in the air outside.

I waited until most of the group had filed out. Jun was standing at the whiteboard, looking at the diagram I had drawn, the propagation arrows in their different weights.

He did not say anything. He sat down in the nearest chair and put his elbows on his knees and covered his face with his hands. Not dramatically. Just — occupying the shape of a man who is very tired.

I sat in the chair beside him. Not close enough to touch. Close enough that he would know I was there.

After a long time he lowered his hands and looked at the whiteboard. "Forty hours," he said. "I need to sleep."

"Yes."

"After I sleep, I have to call the legal team, call the contractor's lawyers, call the board chair, and tell Sera that we're doing a supplementary inoculation with a week's notice." He was reciting a list in the way of someone who needs to hear the list to believe it's finite. "And I have to tell the team that we're still in this."

"Are we?"

He looked at me. "What does your instinct tell you?"

I thought about the die-off pattern in the south plot and the northeast quadrant's three trees still holding cellular function. I thought about the glacial till and the network nodes and the propagation math I had done in the margin of a coalition meeting two weeks ago. "The south site is the right bet," I said. "I don't know if the right bet wins."

"No." He was quiet for a moment. "It rarely does."

Outside, through the barn's high windows, the afternoon light had turned the particular amber that came before the smoke haze settled in for the evening. Somewhere to the southwest, the advance front was burning. The sound of it didn't carry this far. The silence where the sound should have been was its own kind of information.

Jun looked at me with an expression I had not seen before and did not have a name for yet. "You drove up from the coast again?"

"Yes."

"You're always arriving."

"I know."

"I'm glad," he said. "I'm not going to ask you to explain it. I'm just glad."

He stood, with the careful uprightness of someone whose body has been running on fumes and is beginning to negotiate terms. "I'm going to sleep for six hours. Will you still be here?"

"I'll be here."

He nodded, accepting this the way he accepts most things I tell him: completely, without pressing on the edges. He walked toward the back of the barn, where the coalition's overnight rooms were — three small spaces that had been a hayloft, insulated and furnished with the minimal competence of people who needed somewhere to sleep on short notice and were not precious about comfort.

I sat in the barn with the diagram on the whiteboard and the amber light in the windows, and I thought about the archive summary and the detail available on request and the seed matrix preserved off-site, in situ, in conditions appropriate for long-term viability.

I thought: fifty years. And the climate held.
