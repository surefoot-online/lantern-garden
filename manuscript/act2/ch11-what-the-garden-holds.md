### Chapter Eleven: What the Garden Holds

The corridor to her grandmother's wing in the Zhonghe facility had the same smell it always had — the gardenias at reception, the institutional antiseptic underneath, the specific humidity of a building that maintained temperature carefully because its residents needed it. She walked it with the knowledge that she was carrying something Liling had been waiting fifty years for someone to carry to her.

Her grandmother was at the window.

Not looking at the courtyard maple, which she usually was — looking at the light itself, the specific angle of the mid-morning sun through the glass, the way it fell across the windowsill. She had the field-station posture: the chin at the angle, the slight forward orientation, the attention of someone who is reading something worth reading.

"Nǎi Nai," Mei said softly.

She turned. The moment of recognition — the particular expression that was not the confused triangulating but the landing, the specific *you* rather than the general *visitor.*

"Mei-mei," she said.

"I have something to tell you."

She sat in the family chair and took her grandmother's hand and told her. She told her about the cedar on the north ridge, and the birch bark package on the north side of the roots, and Priya's analysis, and the seven viable corridor strains. She told it in the language of the science, which was the language her grandmother had spoken for sixty years and which, she had learned, survived the dementia better than most languages did.

Liling listened. The window was open — she could see it, the quality of the attention, the organizing intelligence working behind her grandmother's eyes. She did not interrupt. She listened with the complete, patient absorption of a scientist receiving field data she has been waiting for.

When Mei finished, her grandmother was quiet for a moment.

"Seven strains," she said. "Which seven?"

Mei listed them by species. Her grandmother nodded at each one — a small, specific nod, the nod of someone confirming what they expected to hear.

"The *Rhizopogon vesiculosus* made it through," Liling said. "I wasn't sure about that one. It was at the edge of viability when I sealed the vessel."

"Priya says it's the most robust of the seven."

"Good. It's the most important for the Douglas fir connectivity. The others will establish, but the *Rhizopogon* is the connective tissue." She looked at her hand in Mei's. "The cedar held?"

"The cedar is three hundred years old. It's doing well."

A faint smile. "Cedars," she said. "They know how to wait."

They sat for a moment. The light on the windowsill. The courtyard maple. The small specific world of the room and its good, careful design.

"Nǎi Nai," Mei said. "I spoke with Dr. Park."

Her grandmother's hand tightened slightly. "Jinhee."

"She says you would have wanted to know someone found it in time."

"Yes." A pause. "How is she?"

"Well. She's in Busan. She has a garden."

"Good." Liling looked at the window. "And Kenji — you know about Kenji."

"I know his name from the oral history accounts. I know he was in the Cascade zone. I know he documented the work and that his 1977 paper drew on your methodology." Mei paused. "I don't know enough yet."

"He stayed," Liling said. Not with grief. With the specific tone she'd used before: something that had been held for a long time and had been held well. "He chose the forest. I chose your mother." She looked at Mei. "Both choices were right for the people who made them."

"Tell me about him."

"He was patient," she said. "More patient than I was. He understood the network mechanisms at a level that — he saw it the way I saw it. Underground. Not as a metaphor. As a fact about how things were connected." She was quiet for a moment. "He was the one who said to preserve the culture in the first place. He said: if you leave it correctly, someone will come with the tools to use it. He said the Garden would see to that." She paused. "I thought he was being mystical. I was wrong."

"Did he cross often?"

"He crossed regularly for two years. Then he decided to stay. He said the forest needed him there, and he wasn't wrong." She looked at the courtyard. "He died in his world. In 2009, I think — someone told me, one of the botanists who kept in contact with both worlds." A pause. "He died knowing the cedar was still there."

She sat with this. With the image of a Japanese forest ecologist choosing to stay in a world that was not his, doing the work that needed doing, dying in the Cascade zone's old-growth section with the knowledge that the cedar on the north ridge was intact.

"Nǎi Nai," she said. "There's a tribunal. Some kind of body that oversees the crossings, that has jurisdiction over what happens at the Garden. I'm going to have to testify to them eventually."

Liling looked at her. The window was still fully open — the organizing intelligence, the field-station precision. "The Tribunal has been there since before I crossed," she said. "We knew they were there. Kenji knew their structure better than I did — he had contacts who had been before them." She paused. "They're not the enemy. They're trying to understand something that doesn't fit their categories."

"What will they want to know?"

"What you used the crossing for. Whether you harmed or helped." She looked at Mei directly. "The standard the Tribunal applies is not whether the crossing was permitted. It's whether the crossing was toward something worth doing." She paused. "Tell them what you found at the cedar. Tell them about the seven viable strains. Tell them what Dr. Park told you. Tell them the truth about the forest and what was done for it."

"And that will be sufficient?"

Her grandmother looked at her with the expression that had no name — the one that was past clarity and past grief and past satisfaction, the one that was the deep-settled expression of someone who has understood something important about time. "That will be what it is," she said. "Which is all anything is."

They sat for a while without speaking. The light on the windowsill moved as the morning did its slow work — the angle shifting, the rectangle lengthening, the quality of the illumination that crossed between diffuse and direct as whatever clouds were moving did their moving. Mei watched it and her grandmother watched it and neither of them felt the need to say anything about it.

After a while Liling said: "How is Jun?"

She had been expecting this, and then she had not been expecting it, and then it arrived and she understood that she had been expecting it all along but had been hoping to shape when it arrived. "He's fighting," she said.

"Good. That's what you do when something is worth fighting for."

"He's fighting well. The coalition is — they're good. The people he's built around him."

"He built well?" Her grandmother looked at the courtyard. "The Japanese man — Kenji — he said something once. He said: the coalition is what the individual can't be. The patience that one person can't sustain, a group of people can hold between them. They pass the patience around. When one person is losing it, another is holding it." She paused. "That's true. I saw it in the informal network we had in the Cascade zone. Helena, and Douglas, and the others who knew what we were doing without fully knowing. They held the knowledge of the sites between them for twenty years."

"And now the coalition holds it."

"Now the coalition holds it. Yes." She looked at Mei. "And now you're in the coalition."

Mei thought about the twelve weeks at the ranger station. The map table. The propagation arrows on the whiteboard. Anya's half-second-notice quality of attention, and Priya's soil cores, and Luca's blue-green visualization of the network saying its things to itself. "Yes," she said.

"Good." Her grandmother closed her eyes for a moment, the brief resting close, the way she did when she was managing the energy required for focus. Then she opened them. "Tell me something. The corridor. The southern sector — how long?"

"The bridging seeding is being planned now. We think six weeks to begin establishment indicators. Longer for the network to integrate the culture's strains into the existing connectivity."

"The *Rhizopogon* will anchor quickly," Liling said. "The slower ones are the *Suillus* strains — they're finicky about moisture gradients. But the *Rhizopogon* will establish fast and create a connectivity backbone for the others to build along." She was speaking with the authority of someone who had prepared this culture specifically and had thought, in the years since, about exactly this scenario. "Make sure the inoculation points along the bridging sequence are at the right depth. Not too shallow — the *Rhizopogon* needs twelve centimeters minimum, or the temperature variance will stress it."

"Priya knows her inoculation depths."

"I know Priya doesn't know me. But you know her. If you tell her that the Rhizopogon went in at twelve centimeters at the cedar site and performed well there, she'll adjust."

"How do you know it performed well?"

Her grandmother looked at her with the mild patience of someone explaining something to a person who already knows the answer. "Because the cedar held," she said. "Because the cedar is three hundred years old and it held, and if the culture in its roots had failed twenty years ago the cedar would have lost its network support and it wouldn't be three hundred and still doing well. The cedar held because the network held. The network held because the culture worked." She settled back. "The *Rhizopogon* worked."

Mei looked at her grandmother's hands. The specific authority in them, still, despite the dementia, despite the dissolution of so much else — the authority of a person who had understood something precisely and who had not, in any of the years since, stopped understanding it.

"Nǎi Nai," she said. "You should have been cited. Your 1967 paper — Simard's work in the nineties got the citations you should have had."

Her grandmother made a small sound that was not quite amusement and not quite indifference. "By 1990 I had given up caring about citations," she said. "I had other things to think about. Your mother was fourteen. The forest in the corridor was — I knew what was happening to it. I'd been watching the data from the Cascade zone for fifteen years by then. The beetle advance, the connectivity decline. The indicators I'd left were showing the right patterns, the right rates. The time I'd spent on the seeding was not wasted." She paused. "A citation would have been nice. But a citation doesn't plant anything."

"No," Mei said.

"You plant things," her grandmother said, simply. "Or you don't. The citations come later, sometimes, or they don't. But the planting is the only thing that matters in the moment of the planting." She looked at the courtyard. "I planted things. Some of them grew. Some of them are growing now." She looked at Mei. "You grew."

She did not say anything for a moment. The unexpected precision of it. The way her grandmother said the word *grew* with the same care she'd used for *Rhizopogon vesiculosus* — not with sentimentality, with accuracy.

"I had good conditions," Mei said.

"You had what you needed," her grandmother said. "That's the job. Give them what they need and trust the mechanism." She was quiet for a moment. "The mechanism works. I know the mechanism works because I've seen it work. In the forest and — otherwise." She looked at Mei. "You're here. The cedar is there. The culture is viable. The mechanism works."

Fang-ling came in at eleven-thirty to check on the afternoon medication schedule, and the rhythm of the visit changed — Liling shifted into the social mode she maintained with the facility staff, the appropriate responses in appropriate order, the practiced navigation of the ordinary world. Mei watched this and thought about the layers of competence that the dementia left intact: the botanical knowledge, the spatial orientation when windows opened, and now this — the social fluency, the ability to track a room's expectations and meet them. Liling had always been multiple kinds of competent at once. She still was.

Fang-ling left. Liling's attention drifted slightly — the soft mode, not lost, just less anchored to the specific present moment.

"Kenji had a daughter," Liling said, to the window. It was not quite addressed to Mei. It was the kind of statement that emerged in the drift — a fact surfacing. "She was born after he went back to Japan the first time. He went back, met someone, came back to the corridor. He told me about her." She paused. "She'd be in her forties now. I always wondered —" She stopped.

"She's a mycorrhizal ecologist," Mei said. "In my world. She's at the University of British Columbia."

Her grandmother turned slowly to look at her.

"I haven't contacted her," Mei said. "I don't know if she knows about the crossing. But she's working on mycorrhizal systems. Pacific Rim temperate forest." She paused. "It runs in the paternal line too."

Her grandmother looked at her for a long moment. Something was happening in her face — the deep layering of feeling behind a surface that had learned to be careful. Then she looked back at the window. "Good," she said. Her voice had the particular quality of something settled. "Good."

They sat until the lunch cart came down the corridor, the squeaking wheel audible through the closed door — a sound that marked the transition, that told Mei the visit was at its natural end.

She rose and bent to kiss her grandmother's forehead. Her grandmother's hand came up and held her wrist briefly, the same grip as before — not urgent, not weak. The grip of someone with something specific to say and the determination to say it correctly.

"The Garden is patient," Liling said. "It doesn't need us to hurry. The network is patient — it's been working underground for a hundred years in that corridor, even when we could only see the evidence of it from above. Be patient with the process." She released Mei's wrist. "But not too patient. The beetle season is real."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway." A faint smile. "Go."

She went.

---

She called her mother from the street outside the facility, from the same bench where she'd sat before, in the same Taipei afternoon warmth.

"I visited," she said when Huimei answered.

"How was she?"

"She was — in a window. A long one." She looked at the street. "She spoke about the forest. About the culture she preserved. She knew the species by name, knew the depth specifications for the inoculation."

Her mother was quiet for a moment. "She remembers the science."

"The science is still intact. The science and — some things that aren't science." She paused. "She asked about Jun."

"Who's Jun?"

She explained Jun. She told her mother about the coalition, the beetle advance, the southern corridor, the bridging seeding sequence. She told her more than she'd told her in previous conversations — she was, she realized, closing the gap. Building toward the full disclosure she'd promised. She was not yet there. But she could feel the shape of it, the size of what she was going to hand her mother, and she was learning the right order in which to hand it.

Her mother listened. When Mei finished, Huimei said: "You've been working with this coalition for how long?"

"Ten weeks. Eleven."

"And your grandmother knew about it."

"She could smell it on me," Mei said. "She said so. She recognized the — she said I smelled like the crossing."

A silence.

"Like the crossing," Huimei repeated. The careful voice. The quality of someone who has been turning a door handle for months and has just felt the latch give.

"Māmā," Mei said. "I'm going to tell you everything soon. Not on the phone. I'm going to tell you everything when I can sit with you in person and have the time it takes to say it properly." She paused. "What I can tell you now is that Nǎi Nai did something remarkable in the Cascade zone fifty years ago. And the people in the coalition are just now finding out what it was. And it matters. It's going to help them save a forest that needed what she left."

A longer silence.

"She planted something," Huimei said.

"Yes."

"She kept saying she planted something."

"She planted something," Mei said. "And it held. For fifty years, in the ground, under a three-hundred-year-old cedar on a ridge north of the main forest survey zone. It held, and we found it, and it's still viable, and we're going to use it."

The silence from her mother's end was the specific silence of someone receiving something they had been waiting for without knowing they were waiting. Not surprise — something past surprise. The settling of a weight that had been held so long it had become part of the posture, and was now being set down.

"Okay," Huimei said. Quietly. "Okay."

"I'll tell you everything soon," Mei said. "I promise."

"I know you will." Her mother's voice had the specific quality she reserved for the moments when the mild accusation was entirely set aside and what was underneath it spoke directly. "I've always known you would. I was just waiting for you to be ready."
