### Chapter Fifteen · Found

The message from Jing-wei arrived on a Tuesday, late afternoon, while I was on a work call with Hana Park that I had been successfully conducting with a level of professional presence I was rather proud of, given everything.

I read it after the call.

*Dear Ms. Chen,*

*I have found the other two researchers affiliated with the Pacific Research Collective.*

*Researcher one: Dr. Park Jinhee, South Korean, botanist, affiliated with Seoul National University until 1971. Her SNU record shows a resignation in November 1971 — she did not give a reason. She was in British Columbia from 1972 to 1974, sponsored by the Pacific Research Collective on a Canadian research visa. She returned to Korea in 1974 and took a position at a smaller regional university, where she worked until her retirement in 2001. She is now seventy-eight. I have found a contact address.*

*Researcher two: Dr. Tanaka Kenji, Japanese, forest ecologist, Kyushu University. His Kyushu record shows a sabbatical in 1971–1972 that was extended, without institutional explanation, through 1975. The Pacific Research Collective sponsored his visa renewals from 1972 to 1975. He returned to Kyushu in 1975 and resumed his position, retiring in 2003.*

*Dr. Tanaka Kenji.*

*I searched his publication record after 1975. He published extensively on mycorrhizal systems in Pacific Rim conifer forests, with a particular focus on network recovery in degraded ecosystems. His 1977 paper, which was one of the first in the Japanese literature to argue for the primacy of network intervention over individual-tree intervention, cites several methodological approaches that closely parallel your grandmother's 1968–1972 work — work he could not have read in Japanese translation, as her papers were not translated.*

*I think Dr. Tanaka Kenji knew Dr. Wǎng Liling before 1977. I think he knew her work before it was available in Japanese. I think he knew her.*

*I will contact Dr. Park Jinhee if you wish. Dr. Tanaka Kenji passed away in 2011. But Dr. Park may know about the Pacific Research Collective and what it was.*

*Jing-wei*

---

I read the message twice. Then I read the name again.

*Dr. Tanaka Kenji.*

He knew about the roots. He understood them.

---

I sat with the message for a long time. My apartment, the Tuesday evening light, the substrate model open on one screen and Liling's 1972 paper open on another — the two things I had been holding simultaneously for three weeks, the work in Jun's world and the research into Liling's, and now the two things had connected at a name.

He was real. Kenji was a real person who had worked in the same forest, sponsored by the same made-up institution, for approximately the same three-year period as Liling. A Japanese forest ecologist who understood mycorrhizal networks and who had published, after returning to his world, work that drew on methodology he could only have learned from her.

He was, in every meaningful sense, Jun's antecedent. The person who had been in Jun's world before Jun understood the forest the way Jun understands it now.

And Liling had loved him. Whatever it meant to love someone in those circumstances, across three years of shared work in a dying forest, she had loved him enough to keep saying his name fifty years later in a care facility in Zhonghe, in the rooms where most of the lights were off.

I wrote to Jing-wei: *Yes. Contact Dr. Park. Please tell her we're trying to understand the Pacific Research Collective and what happened in the Cascade zone between 1972 and 1975. Tell her it matters.*

Then I called my mother.

"I need to visit Nǎi Nai," I said. "Soon."

"Is something wrong?"

"No. I just — I have questions I need to ask her. While she still can answer."

My mother was quiet for a moment. "She's having more bad days. More than good. But she has her windows." A pause. "Ah-Mei. What have you found?"

"I'll tell you when I come," I said. "I'll tell you everything."
