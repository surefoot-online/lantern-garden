### Chapter Twelve: The Bridging Sequence

She was in Jun's world for five consecutive days building the bridging seeding sequence, and she tracked the accumulating crossing-fatigue with the specificity of a person who has learned to treat her body as a monitoring instrument: the heaviness that was not quite tiredness, the eyes that were slow to focus, the specific quality of sleep that was deeper than her usual sleep and left her dreaming of forests she could not locate in either world.

The sequence was not simple. The bridging seeding had to connect the active inoculation sites in the southern corridor to the north ridge culture, and that connection had to be established in a specific order that accounted for the existing network's fragmentation pattern, the substrate changes across the corridor, and the culture's own preferred propagation conditions. She was building a map not of where things were but of where they needed to go in what order, which was a different kind of spatial problem from any she'd worked on before and one that she found she was, in some way she couldn't quite name, specifically suited to.

Priya and Luca worked with her. They argued about the sequence in the productive way of people who know each other's reasoning well enough to push against it without losing the thread — Priya from the ecological side, Luca from the data architecture, Mei from the spatial logic. The argument was good. The sequence that came out of it was better than the one she'd arrived with.

Thomas came to the planning session on the second day. He sat at the edge of the group and listened with the specific quality of listening that was his contribution to coalition meetings — the receiving of what was being built, the testing of it against a different kind of knowledge. When they reached the section of the sequence that addressed the north ridge and the culture's role as interior node, he said:

"The cedar has been there through all of it. Through the beetle advance, through the clearcut litigation, through the years when nobody from the coalition could get budget to survey the north ridge properly. It's been doing its slow work the whole time." He looked at the map. "I want to do a land acknowledgment when we plant at the cedar. When we formally establish the bridge to the culture. That site is — it's a significant site. It should be recognized."

"Yes," Jun said. "Whatever you think is right."

"I'll prepare something." He looked at Mei. "Your grandmother chose that site specifically. She knew the cedar."

"She spent eighteen months preparing the preservation," Mei said. "She chose the north side of the roots, said the bark had to be birch, used pine resin for the seal. She knew what she was doing."

"She knew this land," Thomas said. It was not an accusation. It was an acknowledgment — of the specific form of knowledge that comes from paying full attention to a place for long enough that it tells you things it doesn't tell everyone. "She came from somewhere else and she paid attention."

"She came from somewhere else and she understood what she was working on," Mei said carefully. "She didn't presume. She asked."

Thomas looked at her. "Yes. That's the word." He turned back to the map. "Okay. Let's finish the sequence."

---

She told Jun about the Tribunal on the third day, sitting in the analysis room in the early evening after the rest of the team had gone.

She told him about what her grandmother had said — that the Tribunal had been there since before Liling crossed, that they were not the enemy but were trying to understand something that didn't fit their categories. She told him about the grey room and the two flags she hadn't recognized, the manufactured neutrality of a body that was trying to convene a jurisdiction that didn't yet exist.

He listened.

When she finished he was quiet for a long time.

"When?" he said.

"I don't know exactly. My grandmother said eventually. She said to tell them the truth about the forest and what was done for it." She looked at the map on the wall. "I think the closer the bridging sequence gets to completion, the closer the testimony gets. I think the Tribunal is tracking the crossings and wants to assess them when there's enough to assess."

"Are you afraid of it?"

She thought about this. "No," she said. "I'm going to tell them what I've seen. What my grandmother did. What the culture preserved. What the coalition has built." She paused. "The only thing I'm afraid of is being asked to stop before the sequence is complete."

He looked at her.

"The sequence needs to be established before the next beetle season," she said. "If I have to stop crossing before that —"

"You won't have to stop." He said it simply. "I don't know anything about this Tribunal but I know the argument you have for them, and the argument is a forest that was dying and a culture that survived fifty years specifically so someone could use it to save it." He looked at the map. "That's not a difficult case."

"I know. But the cases that aren't difficult are sometimes the ones that need to be made most clearly, because the people making the judgment have decided they're complicated."

He almost smiled. "Spatial conclusion again."

"You have a good model, you state the model, you let the geometry speak."

"And if the geometry isn't enough?"

"It's always been enough," she said. "In every room where the spatial argument was clearly stated and the data was correct and the stakes were real — it's been enough. Eventually."

He looked at her for a moment. "How eventually?"

"The northeast corner of the plaza in Yaletown has been draining wrong for seven years," she said. "But it is, technically, still draining."

He laughed — the full expression, not the almost. She had not heard it many times. It had the quality of something that had been held for a while and was briefly released, that filled the room and was gone before it could be examined.

"Okay," he said. "We'll make the case."

---

The supplementary monitoring three weeks after the initial inoculation showed eleven nodes above threshold. Three weeks ago: four. The central cluster had extended, bridged across the substrate gradient she'd identified as the pathway, reached the zone of better moisture retention that the buried drainage axis ran through.

Sera presented the numbers at the Thursday team meeting and the room received them in the particular way that good news, after long bad news, was received: not with elation but with the specific quality of relief that is heavier than joy. The acknowledgment of something that had been fought for and had not, despite everything, been lost.

"The network is talking," Luca said. He had the connectivity visualization on the main screen — the blue-green rendering, the forest saying the things it needed to say to itself. Eleven points now instead of four, connected by the lines that indicated established hyphal bridge. It looked, she thought, like a constellation. Not random — purposeful. The kind of pattern that meant something because it was a pattern, because the connections were where they needed to be.

"The north ridge seeding is scheduled for next week," Jun said. "When we establish the bridge from the south corridor to the culture site, the connectivity model shifts significantly."

He advanced to the next slide. The projected connectivity, incorporating the seven viable strains from Liling's culture as the interior node, the southern corridor's established network as the outer ring, the bridging seeding as the connection. The projected result: a corridor that was not what it had been before the beetle advance, not the old-growth composition that had accumulated over three centuries. But a corridor with the original mycorrhizal composition in its genetic structure, building from the interior out, with the resources it needed to do what it had always done — to communicate, to distribute, to manage succession, to carry what was needed from the dying to the living.

The forest talking to itself. In the language it had been using since before any of them were born.

"This is still a model," Priya said, from her seat. "The culture's viability in field conditions — we don't have that data yet. The bridge seeding may not establish at the projected rate."

"I know," Jun said. "This is the model."

"The model is very good," Thomas said.

"The model is what we're working toward," Jun said. "When we have the field data, we adjust the model. That's how it works."

---

She spent the fourth day of the five-day stay in what the coalition called the library, which was the room with the bookshelves and the two reading chairs and the archive boxes that Bettina maintained with the specific organizational logic of someone who had been the only user of a system for three years and had made peace with the fact that it would be incomprehensible to anyone else.

She was looking for anything related to the southwest quadrant of the old-growth section in the decade before the coalition had been formed.

What she found was a folder labeled *1988-1993: Stewardship Concerns* that Thomas had apparently contributed to the archive, which contained correspondence between his father and the provincial natural resources ministry about a series of encroachments on the old-growth section's buffer zone. The letters were formal, measured, and documented a sustained effort by a provincial bureaucracy to avoid acknowledging that the monitoring reports it was being sent required a response.

She read through them with the attention of someone who has been, in her professional life, on the receiving end of bureaucratic non-response and knows its texture well. Thomas's father had been patient. He had been more patient than the situation warranted, she thought, because he understood that patience was the only available instrument and that impatience would give the bureaucracy a pretext for further delay.

In the folder, behind the correspondence, was a document she hadn't expected: a monitoring summary, typed, dated 1991, titled *North Ridge Quadrant: Annual Survey Notes.* The author was listed as T. Matsuda.

Tanaka Kenji's family name was Tanaka. But Elio Matsuda had been one of the botanists in the 1973 working group. Could there be a Matsuda also doing north ridge monitoring?

She read the summary. The tone was familiar — the same precise, measured quality she'd been seeing in the field records. The survey covered the north ridge quadrant, the boulder field sector, the old-growth interior. It noted, in the section on the split-trunk cedar, *ongoing evidence of mycorrhizal activity in the root zone, consistent with previous years' surveys.* It noted that the root zone chemistry remained anomalous compared to surrounding substrate, *suggesting continued presence of introduced species, likely from earlier experimental inoculation.*

She photographed the document. She brought it to Jun.

He read it. He looked up. "T. Matsuda," he said.

"Elio's family," she said. "He must have passed the monitoring to them. After he moved to the coast — his connections in the interior, people who knew about the cedar, who continued the work." She paused. "Kenji set up the monitoring. He may have arranged local continuity as well. Multiple people watching the north ridge over the decades, without any of them having the full picture of why."

Jun was quiet for a moment. "Thomas," he said.

"What?"

"Thomas's father was involved in the old-growth advocacy. The correspondence in the folder. And Thomas is the one who said we should acknowledge the cedar site." He looked at the monitoring summary. "Does Thomas know about the cedar's history? Beyond what we've told him?"

She thought about Thomas's response when she'd said Liling hadn't presumed, had asked. *Yes. That's the word.* The specific quality of that acknowledgment — not just accepting the information but confirming it against something he already held.

"I don't know," she said. "I should ask him."

She found Thomas at the end of the day, before she crossed back, sitting on the station's front step with the end-of-day quality that the people who'd been doing this work for years shared: the specific stillness of someone who had used all of themselves and was letting the quiet do its work.

She sat beside him.

"The north ridge monitoring," she said. "The 1991 summary in the Stewardship folder. T. Matsuda."

Thomas looked at her.

"There's a continuity of monitoring on that cedar site that goes back further than the coalition," she said. "Someone was watching it for decades before the coalition even existed."

"Elio had people he trusted up there," Thomas said. "He was always careful about who he told about that cedar. He'd say there was a planting there, something old, something that needed to not be disturbed. He never said what kind of planting or who'd done it." He looked at the forest. "My father knew Elio. They had the same cautious relationship with things that were alive and delicate and needed to not be disturbed by people who didn't understand them." A pause. "When I started the coalition, Elio told me about the cedar. He said: there's something in that section that's been there a long time and it's doing its work. Don't let anyone survey it until you have someone who knows what they're looking at."

"And then I arrived."

"And then you arrived." He looked at her. "I didn't know it was your grandmother's work. I didn't know about the crossing — I still don't know everything about the crossing. But Elio said someone would come who knew what they were looking at, and you came and you knew what you were looking at."

She sat with this for a moment. "How long had you known I would come?"

"Not known. Hoped." He was quiet. "We've been doing this work for three years and the coalition has good people but we've been missing something spatial. Something that could see the topology the way the network sees it. I had a feeling it would come from outside. From somewhere unexpected."

"Not from outside," she said. "From further along in the same problem."

He looked at her.

"My world is ten, fifteen years behind this one on the trajectory," she said. "Maybe twenty. We're working on the same problem but we can still see ahead of it from our position. The work I've been doing here — the hydrological derivation, the spatial model — it's the application of analytical tools developed in a world where the crisis hasn't fully arrived yet. That's what I brought." She paused. "That's what my grandmother brought fifty years ago. The scientific framework from a world where it was theoretical. Applied to a world where it was urgent."

Thomas was quiet for a long time. "And now?" he said.

"Now I carry what I've seen here back to my world," she said. "The full-scale version. The emergency that my world is still ahead of." She looked at the forest. "That's what the crossing does. That's what it's been used for."

He nodded. He was looking at the forest with the expression she'd learned to read as Thomas seeing the full picture — the temporal picture, the long picture, the one that extended in both directions from the present moment. Then he said: "Liling planted something for fifty years out."

"Yes."

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to — she could read it in what he left alone: that the work done here was also preparation, that neither world was operating in isolation, that the Garden had been doing this longer than any of them had been paying attention. He looked at the forest the way he always looked at it, and let her arrive at the rest herself.

"The Garden connects people who are paying attention," she said. "To the same problem. From different vantage points."

He stood, with the slow uprightness of a large man who has been sitting on a step for a while. He put out his hand, not in a handshake exactly — a different gesture, palm slightly up, an acknowledgment she didn't have a cultural framework for but understood as an offering rather than a transaction.

She took his hand briefly.

"The cedar is glad you found it," he said. This was not, she understood, metaphor. It was the kind of statement that was true in a register she had been learning to receive since she first walked into the old-growth section and felt the specific weight of a forest that was still, in this section, fully itself.

"Tell it I said thank you," she said.

He smiled — the full expression, rare in Thomas too, that came from a place she respected for its rarity.

She crossed back that evening.
