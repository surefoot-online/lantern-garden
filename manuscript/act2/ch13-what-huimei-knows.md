### Chapter Thirteen · What Huimei Knows

I crossed on a Thursday morning and told Jun about the TFRI report over bad coffee in the monitoring station, which had become our default meeting room for anything that wasn't technically a coalition meeting.

He listened in the way he listens: completely, without interrupting. When I finished he sat for a moment with his cup in both hands and said: "The Pacific Research Collective."

"You've heard of it?"

"No. But a made-up institution sponsoring visa renewals is — that's not what I was expecting." He set down the cup. "Someone helped her stay. Someone with enough institutional credibility to satisfy the Canadian immigration service but without an actual institutional footprint."

"Jing-wei thinks it's a front organization."

"For what?"

"She doesn't know. I don't know." I looked at the monitoring station's map wall, which had Jun's and Sera's various overlays pinned to it in layers. "What I know is that Liling was in the Cascade zone for three years, affiliated with no known institution, and brought back a mycorrhizal matrix that she planted somewhere and preserved in situ." I paused. "In situ. In the place where she collected it. Which means she left it in the forest."

Jun was very still.

"In Jun's world's forest," I said.

He looked at me.

"If the Lantern Garden functions the same way for everyone who crosses it," I said, "and if the crossing deposits you in approximately the same location — the same coordinates, give or take — then the forest Liling was doing her fieldwork in, in 1972 to 1975, was your forest. This forest."

Jun stood and walked to the map wall. He put his finger on the test zone's location. Then he moved it to the historical records section — a different overlay, older, the coalition's oral history data plotted against topography.

"There are stories," he said. "I mentioned this to you when we spoke about the historical records. The woman who worked with local botanists in the early seventies. Lin or Ling. I said legend, not documentation, because we didn't have documentation. We have stories from two of the botanists who were active in this zone in that period — one of them is still alive. He's in his eighties." He was still looking at the map. "He's been telling this story for fifty years and the coalition has always categorized it as interesting local history. Not evidence of anything."

"It's evidence of something," I said.

"Yes." Jun turned from the map. His expression had the quality of someone recalculating — not distressed, but reconfiguring. The working face I had come to recognize. "I'll call him today. Can you stay until the weekend?"

"I can stay."

"Then I'll call him today and we'll see what he says."

---

We drove to the coast the following morning, Jun and I, in the coalition's truck, which had the specific rattling acquaintance with rough road that comes from having been driven on rough roads for three years without the maintenance budget required to address the consequences. The drive was three hours on roads that improved and then worsened as we moved from the main coalition territory into the smaller coastal communities where the forest had once been the primary employer and was now — in Jun's world — a different kind of employer, the kind that pays people to count what's left.

The man we were going to see was named Elio Matsuda, and he was eighty-three years old, and he had been a forest ecologist with the provincial government for thirty years before the government had stopped funding the kind of forest ecology that required looking at what the forest was actually doing as opposed to what the extraction permits said it was doing. He had retired to the coast, which was where he'd grown up, and he lived in a house on a hill above the water with a kitchen garden that was doing well despite everything and a view of the sound that was clear on some days and smoke-hazed on others.

He made us tea without asking if we wanted any.

"You want to know about Lin," he said. He had small, direct eyes behind thick glasses, and the particular economy of speech that comes from years of writing field reports that no one read who needed the information they contained.

"Everything you know," Jun said.

"Everything I know doesn't take long." He sat across from us with his own tea. "I was twenty-eight. Working for the provincial natural resources branch. The coalition didn't exist yet — this was before the collapse, when we still thought the cascade models were worst-case scenarios." He looked into his tea. "There were three or four of us doing independent plot surveys in the Cascade zone. Informal. Not sanctioned — the provincial government wanted numbers, not interpretation. We were doing interpretation."

"And she found you?"

"She came to one of our working sessions. Monthly, we'd meet at — it was a diner in Pemberton. She came one month with a man I didn't know, who introduced her as Lin, no affiliation given. She sat in the back and listened. At the end she asked three questions. They were the best questions anyone had asked about the work in a year."

"What kind of questions?" I asked.

"About the connectivity gradient. About how we were measuring network density across the substrate boundaries." He looked at me with the directness of an old man who has learned to be efficient with whatever attention remains. "You're a landscape architect, Jun tells me."

"Yes."

"She was something like that. She saw the topology. She could see — in the way that some people can, and most people can't — the shape that the data was making in three dimensions across actual terrain." He put down his tea. "We worked with her for two years. She was very careful about what she told us. Not deceptive — careful. She had information we didn't understand the source of. Better understanding of the network's behavior in stressed conditions than anything we had in the literature. I always assumed she had access to some research we weren't reading — Japanese, maybe, or Taiwanese. There was a lot of good Pacific Rim work that wasn't being translated."

"It was Taiwanese," I said.

He looked at me.

"The researcher. She was a Taiwanese botanist named Wǎng Liling. She was affiliated with the Taiwan Forestry Research Institute. And she published significant work on mycorrhizal connectivity in the late sixties and early seventies." I looked at Jun. "None of which was being read in this country in 1973."

Elio was quiet for a moment. "That makes sense," he said finally. "That makes a great deal of sense." He looked at the window, the sound visible through it, gray-green in the clouded light. "She said once that she was working on something in the forest that she would leave behind. Not specimens — something else. She asked if any of us knew of a cedar on the ridge north of the main survey zone. The one with the split trunk, she said. She'd found it on her own. She went up there once or twice a week, toward the end of the second year."

"You never went with her?"

"I offered. She said it wasn't necessary — that the site didn't need two sets of footprints." He picked up his tea again. "She left in — it must have been late 1974, early 1975. Said she had to return home. She didn't say where home was." He looked at us both. "She took nothing with her that I saw. Whatever she was working on at that cedar, she left it there."

On the drive back, Jun and I were quiet for a long time. The smoke haze had come in from the southwest, making the late afternoon light orange rather than gold.

"The cedar on the ridge," Jun said finally.

"North of the main survey zone. The one with the split trunk." I had been thinking about Liling's field summary, the phrase she had used: *the old cedar on the ridge.* "She knew where it was. She memorized the coordinates." I paused. "Or she would have."

Jun glanced at me briefly from the driver's seat. "What do you mean?"

"The crossing takes peripheral memory," I said. "Not core memory. Not methodology. Not the coordinates of something you went back to every week for two years." I looked at the orange sky. "If Liling is still in there — if her dementia hasn't taken the core things — she might still have those coordinates."

Jun was quiet. The truck rattled on the road that had worsened. 

"You're saying we should ask her," he said.

"I'm saying she might know," I said. "And I'm the only person who can ask her in a way that she might answer."
