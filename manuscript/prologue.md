## PROLOGUE: TESTIMONY, SESSION ONE

The room has been designed to feel neutral, which is the same as saying it has been designed. I notice this the way I notice everything: the proportions first, then the materials, then the light. The ceiling is high enough to allow authority without inducing awe. The walls are a grey that has no regional character — not the grey of Vancouver rain or the grey of Taipei winter, not the grey of fog or ash. A manufactured grey, the kind that comes from a committee making a decision about committees. The table in the center is laminate over particleboard, which surprises me. Whoever commissioned this room ran out of budget at the furniture.

Two flags stand in the corners behind the panel. I look at them carefully. I have spent six months preparing for this and I spend a moment now, before the recorder is switched on, trying to read them as I would read a site — looking for the narrative in the placement, the story in the grain. The flags are gold and blue. The symbols on them are not ones I recognize from any national standard in my world or in Jun's. I file this.

The chair of the Tribunal is a woman in her sixties. She has the face of someone who has listened to testimony for most of her professional life — not unkind, not moved, exactly the right distance from both. She thanks me for coming. I thank her for the invitation, which both of us understand is a euphemism. A recording light goes red. A junior staff member at the far end of the table opens a laptop.

"For the record," the chair says, "could you state your full name, your occupation, and your place of residence."

My name is Mei Chen. I am thirty-four years old. I am, professionally, a landscape architect. I specialize in urban ecological design — the way cities can be built to think, structurally, about the natural systems they've displaced. I am good at this. I am, my colleagues would say, unnervingly good at reading the logic of a terrain — at seeing, in the way a site sits in its watershed or a hillside holds its moisture, the argument a landscape is making.

I state my name. I state my occupation.

For place of residence, I pause.

The chair waits. The junior staff member's fingers hover over the keyboard. The recording light stays red.

I look at the two flags I do not recognize. I look at the manufactured grey of the walls, and the laminate table, and the woman who has listened to more testimony than any one person should be asked to accumulate. I think about the right answer. I think about what an answer, given to a body with uncertain jurisdiction and unclear interests, commits you to.

I consider several possible things to say.

I say nothing, yet.

The room holds the silence the way certain landscapes hold water — not naturally, but by design.
