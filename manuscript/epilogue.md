## EPILOGUE · TESTIMONY, SESSION FINAL

The room is the same.

This is the first thing I notice when they bring me back in for the final session: the room has not changed. The manufactured grey of the walls. The ceiling at its authority-permitting height. The laminate table over its particleboard base. The two flags in the corners with their gold and blue that I still cannot place in any national registry I know. The recording light, waiting to go red.

The chair of the Tribunal has the same face she always has: the face of someone who has listened to testimony for most of her professional life. Not unkind. Not moved. Exactly the right distance from both.

I have been in this room eleven times. I have given testimony covering approximately six weeks of my life, from multiple angles, in the retrospective first-person voice that the Tribunal requires: precise, organized, calibrated for what the institutional record can receive. I have given them dates and vectors and substrate compositions and propagation models. I have given them Jun and Priya and Henriksen and the coalition's work in as much detail as serves the Tribunal's mandate. I have given them Liling — her science, her crossing, the burial protocol, the species selection, the viability of the matrix after fifty years. I have given them a landscape architect's account of an interworld ecological entanglement that has been ongoing since 1972.

I have not given them the Garden.

I have given them facts about the Garden — its location, its relationship to the crossings, the phenomenological experience of the liminal interval — but not the Garden itself. Not the luminescence. Not the four hours I spent in it after Liling died, mapping and drawing and sitting with the specific light and the specific quiet of the place that had held her, too, in her last months. Not the understanding that arrived in that sitting about what the crossing is and what I am. Not the asymmetry between the two entry points, which I am still learning to understand and which belongs to a level of analysis that the Tribunal's forms cannot hold without distorting.

I have withheld this. Not out of fear. Out of the conviction that some things cannot yet be examined without damage, and that the Tribunal, for all its patience and its careful neutrality, is not the right instrument for what the Garden is.

The chair opens the final session with the items that institutional processes require: confirmation of the testimony record, verification of the dates and details I have provided, the standard questions about accuracy and completeness.

I confirm. I verify. I answer accurately.

Then the chair looks at her papers and says: "Ms. Chen. I have a question that falls outside the standard testimony categories. I want to note for the record that you are not required to answer it, but that your answer, if you choose to give one, will be relevant to the Tribunal's assessment."

I wait.

"The capacity you have described," she says. "The crossing capacity. You've indicated it's genetic, running through the maternal line of your family. You've described your grandmother as the first conscious crosser. Yourself as the second." She pauses. "Is it possible that others have this capacity? Others outside your family? Is the capacity reproducible by persons without the genetic predisposition, through some mechanism of the Garden itself? And—" She stops. "I want to be careful about how I ask this. Is it a risk? Is it something that could be acquired, or spread, or—?"

She does not finish the sentence. The junior staff member at the end of the table stops typing.

I look at the two flags. The gold and the blue.

I consider what is being asked.

She is asking whether the crossing is a security risk. She is asking whether the Garden is a contagion, or a weapon, or a doorway that could be forced open by persons without the capacity. She is asking whether what Liling and I are could become what anyone is, with the right circumstances or the right knowledge. She is asking, in the institutional language available to her, what the Tribunal would need to know to manage this. To regulate it. To ensure that the between-place can be supervised.

I understand why she is asking. The question is rational. The Tribunal's mandate requires it.

I look at her face — the not-unkind face, the professionally distanced face, the face of someone who has listened to more testimony than any one person should be asked to accumulate — and I make the decision that I have been preparing for, at some level, since the first time I sat in this room and did not answer the question about my place of residence.

"I don't know whether others have the capacity," I say. "My grandmother and I are the two confirmed crossers in our family's history. My grandmother believed her mother may have had the capacity, uncrossed or unconscious. Whether the capacity exists in other families, other lines — I have no evidence either way."

"But is it possible?"

"Yes," I say. "It's possible."

"And the other question. The Garden itself — can it be accessed without the genetic capacity? Can it be — entered — by someone who doesn't have what you have?"

I pause for exactly the length of pause that an honest answer requires. Not longer.

"I don't know," I say. This is true. I do not know. I have never seen anyone without the capacity attempt to enter the Garden, and I have no framework for what would happen if they tried. The Garden may exclude them entirely. The Garden may be invisible to them. The Garden may offer them something I cannot predict. I genuinely do not know.

"I can tell you," I say, "that in six months of crossings, I never encountered another crosser in the Garden. I never found evidence that anyone had been in the between-place who did not have the capacity. The Garden, in my experience, is not promiscuous with its access. But I cannot give the Tribunal a guarantee based on absence of evidence."

The chair writes something. The junior staff member types.

"On the question of risk," I say, before she can ask the follow-up I can see forming. "I want to address this directly."

She looks up.

"I crossed," I say. "I crossed repeatedly, over a six-week period, between my world and Jun's world. I crossed to assist a scientific coalition fighting an ecological crisis in a world that is not mine. I crossed to find a seed matrix that my grandmother planted in 1974, to give it to people who knew what to do with it, to help establish a fungal network corridor that may — I want to be careful about claiming more than the data supports — that may extend the survival of temperate rainforest ecosystems in a world that is running out of time."

I pause.

"The question of whether this constitutes a risk depends on your framework for risk," I say. "If the concern is jurisdictional — which world has authority over my actions, which world's law governs the crossings — then I understand why the Tribunal needs an answer. I'm not able to give you one, because I don't believe the crossing is jurisdictionally locatable. It exists between the worlds. It is governed by neither."

"Ms. Chen—"

"I'm not saying this to be obstinate," I say. "I'm saying it because I believe it's accurate, and because I think you already understand it, and because what you're asking me to give you — a guarantee, a containment protocol, a legal framework that makes the crossing administrable — is something I don't have to give. Not because I'm withholding it. Because it doesn't exist in the form the Tribunal needs."

The chair is quiet.

"I can tell you," I say, "that the corridor is viable. The matrix is established. The ridge deployment has been completed, and the culture's preliminary propagation data, which I submitted to the Tribunal's scientific panel last week, suggests that the southern cluster's northern bypass is establishing faster than projected. The coalition's ecologists believe, with appropriate caution, that the corridor will be sufficiently networked to function as a propagation vector within eight months." I pause. "That's what I can give you. That, and my testimony, and the full account of what I did and why."

"And what you chose not to tell us," the chair says. Not accusatory. Noting.

"Yes," I say. "And that."

She looks at me. The not-unkind face. The professional distance.

"What are you protecting?" she says. It is the most human question she has asked in eleven sessions.

I think about how to answer this. I think about the Garden in its luminescence, and Liling in the sea-glass chair, and Jun at the monitoring station revising his framework in the early morning without losing his direction, and my mother in the parking lot of the care facility asking whether Liling had been happy, and the ridge on the survey day with the hyphal threads in Priya's hand lens.

"Something that cannot yet be examined without damage," I say. "The Garden. What it is — not the facts of it, which I've given you, but the nature of it. The crossing's nature." I pause. "Some crossings are not for tribunals."

The chair looks at me for a long time.

Then she says: "For the record, Ms. Chen. What do you claim as your place of residence?"

The question I have been preparing for since the first session. The question the novel has been approaching for all its length. The question that the Tribunal's forms were not designed to receive.

I think about the Garden's specific light, which is without color temperature and without directionality and which is not the light of my world or Jun's world but the light of the place itself. I think about the naturalistic distribution of the lantern-plants, which have found their positions over time in response to the conditions of the site. I think about the spatial model in my notebook, the pages of it, the landscape architect's record of a place that no instrument can measure.

I think about Liling, who crossed and came home and never fully left. I think about myself, who crossed and came home and never fully left.

"The crossing itself," I say.

The chair's pen pauses.

"That isn't a recognized—"

"I know," I say.

The junior staff member has stopped typing. The recording light is red.

I look at the flags in the corners. The gold and the blue that I still cannot place in any national registry I know, flags belonging to an institution that emerged from two worlds negotiating over something they did not fully understand, trying to manage what they could not yet name.

I look at the flags and I look at the manufactured grey walls and the laminate table and the woman who has listened to more than any one person should be asked to carry.

And there, at the edge of the hearing room — at the threshold between what I am saying and what I am keeping, between the testimony and the silence beyond it — I can see them.

The lantern-plants.

Not really. I know they are not in this room. I know the room is grey and neutral and carefully designed to feel like nowhere. I know I am in a chair in a hearing room in a building that my taxi driver drove past the harbor to reach, that took forty minutes in light traffic. I know Liling is dead and Jun is at the monitoring station and my mother is at Liling's apartment in Taipei sorting through the boxes with the care of someone learning to touch what was precious.

But the light is there, at the room's edge. Faint. Familiar. The luminescence that deepens when you sit still long enough for the Garden to show you what it is.

Both are true. I am in the hearing room and I am in the Garden. I am in the testimony and I am in the between. I am answering the chair's questions and I am standing in the luminescence among the lantern-plants in their naturalistic distribution, each one in the position it has found over time, each one doing what it does in the light that comes from within it.

The recording light is red.

The junior staff member is waiting.

I look at the chair of the Tribunal, who is looking at me with the face of someone who has heard, across an entire testimony, both what was said and what was not said, and who has decided, with a professionalism and a humanity that I did not expect from the institution she represents, to let both stand.

"I know," I say.

I fold my hands on the table.

I am here.
